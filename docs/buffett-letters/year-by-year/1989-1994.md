## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 1989 (letter dated March 2, 1990)

**Context.** Net worth rose $1.515 billion (44.4%); 25-year book value compounding 23.8% from $19.46 to $4,296. The gain came after a $712 million tax charge, mostly deferred, on unrealized gains. Berkshire bought Borsheim's (jewelry, Omaha), nearly doubled its Coca-Cola position to 23.35 million shares, bought three convertible preferreds (Gillette $600m, USAir $358m, Champion $300m), sold most of its tax-exempt and low-coupon WPPSS bonds, issued $902.6 million face of zero-coupon convertible debentures (netting $400m), and wrote up to $250 million of catastrophe reinsurance in a ten-day window after Hurricane Hugo and the San Francisco earthquake. The letter is best known for the section "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years (A Condensed Version)" and a long attack on zero-coupon/PIK junk bonds and "EBDIT".

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Intrinsic value is what counts and is defined operationally: "With perfect foresight, this number can be calculated by taking all future cash flows of a business - in and out - and discounting them at prevailing interest rates. So valued, all businesses, from manufacturers of buggy whips to operators of cellular phones, become economic equals."
- Returns come from two sources ("double-dip"): growth in intrinsic value of the businesses plus the market re-rating them toward fair value. Once the re-rating is done, only the first source remains ("single-dip"). He explicitly says yearend 1989 prices of the major holdings are no longer cheap relative to intrinsic value.
- High growth rates must self-destruct in a finite world (Carl Sagan's bacteria): "A high growth rate eventually forges its own anchor." Size is the enemy of returns.
- Deferred taxes on unrealized gains are economically "an interest-free loan from the U.S. Treasury that comes due only at our election" – a transfer tax payable only if you move from one asset to another.
- The arithmetic of buy-and-hold vs. trading: $1 doubling annually and sold each year for 20 years with 34% tax leaves ~$25,250; one investment that doubles 20 times and is sold once leaves ~$692,000 – a 27:1 difference solely from the timing of tax payments. But he stresses they hold for the relationships, not the math: "we would rather stay put, even if that means slightly lower returns."
- Look-through earnings introduced as the right measure of Berkshire's earning power: reported operating earnings plus Berkshire's share of investee retained earnings (ex capital gains). Retained earnings at investees may be worth more than dividends because they are deployed by "talented, owner-oriented managers."
- The non-insurance "Sainted Seven Plus One" earned 57% after tax on average equity with no net leverage – the model of a good business.
- Retailing formula (NFM/Borsheim's): one location with unparalleled selection, lowest operating costs, shrewd high-volume buying, gross margins and prices far below competitors, family on the floor. Low prices drive volume which drives selection, "and so the circle goes 'round and 'round."
- Newspaper economics: high household penetration makes a paper a "megaphone" to the whole community; low-penetration papers eventually lose ad rates and profits. Product quality (news hole) drives penetration.
- Good managers matter even in good businesses: he credits Stan Lipsey with "at least five extra percentage points in profit margin" versus an average manager.
- Insurance: the "underwriting cycle" is a misnomer. Insurance is a commodity sold by hundreds of participants at independently set prices, so profits are subnormal except when there is a shortage of capacity – and capacity "primarily depends on the mental state of insurance managers." Good profits only when insurers are frightened.
- "Needs and desires have nothing to do with the long-term profitability of industries. Instead, economic fundamentals determine the outcome."
- Berkshire will write huge cat covers and retain the risk because (1) enormous capital and (2) "we simply don't care what earnings we report quarterly, or even annually, just as long as the decisions leading to those earnings (or losses) were reached intelligently." Willing to look foolish but not act foolishly.
- Securities selection: choose among five categories (long-term stocks, medium bonds, long bonds, cash, arbitrage) purely on highest after-tax "mathematical expectation," limited to things they understand; goal is eventual net worth, not reportable earnings.
- Arbitrage only when the deals make economic sense; otherwise it is "the greater-fool game."
- Circle of competence applied to the preferreds: they understand Gillette's economics but not investment banking, airlines or paper – "we're agnostics, not atheists" – so they structure those investments as senior securities. Even so, "The only way Berkshire can achieve satisfactory results from its four preferred issues is to have the common stocks of the investee companies do well."
- Rank order of capital deployment: best is a wonderful business unappreciated by the market or acquiring 80%+ of a fine business with fine management; negotiated preferreds are inferior to both.
- Free cash flow defined for LBO purposes: "operating earnings plus depreciation and amortization less normalized capital expenditures." EBDIT (EBITDA) is "an abomination"; depreciation is a real expense at 95% of businesses. "Whenever an investment banker starts talking about EBDIT ... zip up your wallet."
- Zero-coupon/PIK bonds let promoters stretch "the time elapsing between folly and failure." "A base business can not be transformed into a golden business by tricks of accounting or capital structure." "No financial instrument is evil per se."
- Investment bankers should act as gatekeepers with at least "bartender morality."
- Acquisition criteria restated (six items: $10m+ after-tax earnings, consistent earning power, good ROE with little debt, management in place, simple business, offering price); "Please include me out" of turnarounds, new ventures and auctions.

**Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years (covered carefully).**
1. Buying control of Berkshire itself – a textile business bought because "the price looked cheap." This is the "cigar butt" approach: one free puff, but "Unless you are a liquidator, that kind of approach to buying businesses is foolish." Two reasons: the bargain usually isn't ("never is there just one cockroach in the kitchen") and the low return erodes the initial advantage. "Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre." Hochschild Kohn (Baltimore department store bought below book with real-estate and LIFO extras) is the second example – sold three years later for about cost. Lesson: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner."
2. "Good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags." Able, honest managers at Berkshire textiles and Hochschild Kohn were "running in quicksand." "when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."
3. "Easy does it." "Charlie and I have not learned how to solve difficult business problems. What we have learned is to avoid them." Seek "one-foot hurdles." Exceptions: a wonderful business with a one-time, huge but solvable problem (American Express, GEICO). "we've done better by avoiding dragons than by slaying them."
4. The institutional imperative – "my most surprising discovery." Four forms: (1) institutions resist change in direction; (2) projects/acquisitions materialize to soak up available funds; (3) any leader's craving is quickly supported by rate-of-return and strategic studies; (4) peer behaviour is "mindlessly imitated." He manages Berkshire to minimize it and invests in companies alert to it.
5. Go into business only with people you "like, trust, and admire." This alone won't save a bad business, but "We've never succeeded in making a good deal with a bad person."
6. Errors of omission: passing on "a couple of really big purchases that were served up to me on a platter and that I was fully capable of understanding." "the cost of this thumb-sucking has been huge."
7. Conservative leverage was NOT a mistake even though higher leverage would have raised the 23.8% ROE: "A small chance of distress or disgrace cannot, in our view, be offset by a large chance of extra returns." "If your actions are sensible, you are certain to get good results; in most such cases, leverage just moves things along faster."
Also: Coca-Cola – he admits he noticed the product in 1936 and bought no shares for 52 years ("Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes"); he should have bought as soon as Goizueta and Keough took over.

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**
- Borsheim's: huge volume at one location gives enormous selection and an expense ratio about one-third of comparable jewelers; integrity of Ike Friedman ("We bought the business without an audit").
- See's: 8% poundage gain, same-store gains, advertising tripled with a terrific payoff; Chuck Huggins' quality-and-service culture.
- Nebraska Furniture Mart: 75.3% carpet share in Omaha; Mrs. B at 96 starts a competing store after a family dispute.
- Buffalo News: #1 household penetration among major metros, 50.1% news hole, seventh straight profit record while population shrinks.
- Fechheimer, Scott Fetzer/World Book/Kirby (Ralph Schey "also manages capital extremely well"; Kirby international sales quintupled in four years).
- Coca-Cola: Goizueta and Keough's "rare blend of marketing and financial skills"; world's most ubiquitous product with overseas sales exploding. Position doubled.
- Cap Cities, GEICO, Freddie Mac, Washington Post: unchanged holdings.
- Gillette preferred: business "very much the kind we like" and understandable; Salomon, USAir, Champion: industries he cannot forecast, hence preferreds; managers (Gutfreund, Mockler, Colodny, Sigler) liked and trusted. Preferreds expected to return "moderately above those achieved by most fixed-income portfolios."
- WPPSS low-coupon bonds sold after doubling and yielding 15–17% tax-free.
- Catastrophe reinsurance: wrote $250m when prices turned attractive after Hugo and the quake; Berkshire as a "stabilizing force" supplying capacity when it is scarce.
- Zero-coupon converts issued at 5.5% with tax deductibility of accrual ("positive cash flow").

### 1990 (letter dated March 1, 1991)

**Context.** A "lackluster" year: net worth up $362 million (7.3%) after a mid-year slide; the four "permanent" holdings (Cap Cities, Coke, GEICO, Washington Post) were flat in aggregate. Recession hit media and retailing; media stocks fell for what he judges to be secular reasons. Berkshire bought just under 10% of Wells Fargo for $290 million in a panic over California real estate, bought $440 million of RJR Nabisco junk bonds, became the largest U.S. writer of "super-cat" reinsurance, and the Gillette preferred was called for conversion. The letter introduces the formal "cost of float" framework with a 24-year table, reprints Ben Graham's 1936 U.S. Steel accounting satire (Appendix A) and Buffett's letter to a prospective seller (Appendix B).

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- A shareholder earns the company's return only if he buys and sells at the same relationship to intrinsic value; hence Berkshire hopes to trade "consistently at about intrinsic value" and dislikes the 1989–90 "value-ignoring volatility" (price +85% then -23% against far smaller intrinsic-value moves).
- Intrinsic value is an estimate; "Charlie and I might, in fact, differ by 10% in our appraisals."
- Head office's two jobs: identify talented managers and give them an environment to work in; then deploy the cash they send. The magic-wand story ("Gee, I'm really good at this") makes the point that headquarters does not create the operating results.
- Accounting earnings are "as pliable as putty when a charlatan heads the company"; use reported numbers "as a beginning, not an end" in calculating economic earnings.
- Look-through earnings formalised: $250m share of investee retained earnings, less $30m hypothetical tax, plus $371m reported operating earnings = ~$590m. Repurchases by Coke with retained earnings are more tax-efficient for Berkshire than dividends it would reinvest: "The way they are accounted for is of no importance, but their ownership and subsequent utilization is all-important."
- The non-insurance group earned 51% on equity without leverage and in prosaic industries (furniture, candy, vacuum cleaners); the explanation is "outstanding operating managers, not fortuitous industry economics." Managers remitted over 80% of earnings to Omaha over five years.
- Low-cost retailing wins: Borsheim's operating costs ~18% of sales vs ~40% for competitors; NFM 15% vs Levitz 40% and Circuit City 25%. "What works with diapers works with diamonds." The "magnet" model expands territory without opening new stores.
- Do not cut product quality in adversity (news hole kept at 50%+).
- Media: the past superiority came from "unusual pricing power," not physical growth; more channels, slower ad growth and non-advertising retailers have diminished it, and this "materially reduce[s] the intrinsic value" of the media holdings "though all remain fine businesses."
- Insurance pricing: results improve only when managers "run from business" out of fear. "The most important thing to do when you find yourself in a hole is to stop digging."
- Super-cat economics: Berkshire concentrates rather than spreads risk; annual combined ratio will be "close to either zero or 300%"; shareholders can handle lumpy results. "Charlie and I always have preferred a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%." Historical loss data are "of very limited value" for pricing; they write only at prices commensurate with risk and let volume fall when competitors get optimistic.
- The cost-of-float framework: float = loss reserves + LAE reserves + unearned premiums − agents' balances − prepaid acquisition costs − deferred charges on assumed reinsurance. Cost of float = underwriting loss ÷ average float. An insurer has positive value only if cost of float (plus ~1 point of tax penalty for indirect investing) is below alternative sources of funds. Short-period figures are meaningless. In 19 of 24 years Berkshire's cost was below the government's borrowing cost; GEICO's cost is "considerably less than zero."
- Long-tail pricing: pricing to a target combined ratio of 110–115 is "business suicide"; prices must carry "a healthy margin of safety against the societal trends that are forever springing expensive surprises on the insurance industry."
- Banking: 20:1 leverage means small mistakes destroy equity; no interest in a poorly managed bank at a cheap price, only well-managed banks at fair prices. Wells Fargo bought at under 5x after-tax earnings; a stress test (10% of all loans go bad with 30% loss) still roughly breaks even.
- Buyers of businesses should welcome falling prices as they welcome falling food prices; "It's optimism that is the enemy of the rational buyer." But contrarianism for its own sake is as foolish as crowd-following: "What's required is thinking rather than polling."
- Open-minded about category (junk bonds, banks) "so long as we understand the business we're buying into and believe that price and value may differ significantly."
- Debt: the "dagger on the steering wheel" theory of LBO discipline is rejected; "The roads of business are riddled with potholes; a plan that requires dodging them all is a plan for disaster." Graham's "Margin of Safety" are still "the right three words."
- Statistical trap: new-issue junk is not the same universe as fallen angels (the "Kool-Aid ... Jonestown" analogy); beware past-performance proofs.
- Convertible preferreds are worth at least their value as straight preferreds; the press's "conversion value only" method is wrong.
- Commodity businesses: "In a business selling a commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor." (USAir)
- Charity: corporate money is the shareholders'; executives should give to alma maters with their own money.
- Appendix B (letter to a seller): Berkshire buys to keep, has no operating staff, adapts to the seller's methods, pays cash, wants family to keep ~20%, and tells the seller plainly "you would be no richer after the sale than now."
- Appendix A (Graham's 1936 satire) shows the reductio of accounting tricks: writing plant down to minus $1bn, paying wages in options, inventory at $1, contingency reserves – all "modern" devices that create earnings without any change in operations. Buffett notes "excesses similar to those he then lampooned have many times since found their way into the financial statements of major American corporations."

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**
- Wells Fargo: "best managers in the business" (Reichardt, Hazen); >20% ROE, 1.25% ROA; earns >$1bn pretax after $300m loss provisions; bought during a bank-stock panic at <5x earnings. He compares Reichardt/Hazen to Murphy/Burke at Cap Cities: trust each other, pay well but keep headcount low, attack costs in good times too, "stick with what they understand."
- RJR Nabisco bonds: credit better than perceived, asset sales, equity added, well run.
- USAir preferred: an "unforced error" – bought at the moment of Piedmont integration trouble and industry "kamikaze pricing"; "our investment is now less secure than at the time I made it."
- Gillette: devotion to quality, Colman Mockler eulogised.
- Media (Buffalo News, Cap Cities, Post): fine businesses, but franchise weakening; News earnings fell 5% and will fall more.
- Borsheim's, NFM, See's (Albuquerque customer uprising; 5% price increase), Fechheimer, Scott Fetzer (Ralph runs 19 businesses; Kirby Generation 3; Campbell Hausfeld).
- GEICO (48% owned): negative cost of float driven by "extraordinary operating efficiency and its careful classification of risks."

**Mistakes admitted.** USAir timing ("I committed an 'unforced error'"); "colossal mistakes" in insurance before Mike Goldberg ("In the insurance business, there is no statute of limitations on stupidity"); the economic mistake of sticking with textiles (though choosing Ken Chace was no mistake). He also admits having derided synergy and then finding it (See's cart in NFM).

### 1991 (letter dated February 28, 1992)

**Context.** Net worth up $2.1 billion (39.6%), almost $1.6 billion of it from the P/E re-rating of Coca-Cola and Gillette (Gillette preferred converted to 24 million common shares on April 1). Buffett spent August 1991 onward as Interim Chairman of Salomon after its Treasury-auction scandal. Berkshire acquired H. H. Brown (work shoes), bought Guinness (first significant non-U.S. holding), American Express "Percs" and First Empire preferred, and sold its RJR bonds. Look-through earnings fell 14% (media decline, Gillette conversion, Wells Fargo break-even, lower insurance profits). Underwriting loss $119.6m, cost of float 6.31%. Key sections: the franchise-vs-business distinction with media valuation math, "Twenty Years in a Candy Store" (See's), Keynes on concentration, and the Fannie Mae "Mistake Du Jour."

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Scale kills returns: at $20m of capital a $1m idea added five points; now a $370m idea is needed. "And there are many more ways to make $1 million than to make $370 million."
- Re-ratings give a "double-dip" that "can't recur annually."
- Headquarters' job: "to treat them right and to allocate the capital they generate."
- Look-through earnings must grow ~15% for intrinsic value to grow 15%; since 1965 they grew at ~23%, matching book value. He advises individual investors to compute their own look-through earnings and build a portfolio that "will deliver him or her the highest possible look-through earnings a decade or so from now" – forcing attention on business prospects rather than market prospects. "In investing, just as in baseball, to put runs on the scoreboard one must watch the playing field, not the scoreboard."
- **Franchise vs. business (covered carefully).** An economic franchise arises from a product or service that "(1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation." All three show up as the ability "to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital." Franchises "can tolerate mis-management" – inept managers reduce profits but "cannot inflict mortal damage." A "business," by contrast, "earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight"; tight supply doesn't last; superior management can hold low-cost status longer but always faces attack; and "a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management." Many enterprises are in between – "weak franchises or strong businesses." Media have slipped from franchise toward business because supply of channels grew while demand ("500 million American eyeballs and a 24-hour day") cannot.
- **Valuation math for a weakened franchise (covered carefully).** Old assumption: media earnings grow 6% forever "without the employment of additional capital" (depreciation ≈ capex, little working capital), so reported earnings are freely distributable – a perpetual annuity growing 6%. At a 10% discount rate, $1m of after-tax earnings is worth $25m (25x after-tax, ~16x pretax). New assumption: $1m is "normal earning power" that "bob[s] around" cyclically, growing only if owners commit more capital. Discounted at the same 10%, value falls to $10m (10x after-tax, ~6.5x pretax). "Dollars are dollars whether they are derived from the operation of media properties or of steel mills" – what justified the premium was growth without incremental capital. "valuations must change dramatically when expectations are revised." Cyclical factors hurt current look-through earnings "but these factors do not reduce intrinsic value."
- Berkshire does not sell permanent holdings "simply because we see ways to use the money more advantageously elsewhere."
- Leverage amplifies the shrinkage of asset values; Cap Cities and the Post are nearly debt-free while peers carry debt of five times net income.
- **See's as the model franchise.** Bought in 1972 for $25m against $7m tangible net worth ("not yet fully appreciative of the value of an economic franchise"). Sales $29m→$196m; pretax profit $4.2m→$42.4m; yet net worth is only $25m, so only $18m of earnings were reinvested while $410m pretax was sent to Blue Chip/Berkshire. "For an increase in profits to be evaluated properly, it must be compared with the incremental capital investment required to produce it." The one insight: "untapped pricing power." "ownership of See's has taught us much about the evaluation of franchises. We've made significant money in certain common stocks because of the lessons we learned at See's."
- Managers of the best kind "cannot be 'hired'" – Berkshire must "provide a concert hall in which business artists of this class will wish to perform."
- Owner-like compensation at H. H. Brown: $7,800 salary plus a share of profits "after these are reduced by a charge for capital employed." Most systems are "long on carrots but short on sticks" and "treat equity capital as if it were cost-free." "Managers eager to bet heavily on their abilities usually have plenty of ability to bet on."
- Insurance: combined ratios now "largely irrelevant" to Berkshire; what matters is cost of float, beaten the government's borrowing rate in 20 of 25 years. Managements that under-reserve "paper over operating problems with accounting maneuvers" ("touch up the x-rays"). Super-cat priced to pay out ~90% of premiums over time; one-year GAAP accounting is "ill-suited" because reserves cannot be built in quiet years. "Innocents" will return and prices will plunge after a few quiet years; "Insurers, though, like investors, eventually repeat their mistakes."
- The stock market "serves as a relocation center at which money is moved from the active to the patient."
- Investment approach: "large businesses with understandable, enduring and mouth-watering economics that are run by able and shareholder-oriented managements," bought at a sensible price. Berkshire is "simply not smart enough ... to get great results by adroitly buying and selling portions of far-from-great businesses." Calling active traders "investors" is "like calling someone who repeatedly engages in one-night stands a romantic."
- Concentration: the private-companies-in-Omaha thought experiment (assess economics, assess people, buy a few of the best at sensible prices; you would not own an equal slice of every business in town). "Our motto is: 'If at first you do succeed, quit trying.'" Keynes's 1934 letter quoted in support.
- Mistakes of omission cost as much as commission; confine the mea culpa to things within competence (not Xerox, Apple, Wal-Mart).
- Airlines: bankrupt carriers "burn-the-furniture" pricing; "the industry, in aggregate, has posted a net loss since its birth after Kitty Hawk."

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**
- Coca-Cola and Gillette: "two of the best companies in the world," earnings expected to grow at hefty rates; Coke stock alone now worth more than Berkshire's entire 1988 net worth.
- See's: franchise, pricing power, minimal capital needs; 21.6% record pretax margin despite 4% lower poundage.
- H. H. Brown: leading North American work-shoe maker in a tough, import-dominated, inventory- and receivable-heavy industry; prospers only because of outstanding management (Frank Rooney, ex-Melville CEO with 20%+ ROE record) and the owner-like pay system.
- Media (Buffalo News, Cap Cities, Washington Post): intrinsic value "declined materially" but balance sheets and managements make them the only comfortable media holdings.
- Guinness: earns money like Coke/Gillette, mostly internationally.
- Wells Fargo: break-even look-through year – "a low-level possibility" he had flagged.
- Salomon: preferred slightly up in value despite the scandal; he is interim chairman.
- USAir: "decimating" year for the industry; low valuation assigned; Seth Schofield praised.
- American Express Percs ($300m, 8.85%, capped upside) and First Empire preferred (small, but "like being his partner on any scale" – Bob Wilmers).
- Freddie Mac position slightly increased; RJR sold; WPPSS high-coupons held.

**Mistakes admitted.** Fannie Mae: decided in early 1988 to buy 30 million shares, bought 7 million, stopped when the price rose "in frustration," then sold out of "distaste for holding small positions." Cost of the omission by yearend 1991: "about $1.4 billion." He notes he did not repeat the stop-buying error with Coke. Also: being an insurance "innocent" when he personally ran the operation; the $25m See's price cap was "dumb insistence" and they were "lucky"; the rear-view-mirror admission that no one in media saw the deterioration coming.

### 1992 (letter dated March 1, 1993)

**Context.** Per-share book value +20.3%; net worth +$1.52 billion. Buffett left Salomon in June after ten months. Berkshire called its zero-coupon convertibles (most redeemed for cash; 6,106 shares issued), bought 82% of Central States Indemnity (credit-card insurance), H. H. Brown bought Lowell Shoe, and in stocks Berkshire doubled Freddie Mac, added Wells Fargo and Guinness, and bought 14% of General Dynamics (an arbitrage that became a long-term holding). Hurricane Andrew cost about $125m in super-cat losses, roughly equal to the year's super-cat premiums. Key sections: the value-vs-growth discussion with John Burr Williams' discounted-cash-flow definition, the margin-of-safety restatement, the secondary-market-vs-new-issue argument, the post-retirement health-cost rule, and the stock-option accounting polemic. (Note: the Aesop "bird in the hand" passage is NOT in this letter; the only Aesop-like reference in this batch is the 1989 "a-bird-in-the-bush-may-be-worth-two-in-the-hand" line about investee retained earnings. The DCF passage here is attributed to John Burr Williams.)

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Issue shares only "when we receive as much value as we give"; growing the company is not the goal, growing per-share wealth is. The bank-merger anecdote ("you'll never again make a deal this dumb") illustrates value destruction via stock swaps.
- Book value is "a conservative, but useful, proxy" for intrinsic value at Berkshire.
- Three caveats in reading Berkshire's record vs. the S&P: operating-business returns vary over time; Berkshire's returns are after corporate tax while the S&P is pretax (an 18% S&P gain becomes "well short of 13%" for a corporate holder) – "a structural disadvantage we simply have to live with"; and two predictions: the S&P's next decade will be far worse than the last ("stocks cannot forever overperform their underlying businesses") and Berkshire's capital base will cut its relative advantage. He still calls short-term market forecasts "poison."
- Volatility is accepted and welcomed: "A tolerance for short-term swings improves our long-term prospects. In baseball lingo, our performance yardstick is slugging percentage, not batting average."
- Acquisitions: be "active, interested and open-minded, but it does not pay to be in a hurry." The toad-kissing princess: acquirers paying dearly for toads, then a "restructuring" charge where "the CEO receives the education but the stockholders pay the tuition." His own cheap toads croaked too. "Practice doesn't make perfect; practice makes permanent" – hence the switch to "good businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices."
- Add-on acquisitions by subsidiaries "enlarge the domain of managers we already know to be outstanding" – low-risk, high-return.
- Look-through earnings of $604m must reach $1.8bn by 2000 for 15% growth; capital allocation today is aimed at maximizing look-through earnings in 2000. But long-term focus is not an excuse: "If plantings made confidently are repeatedly followed by disappointing harvests, something is wrong with the farmer." Be suspicious both of managers who pump short-term earnings and of those who hide chronic failure behind "long-term focus." For some companies and industries "there simply is no good long-term strategy."
- Insurance: Hurricane Andrew – "It's only when the tide goes out that you learn who's been swimming naked." Super-cat pricing cannot extrapolate past experience (global warming, coastal population growth, rare quakes in Charleston and New Madrid). Berkshire rejects "more than 98%" of reinsurance offered; "a fool and his money are soon invited everywhere." Super-cat premiums are recognised only when a loss occurs or the policy expires, so profits appear only in Q4. If Berkshire didn't beat the government's borrowing rate on float, "there would be no economic reason for us to be in the business."
- The 1977 equity creed restated: a business "(a) that we can understand; (b) with favorable long-term prospects; (c) operated by honest and competent people; and (d) available at a very attractive price" – with "very attractive" now relaxed to "attractive" because of size and markets.
- **Value vs. growth (covered carefully).** The two are "joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive." "value investing" is redundant – "What is "investing" if it is not the act of seeking value at least sufficient to justify the amount paid?" Paying more than calculated value hoping to sell higher is speculation ("neither illegal, immoral nor - in our view - financially fattening"). Low P/B, low P/E and high yield are "far from determinative" of value; their opposites are "in no way inconsistent with a "value" purchase." Growth per se "tells us little about value" – the airline industry financed "profitless (or worse) growth." "Growth benefits investors only when the business in point can invest at incremental returns that are enticing - in other words, only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value. In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental funds, growth hurts the investor."
- **Discounted cash flow (John Burr Williams, The Theory of Investment Value).** "The value of any stock, bond or business today is determined by the cash inflows and outflows - discounted at an appropriate interest rate - that can be expected to occur during the remaining life of the asset." Same formula for stocks and bonds; the difference is that bonds have defined coupons while for equities "the investment analyst must himself estimate the future "coupons"," and management quality dramatically affects equity coupons. Buy whatever the DCF shows cheapest "irrespective of whether the business grows or doesn't, displays volatility or smoothness in its earnings, or carries a high price or low in relation to its current earnings and book value." If bonds come out cheaper, buy bonds.
- Best business to own: one that "over an extended period can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return." Worst: one that "must, or will, do the opposite." "Most high-return businesses need relatively little capital," so their shareholders usually benefit from high dividends or buybacks.
- Two defences against the difficulty of estimating coupons: (1) stick to simple, stable, understandable businesses – "What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know. An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes." (2) "we insist on a margin of safety in our purchase price. If we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not interested in buying." The margin of safety is "the cornerstone of investment success."
- Secondary market beats new issues: the secondary market, "periodically ruled by mass folly," sets clearing prices that can be half of value; the new-issue market is controlled by sellers who choose timing and never offer bargains – they sell "only when they feel the market is overpaying." Hence Berkshire's negotiated preferreds have matched but not exceeded the "modestly above fixed-income" expectation.
- Airlines again: "A competitively-beset business such as USAir requires far more managerial skill than does a business with fine economics. Unfortunately, though, the near-term reward for skill in the airline business is simply survival, not prosperity." Any high-cost producer "faced extinction."
- Accounting: deferred taxes at current rates (sensible); post-retirement health benefits must be booked – cash-basis accounting let managers make "open-ended promises [that] have created open-ended liabilities." Lincoln's riddle: "calling a tail a leg does not make it a leg."
- Stock options must be expensed: "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?" Imprecision is no excuse – depreciation, loan losses and P&C reserves are all estimates. Berkshire will buy restricted out-of-the-money options from any executive who says they're worthless.
- Corporate overhead: Berkshire's after-tax overhead is under 1% of operating earnings; a company spending 10% on headquarters loses ~9% of value. "We're admirers of the Wal-Mart, Nucor, Dover, GEICO, Golden West Financial and Price Co. models."
- No stock split: the no-split policy attracts rational long-term owners so "our stock consistently trades in a price range that is sensibly related to intrinsic value"; frictional trading costs are a "tax" on owners.
- Debt: zeros called because 5.5% money that could be put back in 1994 was no longer attractive and the conversion option disadvantaged shareholders; "we continue to have an aversion to debt, particularly the short-term kind."

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**

- General Dynamics: began as Dutch-tender arbitrage; became a long-term holding because Bill Anders had "a clearly articulated and rational strategy" executed with urgency and remarkable results.

### 1993 (letter dated March 1, 1994)

**Context.** Per-share book value +14.3% (intrinsic value also ~14%), while the stock rose 39%. Two accounting rule changes cost $145m of net worth (deferred tax at current rates; corporate rate up to 35%) and a third (all equities at market) added $172m. Berkshire acquired Dexter Shoe for 25,203 Berkshire shares (~$420m of stock – the only major stock-issued acquisition in the batch), sold one-third of its Cap Cities at $630 for a $297m after-tax gain, and had an underwriting profit of $31m on $2.6bn of float. Coke and Gillette grew EPS 38%/37% in two years while their stocks rose only 11%/6%. Key sections: Coke's 1919–1993 record and Graham's voting/weighing machine; the discussion of risk, beta, Mr. Market and diversification; corporate governance in three ownership situations.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Definitions: "Book value is an accounting term that measures the capital, including retained earnings, that has been put into a business. Intrinsic value is a present-value estimate of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life. At most companies, the two values are unrelated."
- Market price and intrinsic value "will arrive at about the same destination" over time but diverge in the short run. Graham: "In the short-run, the market is a voting machine ... but in the long-run, the market is a weighing machine." Coke fell 50% in 1920 from its $40 IPO; one share with dividends reinvested was worth $2.1m by 1993. "what will count over time is the earnings performance of these companies."
- Berkshire's market price was "not inappropriate" in a world of 6–7% long rates IF 15% intrinsic growth is achieved – an explicit link between interest rates and justified valuation.
- Selling a terrific business for Berkshire stock (Dexter) converts a 100% interest in one business into a smaller interest in many, tax-free, with liquidity and diversification; the sellers are treated as partners because Buffett has no options or restricted stock: "my gain-loss equation will always match that of all other owners." "At Berkshire, we do not tell .400 hitters how to swing."
- No strategic plan: "it's usually poison for a corporate giant's shareholders if it embarks upon new ventures pursuant to some grand vision." Focus on business economics and managerial character "and then to hope we get lucky in finding the two in combination."
- Financial disclosure principle: "supply you with the financial information that we would wish you to give us if our positions were reversed."
- Look-through earnings exclude capital gains, special accounting items and major restructuring charges; the hypothetical tax rate is 14% (Berkshire's dividend rate). The Cap Cities sale ($297m after 35% tax vs. $152m of look-through retained earnings over eight years) shows look-through earnings are "a conservative representation of Berkshire's true economic earnings."
- "painting first and shooting later" – set the target ($1.85bn look-through by 2000) in advance rather than redrawing it around results.
- Resist "the temptation to do something marginal simply because we are long on cash. There's no use running if you're on the wrong road."
- Taxes: buy-and-hold is "the soundest way to invest" even for a tax-exempt; deferral is a third reason. Li'l Abner: 27.5 annual doubles taxed yearly at 35% = $1m; held and sold once = $130m after tax.
- Insurance: as interest rates fall "the value of float has substantially declined," so combined-ratio comparisons across time are no longer useful. The transfer price of float "should be determined simply by the risk-free, long-term rate of interest." Investment results are a separate analysis.
- Super-cat: a single good year proves nothing. The 25-times-a-century event given 5-for-1 odds: "you may go a straight six, seven or more years without loss. You also will eventually go broke." Berkshire thinks it is getting 3.5-for-1 but "there is no way for us - or anyone else - to calculate the true odds." An Andrew-size storm would now cost $600m. Competitors cannot diversify or lay off super-cat risk; $5bn of new reinsurance capital will chase premiums and Berkshire will shrink volume if prices deteriorate. "the test of a reinsurer is its ability and willingness to pay losses under trying circumstances, not its readiness to accept premiums when things look rosy."
- Holding: "it is usually foolish to part with an interest in a business that is both understandable and durably wonderful. Business interests of that kind are simply too hard to replace." CEOs never sell crown-jewel subsidiaries yet churn personal portfolios on brokers' arguments like "You can't go broke taking a profit." "An investor should ordinarily hold a small piece of an outstanding business with the same tenacity that an owner would exhibit if he owned all of that business."
- Coke's 1938 Fortune story: investors thought they were "looking too late"; volume rose 50-fold from 1938 to 1993 and a fresh $40 in 1938 became $25,000. Dominance plus an unchanged product is not saturation.
- Strategy of few decisions: "we adopted a strategy that required our being smart - and not too smart at that - only a very few times. Indeed, we'll now settle for one good idea a year."
- **Risk, beta and diversification (covered carefully).** Concentration "may well decrease risk if it raises, as it should, both the intensity with which an investor thinks about a business and the comfort-level he must feel with its economic characteristics before buying into it." Risk is defined "using dictionary terms, as 'the possibility of loss or injury.'" Academics define it as relative volatility (beta) and build theories on it: "It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong." Beta produces absurdities – Washington Post in 1973 became "riskier" after it fell. "the true investor welcomes volatility" (Graham's Mr. Market, Chapter 8 of The Intelligent Investor): a manic-depressive market periodically attaches irrationally low prices to solid businesses, which cannot increase hazards "for an investor who is totally free to either ignore the market or exploit its folly." Beta purists ignore what the company makes, its competitors and its debt – "He may even prefer not to know the company's name." Berkshire "would not be disturbed if markets closed for a year or two." "We don't need a daily quote on our 100% position in See's or H. H. Brown to validate our well-being. Why, then, should we need a quote on our 7% interest in Coke?"
- The real risk is "whether his aggregate after-tax receipts from an investment (including those he receives on sale) will, over his prospective holding period, give him at least as much purchasing power as he had to begin with, plus a modest rate of interest on that initial stake." Five factors: (1) certainty with which long-term economics can be evaluated; (2) certainty about management's ability to realise potential and employ cash flows wisely; (3) certainty management will channel rewards to shareholders rather than itself; (4) purchase price; (5) taxation and inflation. "I know it when I see it" (Justice Stewart).
- Moats: Coke (44% of world soft drinks) and Gillette (>60% of blade value) have "an enormous competitive advantage, setting up a protective moat around their economic castles." Peter Lynch: commodity-product stocks should carry the label "Competition may prove hazardous to human wealth." Their betas equal those of run-of-the-mill companies, proving beta ≠ business risk. Pet rocks vs. Barbie: ordinary investors who understand consumer behaviour can tell the difference; beta cannot.
- Circle of competence: in many industries "Charlie and I can't determine whether we are dealing with a 'pet rock' or a 'Barbie'," and couldn't even with years of study; fast-moving technology precludes reliable long-term evaluation (TV manufacturing, computers). "Why search for a needle buried in a haystack when one is sitting in plain sight?"
- When diversification IS right: arbitrage and venture-style bets where each position carries real risk of loss – take many independent positions and think like the casino that owns the roulette wheel but "will refuse to accept a single, huge bet." And the know-nothing investor should own many equities, dollar-cost average, and use an index fund: "Paradoxically, when 'dumb' money acknowledges its limitations, it ceases to be dumb."
- For the know-something investor who can find five to ten sensibly priced companies with durable advantages, "conventional diversification makes no sense ... It is apt simply to hurt your results and increase your risk." Why put money in your 20th favourite rather than your top choices? Mae West: "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful."
- Corporate governance: three cases – (1) no controlling shareholder: directors should act "as if there is a single absentee owner"; boards of ten or fewer, mostly outside, should set CEO standards and meet without him; dissenting directors may go to the owners; requisites are "business savvy, interest in the job, and owner-orientation," not prominence or diversity; (2) controlling owner-manager (Berkshire now): directors can only persuade, and should resign if a serious matter is ignored; (3) controlling owner not managing (Hershey, Dow Jones – Berkshire after his death): the most effective structure because the owner can act and "readily correct any mistake he makes."
- Charity: "the company's money is the owners' money"; shareholders designate gifts.

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**
- Dexter Shoe: "one of the best-managed companies Charlie and I have seen in our business lifetimes"; 7.5m pairs a year made in Maine competitively against imports; Alfond and Lunder keep running it; paid in Berkshire stock. (He later called this a major mistake, but in 1993 it is presented as the year's "important success".)

### 1994 (letter dated March 7, 1995)

**Context.** Net worth +$1.45bn (13.9%); 30-year book value compounding 23% to $10,083. Float grew to $3.06bn at negative cost (underwriting profit $130m pretax), super-cat again "close to a best-case." USAir suspended its preferred dividend and Berkshire wrote the $358m position down to $89.5m through the income statement; Buffett and Munger left the USAir board. New holdings: American Express (now ~10%), Gannett, PNC; Coke increased to 100m shares; Cap Cities sale of late 1993 looks bad with the stock at $85. One small shoe-retail acquisition. Key sections: "fat wallet" and ignoring forecasts; book value vs intrinsic value via the college-education analogy and the Scott Fetzer purchase-premium accounting lesson; intrinsic value and capital allocation (EPS-dilution fallacy, acquisitions as "chain letter in reverse"); compensation philosophy; "price, rather than time, purchases"; the USAir and Cap Cities "Mistake Du Jour."

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- The formula is unchanged and "certain to produce reasonable success": "the purchase at sensible prices of businesses that have good underlying economics and are run by honest and able people." But "A fat wallet, however, is the enemy of superior investment results." Minimum $100m per security now.
- Ted Williams: wait for pitches in the "happy zone"; don't relax standards.
- Ignore political and economic forecasts – 30 years of shocks (Vietnam, price controls, oil shocks, Nixon, USSR, 508-point day, T-bills 2.8%–17.4%) "made the slightest dent in Ben Graham's investment principles." "we have usually made our best purchases when apprehensions about some macro event were at a peak. Fear is the foe of the faddist, but the friend of the fundamentalist."
- Alignment: Buffett and Munger will fare exactly as shareholders do; no compensation giving them more upside than downside; fortunes concentrated in Berkshire shares.
- Partial ownership of great businesses counts: Berkshire's 7.8% of Coke = 21 billion servings and ~$200m of "soft-drink earnings"; Gillette 7% of world blade revenues; Wells Fargo a $7bn "Berkshire Bank." "It's far better to own a significant portion of the Hope diamond than 100% of a rhinestone."
- Intrinsic value defined again: "the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life," subjective, changing with cash-flow estimates and interest rates, yet "the only logical way to evaluate the relative attractiveness of investments and businesses." Book value is "meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value" – the college-education analogy (cost incl. foregone earnings = book value; discounted excess lifetime earnings = intrinsic value).
- Scott Fetzer case: bought for $315.2m vs $172.6m book (premium $142.6m); earnings rose from $40.3m to $79.3m while book value fell to $94m because it paid out $634m of dividends in nine years with no leverage, no sale-leasebacks, no receivable sales. Its ROE would rank first on the Fortune 500 excluding bankruptcy windfalls. The purchase premium has been amortised to $54.2m, so the carrying value ($148.2m) is less than half the original even though earnings doubled – "the intrinsic value of the business has consistently grown, even though we have just as consistently marked down its carrying value." Purchase-premium charges "have no effect on cash or the taxes we pay, and are not, in our view, an economic cost."
- Management lesson from Graham: "in investing it is not necessary to do extraordinary things to get extraordinary results" – true in management too: "handle the basics well and not get diverted."
- **Intrinsic value and capital allocation.** Managers must act to increase per-share intrinsic value, including in buybacks; focusing on whether a deal is EPS-dilutive is dangerous (the MBA student merging with the day labourer). Berkshire has rejected many EPS-accretive deals that would have cut per-share intrinsic value; Gretzky: "Go to where the puck is going to be, not to where it is." Most big acquisitions are a bonanza for the acquiree, a status boost for the acquirer's management and a honey pot for bankers, but "usually reduce the wealth of the acquirer's shareholders ... because the acquirer typically gives up more intrinsic value than it receives." John Medlin: "you are running a chain letter in reverse." A really good business "generates far more money ... than it can use internally"; asking bankers whether to acquire "is like asking your interior decorator whether you need a $50,000 rug." CEOs' "animal spirits and ego" do not need encouragement; "Aw, fellas, all the other kids have one."
- Compensation: pay managers on what they control (Ralph Schey on Scott Fetzer, not Berkshire); large carrots tied to results in the manager's area; charge a high rate for incremental capital and credit the same rate for capital released, symmetrically, so it pays to send unneeded cash to Omaha. "alignment means being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside." Standard options with fixed strike, low payout and ten-year terms give "lush gains to a manager who has done no more than tread water"; holding down dividends raises option value. Arrangements set in five minutes without consultants and never changed. Buffett and Munger don't want pay tied to the whole enterprise and would work for free.
- Look-through earnings $1.03bn vs $1.85bn target for 2000 – ahead of schedule but flattered by super-cat.
- Float: "Float is money we hold but don't own." Insurance is profitable over time only if cost of float is below the cost of alternative funds; otherwise "the business has a negative value." Super-cat requires judgment (few, large, non-standard policies); Berkshire's advantages are Ajit Jain and "towering financial strength" (certainty of paying after a $50bn Long Island windstorm; wrote $400m of California quake cover immediately; will quote $500m same day). Accepting lumpy results beats smoothing: "By accepting the prospect of volatility, we expect to earn higher long-term returns than we would by pursuing predictability." Worst-case super-cat loss ~ $600m after tax; correlated non-insurance losses (See's, Wells Fargo, Freddie Mac in a Southern California quake) are acknowledged. "If you are not comfortable with this level of exposure, the time to sell your Berkshire stock is now."
- Investing: "The truly big investment idea can usually be explained in a short paragraph. We like a business with enduring competitive advantages that is run by able and owner-oriented people." Degree of difficulty doesn't count ("Olympic-diving methods"). "We try to price, rather than time, purchases." "Why scrap an informed decision because of an uninformed guess?" Purchases of National Indemnity, See's, Buffalo News, NFM, Scott Fetzer were made when available at acceptable prices, not on views of "the Dow, the Fed, or the economy."
- Add to existing holdings before looking for new ones: "If a business is attractive enough to buy once, it may well pay to repeat the process."
- Long familiarity with a company's products helps (GEICO since 1951, Washington Post from his paper route, Coke six-packs, American Express at 40% of the partnership after the salad-oil scandal, IDS at 3x earnings in 1953). "The real money was in the syrup."
- Mistakes: "Mistakes occur at the time of decision." USAir was "sloppy analysis," possibly from buying a senior security or "hubris": he "failed to focus on the problems that would inevitably beset a carrier whose costs were both high and extremely difficult to lower." "In an unregulated commodity business, a company must lower its costs to competitive levels or face extinction." Kelleher: "Bankruptcy court for airlines has become a health spa." Rule: "You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it." Cap Cities: sold 10m shares at $63, now $85.25 ($222.5m foregone) – a "repeat offender" after selling at $4.30 in 1978–80.
- Will issue preferred stock in an acquisition only if "receiving as much in intrinsic value as we are giving."

- Coca-Cola (100m shares), Gillette, Wells Fargo: "rare gems"; partial ownership scaled into servings, blades and a "Berkshire Bank."

## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 1989–1994

### 1. What investing IS

- Investing is buying a piece of a business and valuing it as a business: "We select our marketable equity securities in much the way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety" (1992, restating 1977). The four tests: understandable, favourable long-term prospects, honest and competent people, attractive price (1992). The 1994 one-sentence formula: "the purchase at sensible prices of businesses that have good underlying economics and are run by honest and able people."
- Investing is "the act of seeking value at least sufficient to justify the amount paid"; paying more in hope of resale is speculation (1992). "Value investing" is therefore a redundancy (1992).
- The scoreboard is market price in the long run, but prices are set by future earnings, so "watch the playing field, not the scoreboard" (1991). Graham's voting/weighing machine (1993).
- Time horizon is indefinite – "'til-death-do-us-part" (1989), "Rip Van Winkle style" (1989, 1991), "Lethargy bordering on sloth" (1990), "If at first you do succeed, quit trying" (1991), "hopelessly comatose" (1993). They "do not sell businesses - or investee holdings that we have classified as permanent - simply because we see ways to use the money more advantageously elsewhere" (1991). Hold a small piece of a great business with the tenacity an owner would show for the whole (1993).
- The investor should think of his portfolio as a company and maximize its look-through earnings a decade out (1991).
- Few decisions: "being smart - and not too smart at that - only a very few times"; "one good idea a year" (1993). "An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes" (1992).
- Market quotes are optional: wouldn't mind if markets closed for two years (1993); no daily quote needed on See's, so none on Coke (1993).
- "price, rather than time, purchases" (1994); buy when businesses become available at acceptable prices, not on macro views (1994).

### 2. What to look for in a business

- **The franchise definition (1991):** needed/desired, no close substitute in customers' minds, not price-regulated → evidenced by ability to price aggressively and earn high returns on capital; survives bad management. A mere "business" earns excess profits only as low-cost operator or under tight supply, and can be killed by bad management. Media migrated from franchise toward business as supply of channels grew against fixed demand (1990, 1991).
- **Pricing power** is the source of superior economics: media's past strength was "unusual pricing power," not growth (1990); See's "untapped pricing power" was the single key insight (1991); See's 5% price rise lifted margins in a recession (1990).
- **High return on unleveraged capital with little incremental capital:** the Sainted Seven earned 57% (1989) then 51% (1990) on equity with no net leverage; See's needed only $18m of reinvested earnings in 20 years while distributing $410m pretax (1991); Scott Fetzer earned $110m on $116m equity (1992) and has distributed more than 100% of earnings while doubling profits (1994). "Most high-return businesses need relatively little capital" (1992).
- **The best business** "can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return"; the worst must employ ever more capital at low returns (1992). Growth creates value "only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value"; airlines are the archetype of value-destroying growth (1992). Media valuation math: 6% growth with no incremental capital justifies 25x; "bob-around" earnings justify 10x at the same 10% discount rate (1991).
- **Moats:** Coke 44% of world soft drinks, Gillette >60% of blade value; brand, product and distribution "setting up a protective moat around their economic castles" (1993). Commodity businesses: "impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor" (1990); "Competition may prove hazardous to human wealth" (1993, Lynch); a high-cost producer in an unregulated commodity industry "must lower its costs ... or face extinction" (1994).
- **Low-cost operator** as the other durable edge: Borsheim's 18% and NFM 15% operating cost ratios vs 40% for rivals, Wal-Mart's 15% (1990); the volume/selection/price flywheel (1989, 1990).
- **Simplicity, stability, predictability:** stick to businesses "relatively simple and stable in character"; "If a business is complex or subject to constant change, we're not smart enough to predict future cash flows" (1992). Fast-moving technology precludes reliable long-term evaluation (1993). Acquisition criteria require "demonstrated consistent earning power," no projections, no turnarounds, "simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)" (1989–1991).
- **Dominant, unchanged products are not saturated:** Coke's 50-fold volume growth after already dominating in 1938 (1993).
- **Capital-intensive, inventory-heavy, import-exposed businesses** (shoes) can still be excellent only with outstanding management – H. H. Brown, Dexter (1991, 1993).
- A manager's reputation loses to the business's economics (1989).

### 3. What to look for in management

- Go into business only with people you "like, trust, and admire"; "We've never succeeded in making a good deal with a bad person" (1989). Integrity matters more than audits (Borsheim's bought without an audit, NFM without audited statements or warranties – 1989, 1993). Kay Graham's willingness to "treat all shareholders honorably" under a dual-class structure was decisive (1993).
- Capital allocation skill: Ralph Schey "manages capital extremely well" (1989); Coke's Goizueta blends marketing and finance (1989); managers remit 80%+ of earnings to Omaha (1990); the key risk factors include management's wisdom in employing cash flows and its willingness to channel rewards to shareholders (1993).
- The Reichardt/Hazen – Murphy/Burke model: partners who trust each other, pay well but keep headcount lean, attack costs in good times, stick to what they understand (1990). Watson: "I'm smart in spots - but I stay around those spots" (1990).
- Owner-managers who don't need to work but "like to hit home runs" (1989, 1991, 1994); Berkshire provides "a concert hall" (1991) and does "not tell .400 hitters how to swing" (1993).
- Candour: Ken Chace "was always 100% straight with me about its problems" (1990); Ralph "forthright about problems" (1994); beware managers who paper over results with accounting (1991) or who blame chronic failure on "long-term focus" (1992).
- The institutional imperative – four forms – is the chief enemy of rationality; Berkshire invests in companies "alert to the problem" (1989); bankers' lemming behaviour is an instance (1990); "Aw, fellas, all the other kids have one" (1994).
- Compensation should be tied to what the manager controls, charge for capital employed, be symmetrical, and be simple (H. H. Brown's $7,800 salary plus profit after a capital charge, 1991; Scott Fetzer's five-minute arrangement, 1994). Standard fixed-price options reward treading water (1994). Options are compensation and must be expensed (1992; Graham's 1936 satire reprinted 1990).
- Low corporate overhead correlates with performance: Berkshire's is <1% of operating earnings; admired models Wal-Mart, Nucor, Dover, GEICO, Golden West, Price Co. (1992). Corporate charity is owners' money (1990, 1991, 1993).
- Governance: directors should act for a single absentee owner; small, outside-dominated boards; dissenters may go public or resign; the owner-not-manager model (Berkshire's future) is best (1993).

### 4. Valuation

- Intrinsic value = discounted future cash in and out at prevailing/appropriate interest rates (1989, 1992 via John Burr Williams, 1993, 1994). It is "an estimate," subjective, and moves with interest rates; two partners may differ by 10% (1990, 1994). Book value measures capital put in; intrinsic value is cash that can be taken out; at most companies the two are unrelated (1993, 1994 – college-education and Scott Fetzer examples).
- Same formula for stocks and bonds; the difficulty with equities is estimating the "coupons," and management affects them (1992). Buy whichever is cheapest on DCF, including bonds when they win (1992).
- Growth is a variable inside value, not a separate style; it can be negative (1992). Low P/E, P/B or high yield do not define value; their opposites are compatible with value (1992).
- Margin of safety "the cornerstone of investment success" (1992); Graham's three words still right 42 years on (1990); not interested if value is only slightly above price (1992). Insurance pricing needs its own margin of safety (1990).
- Discount rates and interest rates: 10% used in the 1991 media illustration; Berkshire's price "not inappropriate" at 6–7% long rates if 15% growth is achieved (1993); the value of float "substantially declined" as rates fell and should be transfer-priced at the risk-free long rate (1993).
- Stocks cannot forever outperform their businesses; re-ratings are a one-time "double-dip" (1989, 1991); after the catch-up, returns equal business progress ("single-dip").
- Price discipline relaxed from "very attractive" to "attractive" because of size (1992); minimum position $100m (1994).
- Secondary market beats new issues because sellers set the terms in new issues (1992).
- Convertible preferreds are worth at least their straight-preferred value (1990).
- Tax deferral: the 27:1 (1989) and Li'l Abner (1993) arithmetic.

### 5. Temperament and psychology

- Mr. Market (Graham, Chapter 8): the more manic-depressive, the better for the investor who can ignore or exploit him (1993). "the true investor welcomes volatility" (1993).
- Welcome falling prices as a perpetual buyer welcomes cheaper food; "It's optimism that is the enemy of the rational buyer" (1990). Best purchases made at peak macro fear (1994). Wells Fargo bought in a bank panic (1990); Coke bought through a rising price, unlike the Fannie Mae error (1991).
- But "a contrarian approach is just as foolish as a follow-the-crowd strategy. What's required is thinking rather than polling" (1990). Russell: "Most men would rather die than think. Many do." (1990)
- Patience and inactivity: "it does not pay to be in a hurry" (1992); the Podunk hotel ("You're doing it") (1992); "There's no use running if you're on the wrong road" (1993); wait for the "happy zone" (1994).
- Concentration: Keynes's "fairly large sums into enterprises which one thinks one knows something about" (1991); five to ten businesses for the know-something investor; index funds and dollar-cost averaging for the know-nothing (1993); diversification only where individual bets carry real loss risk (arbitrage, venture) (1993).
- Risk is the possibility of loss or injury, assessed by five business factors, not beta (1993). Tolerance for lumpy results: "lumpy 15% ... smooth 12%" (1990); "slugging percentage, not batting average" (1992); volatility accepted in super-cat (1990–1994).
- Circle of competence: "limiting ourselves always to investment alternatives we think we understand" (1989); "agnostics, not atheists" on airlines, paper, investment banking (1989); "how realistically they define what they don't know" (1992); "pet rock or Barbie" (1993); no competence to spot Xerox, Apple, Wal-Mart early (1991).
- Avoid difficult problems: one-foot hurdles, avoid dragons (1989); "Why search for a needle buried in a haystack when one is sitting in plain sight?" (1993); "Degree-of-difficulty doesn't count" (1994).
- Enjoy the process; relationships over marginal returns ("marrying for money ... insanity if one is already rich") (1989).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons

- **Look-through earnings** (introduced 1989, formalised 1990–1994): reported operating earnings + share of investee retained operating earnings − hypothetical tax (first $30m on $250m; later 14%, the dividend rate), excluding capital gains, special items and restructuring charges. Must grow ~15% for intrinsic value to; target $1.8–1.85bn by 2000. Shown to be conservative by the Cap Cities sale (1993).
- Retained earnings used for buybacks by an investee (Coke) are more valuable than taxed dividends reinvested (1990).
- Purchase-price accounting and goodwill amortization are segregated so businesses are seen "as they would have been reported had we not purchased them" (every year); purchase-premium charges "have no effect on cash or the taxes we pay, and are not, in our view, an economic cost" (1994). Scott Fetzer carried at $148m while earning twice what it did at a $315m price (1994).
- Deferred taxes on unrealized gains: an interest-free, election-triggered loan/transfer tax (1989); rule changes in 1993 charged $145m through earnings for appreciation never run through earnings (1993). Berkshire's corporate-tax drag vs the pretax S&P (1992).
- Free cash flow = operating earnings + D&A − normalized capex (1989). EBDIT is "an abomination"; depreciation is as real as labour; capex can be skipped only like meals (1989).
- Zero-coupon/PIK bonds create "income" for one party without expense to the other; "It is impossible to default on a promise to pay nothing" (1989). Galbraith's "bezzle" (1989).
- Earnings are "pliable as putty" under a charlatan; use accounting as "a beginning, not an end" (1990). Graham's 1936 U.S. Steel satire: negative plant values, wages in options, inventory at $1, contingency reserves (1990 appendix).
- Insurance reserves are estimates; under-reserving masks losses ("touch up the x-rays") (1989–1991); one-year GAAP cycles misfit super-cat, so Berkshire defers super-cat premium recognition until loss or expiry (1991, 1992).
- Post-retirement health benefits must be accrued; cash-basis accounting enabled open-ended promises (1992).
- Stock options are compensation, compensation is an expense, expenses belong in earnings; imprecision is no excuse (1992). Fixed-strike options with low payouts reward retained-earnings compounding, not performance (1994).
- Restructuring charges are the "tuition" shareholders pay for CEO toad-kissing (1992).
- Mark-to-market: all equities now at market under GAAP (1993); the USAir writedown had to go through income when deemed "other than temporary" though net worth already reflected it (1994).
- Book value vs intrinsic value: book value "meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value" in general (1994), but a useful tracking proxy at Berkshire (1992–1994).
- Cost of float formula and the float definition (1990–1994, see §8).
- Corporate overhead as a percentage of operating earnings directly reduces value (1992).

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level

- Headquarters' only tasks: pick managers, treat them right, allocate the cash they send (1990, 1991, 1994). Subsidiaries first deploy earnings where returns are good, then remit the rest (1994).
- Issue shares only "when we receive as much in intrinsic business value as we give" (every year; applied to Dexter 1993, preferred-stock authorization 1994). The bank stock-swap anecdote (1992). EPS accretion/dilution is the wrong test; per-share intrinsic value is the right one, for acquisitions and buybacks alike (1994).
- Most acquisitions destroy acquirer value – "a chain letter in reverse" (1994); animal spirits and advisors' incentives drive deal-making (1994); frog-kissing (1992). Add-on acquisitions under proven managers are low-risk (1992).
- Dividends/buybacks: high-return businesses that cannot reinvest should pay out most earnings or repurchase (1992); Scott Fetzer paid out more than it earned (1994); Coke's buybacks praised (1990).
- Buybacks/tenders as opportunities: General Dynamics' Dutch tender became a 14% position (1992).
- Leverage: conservative policy is not a mistake; 99:1 odds of benefit don't justify a small chance of distress (1989); "aversion to debt, particularly the short-term kind," but modest, properly structured debt is acceptable (1992); zeros issued at 5.5% with tax benefit (1989) and called when no longer attractive (1992); LBO "dagger" theory rejected (1990). Banks' 20:1 leverage makes management quality decisive (1990).
- Preferred/negotiated investments are inferior to open-market purchases and to buying whole businesses (1989, 1992).
- No stock split; shareholder quality and low turnover keep price near intrinsic value (1992).
- Don't "do something marginal simply because we are long on cash" (1993); only $100m+ positions (1994).
- Resist grand strategic visions; opportunism within criteria (1993).

### 8. Insurance and float

- Float = loss reserves + LAE reserves + unearned premium reserves (+ funds held under reinsurance assumed, 1994) − agents' balances − prepaid acquisition costs − deferred charges on assumed reinsurance (− prepaid taxes, 1994). "Float is money we hold but don't own" (1994).
- Cost of float = underwriting loss ÷ average float; compare with long-term government yields and alternative funding; add ~1 point for the double-tax penalty on shareholders (1990). Positive value only if cost is below market rates (1990, 1994). Berkshire beat the government's rate in 19/24 (1990), 20/25 (1991), 21/26 (1992) years; float grew from $17m (1967) to $3.06bn (1994), negative cost in 1993–94.
- Combined ratios are now "largely irrelevant" to Berkshire (1991) and, as rates fell, less useful for industry comparisons (1993). Break-even combined ratio ~107–111 earlier, 106–110 by 1992.
- Industry structure: commodity product, hundreds of sellers, capacity set by managers' "mental state"; profits only when insurers are frightened; "underwriting cycle" a misnomer (1989); "stop digging" (1990); under-reserving hides the truth (1991).
- Long-tail lines: pricing to 110–115 is suicide; need a margin of safety (1990).
- Super-cat: concentrated not spread risk; results near zero or 300% combined; priced to pay out ~90% over time; historical data of limited value; innocents return after quiet years; Berkshire's edge is capital (second-largest P&C net worth; 10–20x competitors), Ajit Jain, certainty of payment and ability to write $400–500m same day; reject 98% of offered business; worst case ~$600m after tax; volume falls when prices fall (1990–1994). Andrew cost $125m (1992); a repeat would cost $600m (1993).
- GEICO's negative cost of float stems from operating efficiency and risk classification (1990); it is the model and now 50% owned (1994).
- Insurance is Berkshire's business with "the greatest potential" and the largest gap between intrinsic and book value (1990, 1991, 1993, 1994).

### 9. Mistakes and what changed in his thinking

- Cigar-butt → quality: Berkshire textiles and Hochschild Kohn taught "wonderful company at a fair price"; "Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner" (1989). "Practice makes permanent" – the switch to good businesses at fair prices (1992). See's taught franchise evaluation despite the "dumb insistence" on $25m (1991).
- Good jockeys/bad horses; avoid difficult problems; institutional imperative; only good people; errors of omission (1989).
- Coca-Cola: 52 years of noticing without buying (1989). Fannie Mae: stopped buying on a price rise, sold the small position – ~$1.4bn foregone (1991). Cap Cities: sold at $4.30 in 1978–80 and again at $63 in 1993 (1994).
- USAir: "unforced error" (1990) → "sloppy analysis ... or by hubris" (1994); lesson on high-cost producers in deregulated commodity industries, and "You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it." Confession that he cannot forecast airlines, paper or investment banking (1989) "even my mother acknowledges" (1992).
- Negotiated preferreds have merely met expectations; open-market purchases are better (1992).
- Insurance: "colossal mistakes" before Goldberg; "no statute of limitations on stupidity" (1990); he was an "innocent" (1991).
- Near-miss on a company with huge post-retirement health liabilities (1982, recounted 1992).
- Media: did not foresee the secular decline; "the rear-view mirror is always clearer than the windshield" (1990, 1991); intrinsic value of media holdings written down mentally but holdings kept.
- Earlier "fuzzy thinking" on value vs growth (1992). Earlier scorn of synergy reversed, tongue in cheek (1990). Corporate jet "dogma ... run over by my karma" (1992). Omaha Royals (1993).
- Dexter (1993) is presented as a triumph – the later admission that paying in stock was a serious error falls outside this batch.
- Shift in capital base: from "very attractive" to "attractive" prices (1992), $100m minimum (1994), "A fat wallet ... is the enemy" (1994).

### 11. Macro/market views

- Forecasting: stock forecasters "make fortune tellers look good"; short-term forecasts are "poison" (1992); political and economic forecasts are "an expensive distraction" to be ignored (1994); the 30-year list of unforeseeable shocks (1994). Yet he will make long-horizon statements: stocks cannot forever outperform businesses, so the next decade's S&P returns will be far lower (1992); Berkshire's net worth will fall in at least one of three years (1989 – nearly fulfilled 1990).
- Interest rates: intrinsic value moves with interest rates (1989, 1994); a 10% discount rate in the media example (1991); 6–7% long rates make Berkshire's price defensible (1993); falling rates cut the value of float (1993).
- Inflation: incurred insurance losses grow ~10% a year regardless of general inflation (1989–1991); inflation and taxation are two of the five risk factors (1993).
- Market timing rejected: "price, rather than time" (1994); buy when apprehension peaks (1994); welcome declines (1990).
- Leverage/debt mania: the 1980s LBO/junk cycle, EBDIT, zeros, PIKs, S&L losses dumped on taxpayers (1989); the "dagger" theory and the Tampa TV station with interest above revenues (1990); junk "mine field" but open to fallen angels and distressed RJR (1990).
- Market structure: the secondary market is "periodically ruled by mass folly" and sets clearing prices far from value; new-issue markets never give bargains (1992). The stock market moves money "from the active to the patient"; "idle rich" vs "energetic rich" (1991).
- Media secular change: more channels, fixed eyeballs, fragmenting ad dollars (1990, 1991).
- Banking: 20:1 leverage, lemming lending, bank-stock panic as buying opportunity (1990).
- Catastrophe risk and climate: global warming could shift odds; coastal exposure makes a storm 10x costlier than 20 years ago; non-California quakes (1992); $5bn of new reinsurance capital will chase premiums (1993, 1994).
- Taxes: corporate double taxation is a structural handicap mitigated by buy-and-hold (1992, 1993); Berkshire pays ~0.5% of all U.S. corporate income tax and has "absolutely no complaint" (1993).
- Derivatives: not discussed in these letters beyond convertible preferreds, Percs and options accounting.
