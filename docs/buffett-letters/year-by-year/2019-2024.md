## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 2019 letter (dated February 22, 2020)

**Context.** GAAP earnings of $81.4 billion, of which $53.7 billion was the swing in unrealized gains on the stock portfolio under the 2018 accounting rule Buffett dislikes; operating earnings $24 billion, "little changed." No major acquisition; $5 billion of buybacks (about 1% of the company). Float reached $129.4 billion; underwriting profit in 16 of the last 17 years. The letter's intellectual core is the "Power of Retained Earnings" section built on Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book, plus a long section on the failings of corporate boards. It ends with a "Road Ahead" succession discussion and the directive in his will that executors not sell Berkshire shares. Berkshire per-share market value +11.0% vs S&P +31.5%.

**Core teachings.**
- Ignore GAAP net income that includes mark-to-market swings; focus on operating earnings. The 2018 rule produced a "crazy 1,900% increase in GAAP earnings" from 2018 to 2019 while intrinsic value "grew steadily."
- Retained earnings are the engine of equity returns (Smith/Keynes): a well-managed company that retains part of its profits has "an element of compound interest" working for it, which is why stocks beat bonds over time.
- Berkshire's capital-deployment hierarchy: (1) reinvest in businesses already owned (depreciation $65 billion over the decade vs $121 billion internal capex); (2) buy new businesses meeting three criteria: good returns on net tangible capital, able and honest managers, sensible price; (3) when 100% ownership is unavailable, buy large non-controlling stakes via the stock market.
- "Non-recognition of earnings" for investees: GAAP records only dividends from non-controlled holdings; the retained portion (table: $3.8 billion dividends vs $8.3 billion retained share across the top 10) is working for Berkshire invisibly. Expectation: capital gains "at least equal to – and probably better than" retained earnings, but "in a highly irregular manner."
- Tom Murphy's rule: "To achieve a reputation as a good manager, just be sure you buy good businesses."
- Acquisitions are like marriage; "it is usually the buyer who encounters unpleasant surprises." Many of his deals disappointed; "more than a few were outright disasters."
- A self-correcting feature of a diversified collection: poor businesses stagnate and shrink as a share of capital while good ones grow and absorb capital at attractive rates (the textile example from 1965 to the early 1980s).
- Earnings should be stated after "interest, depreciation, taxes, non-cash compensation, restructuring charges – all of those pesky, but very real, costs that CEOs and Wall Street sometimes urge investors to ignore."
- The equity portfolio is "an assembly of companies that we partly own" earning more than 20% on net tangible equity without excessive debt, not "a collection of stock market wagers."
- Rates: no forecasting; but if rates stay near current levels and corporate taxes stay low, equities will beat long-term fixed-rate debt "far better." Warning: drops of "50% magnitude or even greater" will occur, so equities are for the person "who does not use borrowed money and who can control his or her emotions."
- Buyback rule restated: buy only if (a) price is below value and (b) ample cash remains afterward; "neither of us feels any urgency to buy an estimated $1 of value for a very real 95 cents"; will not prop the stock.
- Board governance: the one real job is to "Find and retain a talented CEO – possessing integrity, for sure"; audit committees are "no match for managers who wish to game numbers," which is driven by earnings guidance and ego; no CEO ever brings in an articulate critic of a deal; director pay (~$250-300k) compromises "independence"; CEOs want cocker spaniels, not pit bulls; he prefers directors who bought stock with their own money.
- "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut" (Wall Street deal advice).
- Circle of competence by analogy: "if you are Bobby Fischer, you must play only chess for money."

**Businesses discussed.**
- Insurance / National Indemnity: "collect-now, pay-later" float model; float grew $39m (1970) to $129.4bn (2019); any decline would be "at the outside no more than 3% in any year"; underwriting profit "16 of the last 17 years"; the value of float to the industry is much lower now because bond yields are "pathetically low" (2-3 cents per dollar vs 5-6 cents historically); Berkshire's capital and non-insurance earnings give it investment flexibility competitors lack; warns of a "Big One" catastrophe possibly from cyber. Discipline "is a religion, Old Testament style."
- GUARD Insurance (Wilkes-Barre): bought 2012 for $221m (~net worth), premium up 379% by 2019, float up 265% – an example of Ajit Jain's picks and of star managers (Sy Foguel) in unlikely places.
- BHE: residential rates in Iowa up less than 1%/yr since 2000, competitor's rates 61-70% higher; wind self-sufficiency in Iowa by 2021; has retained $28 billion and never paid a dividend ("The more we can invest, the more we like it"); ready for $100 billion-scale projects.
- BNSF and BHE: "two lead dogs," $8.3bn combined earnings.
- Lubrizol fire and the irony that a Berkshire insurer was one of Lubrizol's largest insurers.
- Equity portfolio (Apple, BofA, Coke, Amex, Wells Fargo, airlines, Moody's etc.): valued for >20% returns on net tangible equity without excessive debt. Kraft Heinz noted as carried at $13.8bn GAAP vs $10.5bn market.
- Top-10 table highlights that Apple ($773m dividends / $2,519m retained) and BofA are the biggest contributors to invisible retained earnings.

**Mistakes admitted.** Acquisitions where he wonders "what I was thinking when I proposed"; the 1965 textile purchase as the extreme drag example; being among the CEOs who never invited a critic to argue against an acquisition ("include me among the guilty"). Lesson: buy good businesses, and recognize that bad ones at least shrink in relative importance over time.

### 2020 letter (dated February 27, 2021)

**Context.** COVID year. GAAP earnings $42.5 billion; operating earnings $21.9 billion, down 9%; no sizable acquisitions; an $11 billion write-down almost entirely from Precision Castparts. Berkshire repurchased about 5% of its shares ($24.7 billion). Float $138 billion. 10-year Treasury yield 0.93% at year-end. Annual meeting to be held virtually from Los Angeles with Munger. Per-share market value +2.4% vs S&P +18.4%. Sections: PCC mistake, "Two Strings to Our Bow" (conglomerates), "Family Jewels" (the Big Four), buybacks and Apple, "A Tale of Two Cities" (See's, GEICO, National Indemnity, NFM, Clayton, Pilot), "The Berkshire Partnership" (shareholder buckets, partnership history), and BNSF/BHE as asset-heavy but good businesses.

**Core teachings.**
- Operating earnings "are what count most"; the capital-gains components of GAAP "fluctuate capriciously."
- Marketable stocks are "a collection of businesses"; investees' retained earnings are "invisible" under GAAP but "building value – lots of value"; over 56 years Berkshire has received at least an equal amount of capital gains.
- The PCC lesson: a fine business can still be a bad investment if you overestimate "normalized profit potential" and therefore pay too much; the error was in the earnings estimate and so in "the proper price to pay for the business," not in business quality.
- Why conventional conglomerates fail: great businesses don't want to be bought, so conglomerateurs fish among "so-so companies"; they pay "control" premiums; they manufacture an overvalued stock as acquisition currency ("I'll pay you $10,000 for your dog by giving you two of my $5,000 cats"); promotional accounting; "business 'emperors' are found to have no clothes."
- Berkshire's distinction: it will own "all or part" of good businesses; "Whether Berkshire controls these businesses, however, is unimportant to us." Three criteria: "durable competitive strengths, the capabilities and character of its management, and price."
- Owning a non-controlling piece of a wonderful business "is more profitable, more enjoyable and far less work than struggling with 100% of a marginal enterprise" (learned from Munger and the 20-year textile struggle).
- "You are awarded no points in business endeavors for 'degree of difficulty.'"
- Buybacks: make them only when they enhance per-share intrinsic value and leave ample funds; American CEOs buy more when prices are high – "Our approach is exactly the reverse."
- The compounding math of repurchases: Apple's buybacks plus Berkshire's buybacks mean shareholders own 10% more of Apple than in July 2018 "without requiring you to so much as touch your wallet."
- Bonds "are not the place to be these days"; "Risky loans, however, are not the answer to inadequate interest rates" (the S&L lesson).
- Asset-light businesses are best: "The best results occur at companies that require minimal assets to conduct high-margin businesses – and offer goods or services that will expand their sales volume with only minor needs for additional capital." But asset-heavy BNSF/BHE can still be good investments if incremental investment earns appropriate returns.
- "Never bet against America"; the entrepreneurs' stories (See's, GEICO, National Indemnity, NFM, Clayton, Pilot) illustrate that great businesses are built by individuals with little capital, and that Berkshire's job is "simply not to meddle."
- Shareholder philosophy: "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership"; Phil Fisher's restaurant analogy – don't switch from hamburgers to French cuisine; Berkshire doesn't court analysts because "We already have the investors we want."
- Passive investing rebuttal to activity: a "patient and level-headed monkey" throwing 50 darts at the S&P 500 will do fine "just as long as it never gets tempted to make changes"; all that is required is "the passage of time, an inner calm, ample diversification and a minimization of transactions and fees"; "investors must never forget that their expenses are Wall Street's income."
- Partnership incentive design: in BPL he took no salary, only a share of profits above a 6% bogey with shortfalls carried forward; partners trusted him to have "an extreme aversion to permanent loss of capital."

**Businesses discussed.**
- Precision Castparts: "a fine company – the best in its business," run by Mark Donegan; still expected to earn good returns on net tangible assets; but he overpaid ($11bn write-down).
- The "Big Four" jewels: (1) P/C insurance – unique because its capital base and Berkshire's non-insurance cash flow allow an "equity-heavy investment strategy not feasible for the overwhelming majority of insurers"; (2) BNSF; (3) Apple (5.4%); (4) BHE (91%), earnings $122m to $3.4bn over 21 years.
- Apple: cost $36bn for 5.2%; $11bn sold in 2020 yet ownership rose to 5.4% from Apple's buybacks – "the power of repurchases."
- See's: "When a business manufactures and distributes a non-essential consumer product, the customer is the boss"; "Don't mess with my candy."
- GEICO: Leo Goodwin's 1936 insight that a standardized product could be sold direct at lower price; "my first love (of an investment sort)"; $238,288 of business in 1937 to $35bn.
- National Indemnity: Jack Ringwalt's "odd-ball" risks strategy; bought in 1967 with a 15-minute handshake, "I never asked for an audit"; "the only company in the world prepared to insure certain giant risks."
- Nebraska Furniture Mart: Mrs. B's $2,500 start, $50 cash in 1946; bought 80% in 1983 "again without an audit"; family still runs it.
- Clayton Homes and Pilot: Knoxville founders on shoestrings; each now >$1bn pre-tax; Pilot stake 38% heading to 80% in 2023.
- BNSF: carries ~15% of US non-local ton-miles; $41bn invested since 2010, $20bn above depreciation; has paid $41.8bn dividends while keeping ~$2bn cash so it borrows on its own credit; Carl Ice/Katie Farmer raised margins 2.9 points on a 7% volume decline.
- BHE: pays no dividend; $18bn western transmission project 2006-2030 as a "societal necessity"; proceeded on "trust in America's political, economic and judicial systems."
- Kraft Heinz: carried at $13.3bn equity-method vs $11.3bn market.

**Mistakes admitted.** PCC: "I paid too much for the company... I was simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential." "PCC is far from my first error of that sort. But it's a big one." Also the 20-year textile struggle ("It took me a while to wise up").

### 2021 letter (dated February 26, 2022)

**Context.** Little corporate action ("Alas, there was little action of that sort in 2021"). Operating earnings strong; BNSF record $6bn, BHE record $4bn; Apple "share" of earnings $5.6bn vs $785m dividends. Cash $144bn, of which $120bn in T-bills; two-year buybacks of 9% of shares for $51.7bn. Float $147bn, up $9bn. The letter introduces "Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers," the "Four Giants," the Paul Andrews/TTI story leading to the BNSF acquisition, and the three ways to create value (internal growth, part-ownership of public companies, repurchases). Per-share market value +29.6% vs S&P +28.7%.

**Core teachings.**
- Reporting principle: "report to you what we would like to know if we were the absentee owner and you were the manager"; treat all shareholders equally (no analyst meetings; release news Saturday mornings).
- "We own stocks based upon our expectations about their long-term business performance and not because we view them as vehicles for timely market moves... Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers."
- Goal: "meaningful investments in businesses with both durable economic advantages and a first-class CEO."
- Public markets occasionally offer "pieces of wonderful businesses at wonderful prices" – "shooting-fish-in-a-barrel" – which "very rare in negotiated transactions and never occurs en masse"; it is also "far easier to exit from a mistake" in marketable securities.
- The 1955 Berkshire-Hathaway merger as a cautionary tale: Lehman-endorsed "high hopes," then net worth fell from $51.4m to $22.1m in nine years partly through buybacks, "ill-advised dividends" and plant shutdowns; the industry was on a "non-reversible death march." 1965 fix: redeploy cash and "steer essentially all earnings into a variety of good businesses" – reinvestment plus compounding.
- Earnings definition and warning: BNSF's $6bn is "the old-fashioned sort of earnings that we favor: a figure calculated after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation"; "bull markets breed bloviated bull...."
- Interest rates and valuation: "Long-term interest rates that are low push the prices of all productive investments upward, whether these are stocks, apartments, farms, oil wells, whatever."
- Three ways to increase value per share: (1) raise long-term earning power of controlled businesses (internal opportunities now beat acquisitions but are small vs resources); (2) buy non-controlling stakes in public companies ("Today, though, we find little that excites us"); (3) repurchase shares when the "price/value equation is right" – "the easiest and most certain way."
- Buybacks must not overpay: "it would be value-destroying if we were to overpay when we are buying Berkshire"; "Our appetite remains large but will always remain price-dependent." A high-quality, buy-and-keep shareholder base limits buyback opportunities, and he prefers it that way.
- Repurchases raise float per share ($79,387 to $99,497 per A share in two years).
- Financial fortress: always >$30bn cash; "never dependent on the kindness of strangers (or even that of friends)"; cash-heavy periods are "never pleasant; they are also never permanent."
- Personal allocation: since March 11, 1942, always at least 80% of net worth in equities; "My favored status throughout that period was 100% – and still is."
- Why owners sell to Berkshire (TTI): a competitor would gut duplicated functions and move operations; an LBO firm focuses on an "exit strategy"; Berkshire is a permanent home.
- The insurance business "will never be obsolete," volume grows with economy and inflation, "integrity and capital will forever be important."
- Comfortable investors beat headline-driven ones: "people who are comfortable with their investments will, on average, achieve better results than those who are motivated by ever-changing headlines, chatter and promises."
- Career advice: work in the field and with the people you would choose "if they had no need for money."
- The "orangutan effect": explaining an idea clarifies your own thinking.

**Businesses discussed.**
- Four Giants: (1) the insurance cluster – made to order for Berkshire; (2) Apple – 5.55% stake, each 0.1% worth $100m of earnings, accretion came free via Apple's buybacks; Tim Cook "brilliant"; (3) BNSF – "number one artery of American commerce," record $6bn; (4) BHE – record $4bn, 30-fold since 2000, no "green-washing," reinvests all earnings.
- TTI (Paul Andrews): founded 1971 with $500, now $7.7bn volume; employees 2,387 to 8,043 after Berkshire; earnings up 673%; Paul refused raises. Buffett admired "all the qualities that Charlie and I admire."
- BNSF acquisition story: the 2009 Fort Worth board visit (because of TTI) coincided with a bad Q3 report during the Great Recession; Buffett offered a "better long-term home" and the maximum price; deal in 11 days. "BNSF will be a key asset for Berkshire and our country a century from now."
- Japanese trading companies appear in the holdings table (ITOCHU, Mitsubishi, Mitsui), as do Chevron, BYD (via BHE), Occidental preferred/warrants ($10.7bn), Kraft Heinz 26.6% (equity method, $13.1bn), Pilot 38.6% (going to 80% in 2023).
- Todd Combs and Ted Weschler manage $34bn.

**Mistakes admitted.** "I make many mistakes. Consequently, our extensive collection of businesses includes some enterprises that have truly extraordinary economics, many others that enjoy good economic characteristics, and a few that are marginal." The 1955 textile merger and the original Berkshire. Lesson: marketable securities make exiting mistakes easier.

### 2022 letter (dated February 25, 2023)

**Context.** Record operating earnings of $30.8bn while GAAP earnings swung from +$5.5bn to –$43.8bn to –$2.7bn to +$18.2bn across the four quarters. Acquired Alleghany (Joe Brandon); float rose from $147bn to $164bn. Repurchased 1.2% of shares. Berkshire was the largest owner of eight S&P giants (Amex, BofA, Chevron, Coke, HP, Moody's, Occidental, Paramount). Charlie turned 99; the letter has a "Nothing Beats Having a Great Partner" list of Munger aphorisms. Key sections: "What We Do," "The Secret Sauce" (Coke and Amex), buyback defense, the taxes "I gave at the office" section. Per-share +4.0% vs S&P –18.1%.

**Core teachings.**
- Two forms of ownership: controlled businesses (Berkshire directs capital allocation and picks CEOs; "both trust and rules are essential," Berkshire emphasizes trust "to an unusual – some would say extreme – degree"; "our tolerance for personal misconduct is zero") and passive stock ownership.
- Again: "Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers." Goal: "long-lasting favorable economic characteristics and trustworthy managers."
- Markets: "stocks often trade at truly foolish prices, both high and low. 'Efficient' markets exist only in textbooks." Controlled businesses are "almost never available at bargain valuations."
- Schumpeter's "creative destruction": capitalism "creates an ever-growing pile of losers while concurrently delivering a gusher of improved goods and services."
- Honest scorecard: in 58 years "most of my capital-allocation decisions have been no better than so-so"; results came from "about a dozen truly good decisions – that would be about one every five years" plus the advantage of long-term holding.
- The Secret Sauce: Coke bought for $1.3bn (1988-1994), dividends $75m (1994) to $704m (2022); Amex $1.3bn (1995), dividends $41m to $302m; each now ~5% of net worth. A flat-lined $1.3bn mistake (e.g. a 30-year bond) would be 0.3% of net worth. "The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom. Over time, it takes just a few winners to work wonders."
- GAAP earnings "are 100% misleading when viewed quarterly or even annually"; capital gains matter over decades but "quarter-by-quarter gyrations... totally misinform investors."
- Even operating earnings can be manipulated; beating "expectations" is a "managerial triumph" only to the deceivers; "Bold imaginative accounting" is "one of the shames of capitalism."
- Buyback logic via the three-owner auto dealership: a value-accretive repurchase harms nobody; overpaying transfers value to sellers and to "the friendly, but expensive, investment banker." Critics who say all buybacks are harmful are "either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue."
- Risk management: always hold "a boatload of cash and U.S. Treasury bills"; avoid any possibility of "uncomfortable cash needs at inconvenient times"; "Our CEO will always be the Chief Risk Officer – a task it is irresponsible to delegate"; future CEOs will own Berkshire stock "bought with their own money."
- Forecasting: "near-term economic and market forecasts are worse than useless"; "Huge and entrenched fiscal deficits have consequences"; Berkshire offers "some modest protection from runaway inflation" but "far from perfect."
- Never bet against America: "I have yet to see a time when it made sense to make a long-term bet against America."
- Munger's rules (quoted in the letter; see quotes), including the Graham voting/weighing machine, no leverage ("A string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal zero"), patience, concentration ("You don't... need to own a lot of things in order to get rich"), keep learning.
- Buffett's own rule: "Find a very smart high-grade partner – preferably slightly older than you – and then listen very carefully to what he says."

**Businesses discussed.**
- Coca-Cola and American Express: durable dividend growers, bought and held for ~30 years; the "few winners" that carry the portfolio.
- Alleghany: P/C insurer; valuable because Berkshire's strength lets insurance subsidiaries follow "investment strategies unavailable to virtually all competitors."
- BNSF ($5.9bn) and BHE ($4.3bn) each earn above the $3bn threshold only 128 S&P companies reach.
- See's: 101-year-old unchanged product, $400,309 of sales in two meeting days – "What worked for See's in the days of Henry Ford's model T works now."
- Railroads (via Munger): "Warren and I hated railroad stocks for decades, but the world changed... We were slow to recognize the change, but better late than never."
- USAir and Salomon mentioned as "near-disasters" rescued by luck.

**Mistakes admitted.** Most capital-allocation decisions "no better than so-so"; USAir and Salomon rescued by luck; "slow to recognize the severity" of textile problems; businesses "have died, their products unwanted by the public." Lesson: a dozen great decisions, held for decades, overwhelm many mediocre ones.

## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 2019–2024

### 1. What investing IS

- Investing is buying part-ownership of businesses and sharing in their long-term prosperity, whether the position is 100% or 5%. "Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers" (2021, 2022). Stocks are owned "based upon our expectations about their long-term business performance and not because we view them as vehicles for timely market moves" (2021). The $248bn portfolio is "an assembly of companies that we partly own," not "a collection of stock market wagers" (2019).
- The mechanism of return is retained earnings compounding inside the business (Edgar Lawrence Smith / Keynes, 2019; "What worked for Carnegie and Rockefeller," 2020; the 1965 Berkshire redeployment, 2021). Dividends plus investees' retained earnings, not quarterly price moves, are the economic reality; GAAP makes the retained part "invisible" (2020).
- Time horizon: "almost always far longer than a single year. In many, our thinking involves decades" (2024). Coke and Amex held for ~30 years with no trades in 2023 ("Rip Van Winkle slumber," 2023); Bertie made no trades for 43 years (2023). Investors who are "comfortable with their investments" beat those driven by headlines (2021).
- The target investor resembles someone who saves "to buy a farm or rental property rather than... lottery tickets or 'hot' stocks" (2023).
- Returns come from a few big decisions: "about a dozen truly good decisions – that would be about one every five years" (2022); "a couple of good decisions during a lifetime" while avoiding serious mistakes (2023); "a single winning decision can make a breathtaking difference" (2024).
- Owning stocks is "a 'positive-sum' game" and a dart-throwing monkey that never trades will do fine; what is needed is time, "inner calm, ample diversification and a minimization of transactions and fees" (2020).
- Controlled vs non-controlled ownership is a matter of indifference in principle ("Whether Berkshire controls these businesses, however, is unimportant to us," 2020; "We are impartial in our choice of equity vehicles," 2024), with practical trade-offs: public markets occasionally offer "wonderful businesses at wonderful prices" and make exiting mistakes easier (2021, 2024), while control allows directing capital but is almost never sold (2024).

### 2. What to look for in a business

- The three acquisition criteria: "good returns on the net tangible capital required in their operation," "able and honest managers," and "a sensible price" (2019). Restated as "durable competitive strengths, the capabilities and character of its management, and price" (2020); "durable economic advantages and a first-class CEO" (2021); "long-lasting favorable economic characteristics and trustworthy managers" (2022); "good economics that are fundamental and enduring" (2023).
- Return on capital: the equity portfolio earns "more than 20% on the net tangible equity capital required to run their businesses" without excessive debt (2019); Apple, Amex, Coke, Moody's "earn very high returns on the net tangible equity required" (2024).
- Capital intensity: "The best results occur at companies that require minimal assets to conduct high-margin businesses – and offer goods or services that will expand their sales volume with only minor needs for additional capital" (2020). Berkshire owns a few of these "but they are relatively small and, at best, grow slowly." Asset-heavy businesses (BNSF, BHE) can still be good investments if incremental capital earns "appropriate returns" (2020), but "railroads eat capital" and capex above depreciation is "particularly disadvantageous in capital-intensive industries" (2023).
- The best of all: "the rare enterprise that can deploy additional capital at high returns in the future. Owning only one of these companies – and simply sitting tight – can deliver wealth almost beyond measure" (2023). BHE's retention of $28bn with no dividend is welcomed because the reinvestment opportunities exist ("The more we can invest, the more we like it," 2019).
- Durability/moat examples: Coke and Amex products "traveled" worldwide and address "timeless essentials" (liquids; "unquestioned financial trust") (2023); See's product "haven't been materially altered in 101 years" (2022); insurance "will never be obsolete" (2021); BNSF is "indispensable" and will be a key asset "a century from now" (2021, 2023); National Indemnity is "the only company in the world prepared to insure certain giant risks" (2020).
- Customer power: "When a business manufactures and distributes a non-essential consumer product, the customer is the boss" (2020).
- Growth that destroys value: the pre-1965 textile company retained, repurchased, paid dividends and shut plants on a "non-reversible death march" (2021); conglomerates buying "so-so companies" with inflated stock (2020); Coke and Amex both failed when they "tried expanding into unrelated areas" (2023). A business whose business "was headed for extinction" is a bad buy however cheap (2024).
- Predictability is hard: "It's harder than you would think to predict which will be the winners and losers" (2023); Schumpeter's "creative destruction" (2022).
- Warning sign: a "venerable pillar" paying no income tax for a decade is "a blinking yellow light" (2024).

### 3. What to look for in management

- Honesty first: "able and honest managers" (2019); zero tolerance for personal misconduct (2022); McCulloch's "Never deal with a rascal" and "Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked" (2023); fidelity failures hurt "beyond their financial impact" (2024).
- Owner-orientation and passion: Mark Donegan pours the same energy in after sale (2020); Paul Andrews refusing raises and choosing Berkshire over synergies/LBO exit (2021); Pete Liegl asking for $100,000 base plus 10% of incremental earnings, adjusted for capital employed (2024); Japanese CEOs "far less aggressive about their own compensation" (2023, 2024).
- Capital allocation as the manager's key job: investees that buy back stock at good prices are applauded (Apple, 2020-2021; Japanese houses, 2023-2024); companies that overpay for buybacks transfer value to sellers and bankers (2022).
- Candour: Berkshire used "mistake" or "error" 16 times in 2019-23 letters while most companies never do; "if you start fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney" (2024). Report "both the good and bad news, delivered directly from their CEO and not from an investor-relations officer" (2023).
- Institutional imperative / boards: CEOs never bring in a critic of a coveted deal; director fees make "independence" a fiction; CEOs pick "cocker spaniels" not "pit bulls"; earnings guidance drives number-gaming "more often prompted by ego than by a desire for financial gain" (2019).
- Compensation: no options or "one-sided forms of compensation; if you lose money, so do we" (2024); future CEOs must own Berkshire stock "bought with their own money" (2022); directors who bought stock with their own savings are preferred (2019).
- Pedigree is irrelevant: "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!"; "nature swamping nurture" (2024). Managers "who regard 65 as just another birthday" (2023); Mrs. B worked until 103 (2020).
- Delegation and trust: "both trust and rules are essential. Berkshire emphasizes the former to an unusual – some would say extreme – degree" (2022); the job is "simply not to meddle" (2020). Deals done without audits on a handshake (National Indemnity, NFM, 2020; Forest River, 2024).
- Insurance managers specifically must not be optimists (2023, 2024): "nervous, but not paralyzed."

### 4. Valuation

- Price is the third leg of every criterion list ("available at a sensible price," 2019; "price," 2020). The PCC lesson: valuation error came from overestimating "the average amount of future earnings and, consequently... the proper price to pay" (2020).
- Munger's reformulation adopted by Buffett: "wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices" rather than "fair businesses at wonderful prices"; Graham's approach "works but only when practiced at small scale" (2023).
- Intrinsic value is imprecise: "Calculations of intrinsic value are far from precise. Consequently, neither of us feels any urgency to buy an estimated $1 of value for a very real 95 cents" (2019). Buybacks only below intrinsic value and with ample cash left (2019, 2020, 2021, 2023).
- Interest rates as the master variable: "Long-term interest rates that are low push the prices of all productive investments upward... interest rates will always be important" (2021). At 2.5% long bonds, 20%-ROE businesses are "mind-blowing" by comparison (2019); 10-year yield of 0.93% down 94% from 1981 means "bonds are not the place to be" (2020).
- Markets misprice: stocks "often trade at truly foolish prices, both high and low. 'Efficient' markets exist only in textbooks" (2022); controlled businesses "are almost never available at bargain valuations" (2022).
- Replacement value vs purchase price: BNSF's assets carried at $70bn would cost $500bn to replicate; Berkshire bought "at a small fraction of its replacement value" yet earns "a pittance on the replacement value" because capex exceeds depreciation (2023). Book value vs market: Kraft Heinz carried at equity-method value above market (2019, 2020, 2021).
- Japan: bought because financial records showed "the low prices of their stocks" (2024); the yen-funding removes currency from the valuation (2023, 2024).
- Growth and compounding: a $1.3bn Coke/Amex stake growing dividends from $75m to $704m becomes 5% of net worth, while a flat $1.3bn becomes 0.3% – "The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom" (2022).
- Margin of safety in structure, not just price: always >$30bn cash (2021), "boatload of cash and U.S. Treasury bills" (2022), "Never risk permanent loss of capital" (2023).

### 5. Temperament and psychology

- Control emotions and avoid leverage: equities beat bonds "for the individual who does not use borrowed money and who can control his or her emotions. Others? Beware!" (2019); Munger: "A string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal zero" (2022).
- Inactivity: Coke/Amex "rewarded our inaction" (2023); Bertie's 43 years without a trade (2023); the dart-throwing monkey (2020); "sit quietly, listening to no one" (2023).
- Patience: "Patience can be learned" (Munger, 2022); "Patience pays" (2023); building Japanese positions "is like turning a battleship" (2023).
- Fear/greed and panics: markets "can – and will – unpredictably seize up or even vanish" (1914, 2001, 2008); participants "are neither more emotionally stable nor better taught than when I was in school"; "casino-like behavior... now resides in many homes" (2023). Berkshire's edge is being able to act with "huge sums and certainty of performance" when others panic (2023).
- Ignore pundits, guidance, forecasts: "pundits should always be ignored" (2023); forecasts "worse than useless" (2022); Fed actions, "earnings 'miss'" and "subject du jour" are not reasons to sell (2019).
- Concentration: Buffett has had a majority of his net worth in equities since 1942 and "a full 99% of my net worth lodged in Berkshire stock" (2019); his will instructs executors to hold Berkshire despite "an extreme concentration of assets" (2019); Munger: "You don't, however, need to own a lot of things in order to get rich" (2022). Yet for ordinary savers he recommends "ample diversification" via the index (2020).
- Circle of competence: "if you are Bobby Fischer, you must play only chess for money" (2019); "Some we can value; some we can't" (2023); no ability to forecast rates or currencies (2019, 2023, 2024).
- Rationality: Munger – "If you don't care whether you are rational or not, you won't work on it" (2022); "Early on, write your desired obituary – and then behave accordingly" (2022).
- Wall Street's incentives: "their expenses are Wall Street's income" (2020); "what truly causes its denizens' juices to flow is feverish activity" (2023); "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut" (2019).
- Correct mistakes without "thumb-sucking" (2024); "don't bail away in a sinking boat if you can swim to one that is seaworthy" (Munger, 2022).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons

- Mark-to-market in GAAP earnings (post-2018 rule): produces "crazy" swings ($4bn in 2018 to $81.4bn in 2019; quarterly –$43.8bn to +$18.2bn in 2022; "$5 billion a day"); "worse-than-useless"; "100% misleading when viewed quarterly or even annually" (2019, 2020, 2022, 2023, 2024). Focus on operating earnings, defined as GAAP excluding capital gains/losses (2022).
- Operating earnings themselves can be manipulated: "Bold imaginative accounting... one of the shames of capitalism"; beating expectations is "disgusting" (2022); audit committees are no match for CEOs who game numbers (2019).
- Earnings definition: after "interest, depreciation, taxes, non-cash compensation, restructuring charges – all of those pesky, but very real, costs that CEOs and Wall Street sometimes urge investors to ignore" (2019); "after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation" (2021); "'EBITDA' is a banned measurement at Berkshire" (2023); "EBITDA, a flawed favorite of Wall Street, is not for us" (2024).
- Adjusted earnings: "bull markets breed bloviated bull...." (2021).
- Look-through / retained earnings of investees: GAAP records only dividends from non-controlled holdings; Berkshire's share of retained earnings ($8.3bn vs $3.8bn dividends, 2019; Apple $5.6bn vs $785m, 2021) is real value "not in a way that deposits those gains directly into Berkshire's reported earnings" (2019, 2020, 2021).
- Equity-method accounting: Kraft Heinz carried at Berkshire's share of audited net worth ($13.8bn, $13.3bn, $13.1bn) while market value was lower ($10.5bn, $11.3bn) (2019-2021); Pilot moving to consolidation at 80% (2021).
- Write-downs quantify past overpayment: the $11bn PCC charge "is almost entirely the quantification of a mistake I made in 2016" (2020).
- Depreciation vs maintenance capex: at BNSF, outlays above GAAP depreciation of $22bn over 14 years mean dividends to the owner "will regularly fall considerably short of BNSF's reported earnings" unless debt rises (2023). Reported earnings overstate distributable cash in capital-intensive businesses.
- Insurance accounting: long-tail lines can show "large but fictitious profits" for decades; estimation mistakes "can be huge"; "surprises... are almost always negative"; inflation "both monetary and the 'legal' variety" is a wild card (2023, 2024).
- Float is not on the GAAP balance sheet as value: "Though not recognized in our financial statements, this float has been an extraordinary asset" (2022); the $9bn float increase "is not reflected in our GAAP presentation of earnings and net worth" (2021).
- Currency gains on yen debt flow through GAAP income periodically ($1.9bn through 2023; $2.3bn cumulative after-tax, $850m in 2024) though they reflect a hedge, not operations (2023, 2024).
- Issuing shares "immediately juices net worth" – why GAAP net worth growth is not the goal and why Berkshire is "highly averse to issuing shares" (2023).
- Conglomerate accounting tricks: inflated stock as currency, "'imaginative' accounting maneuvers that were, at best, deceptive" (2020).
- Fixed-asset ownership "does not, in itself, signal an investment triumph" (2020) – a large PP&E line is not a merit.

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level

- Hierarchy: reinvest in existing businesses first ($121bn capex vs $65bn depreciation over the decade, 2019; "Reinvestment in productive operational assets will forever remain our top priority"); then acquire whole businesses; then buy non-controlling stakes; then repurchase shares (2019, 2021).
- Retained earnings test implicit: retention is justified where reinvestment opportunities earn attractive rates (BHE; Japanese houses retaining 2/3 of earnings "to build their many businesses"), and is condemned where it fails (textile-era Berkshire; "some of our investees will disappoint, adding little, if anything, to the value of their company by retaining earnings," 2020). Berkshire's single dividend (1967) is called "a bad dream" because reinvestment compounded (2024).
- Buybacks: only when (a) below intrinsic value and (b) ample cash remains (2019); "We will not, however, prop the stock at any level" (2019); the reverse of CEOs who buy high (2020); "value-destroying if we were to overpay" (2021); "the easiest and most certain way for us to increase your wealth" when price/value is right (2021); the three-owner dealership proof that accretive buybacks harm no one (2022); "What is sensible at a discount to business-value becomes stupid if done at a premium" (2023); repurchases also raise float per share (2021). Scale: ~1% in 2019, ~5% in 2020, 9% over 2020-21 ($51.7bn), 1.2% in 2022.
- Acquisitions: they are "similar to marriage" and the buyer gets the surprises (2019); conglomerateurs' premium-plus-inflated-stock model is a trap (2020); internal opportunities now beat acquisitions on return (2021); size has eliminated most candidates (2023).
- Issuing stock: strongly averse (2023); used a little in buying BHE's minority (2024). The conglomerate "dog for two cats" warning (2020).
- Leverage/debt: BNSF borrows on its own credit by keeping cash and paying out only the residual (2020); Berkshire will not raise BNSF debt to fund dividends (2023); "Annual debt maturities are never material" (2023); no reliance on commercial paper or bank lines in 2008 (2023); yen debt only as a currency-matched, fixed-rate hedge (2023, 2024).
- Cash policy: always >$30bn (2021); a "boatload" of T-bills (2022); cash is a residual of not finding opportunities, "never pleasant... never permanent" (2021); equities will always be the majority (2024).
- Selling: Berkshire "almost never sells controlled businesses unless we face what we believe to be unending problems" (2024); will "not knowingly throw good money after bad" (2023).
- Owner-oriented investor relations: no analyst meetings, Saturday releases, Fisher's restaurant consistency (2020, 2021).

### 8. Insurance and float

- Model: "collect-now, pay-later" leaves float that is "money we call 'float' – that will eventually go to others" but is invested meanwhile (2019); float behaves like bank deposits with a stable aggregate (2020); "very sticky" and "immune from precipitous decline" (2021); any decline limited to ~3% a year (2019).
- Growth: $39m (1970), $27.9bn (2000), $129bn (2019), $138bn (2020), $147bn (2021), $164bn (2022, with Alleghany), $171bn (2024); 8,000-fold since 1967 (2022).
- Cost: cumulative underwriting profit means float has been "costless" (2020), "less than nothing" (2021); 16 of 17 years profitable through 2019; $32bn after-tax underwriting profit over 20 years, 3.3 cents per premium dollar (2024). But "We will most certainly not have an underwriting profit in 16 of the next 17 years" (2019) and some years will bring "very large" losses (2021).
- Why Berkshire's float is worth more than peers': capital strength and non-insurance cash flow allow an "equity-heavy investment strategy not feasible for the overwhelming majority of insurers" (2020, 2022); low bond yields have slashed the value of float for the industry to "2 cents or 3 cents" per dollar (2019); reaching for yield via risky loans is "dangerous" (2019, 2020).
- Underwriting discipline: "a religion, Old Testament style" (2019); underwriters "can't be optimists" (2023); "nervous, but not paralyzed" (2024); shrink when prices are inadequate – writing underpriced policies "is corporate suicide" (2024); contest runaway verdicts and fraud (2024); write one-year not ten-year policies on climate-exposed risks (2024).
- Risk: "The Big One" (wind, quake, or cyber) will come and losses will be "very big," but Berkshire will "be eager to add to our business the next day" (2019); climate change "may have been announcing its arrival" (2024); long-tail accounting dangers (2024); legal inflation (2023).
- Growth drivers: economic growth and inflation (2021); "No risk – no need for insurance" (2024).
- People: Ajit Jain hired 1986 with "None" experience – "Nobody's perfect" (2021); GEICO as "first love" (2020) and a gem Todd Combs repolished (2024); GUARD (2019); Alleghany/Joe Brandon (2022); not dependent on reinsurers – "a material and enduring cost advantage" (2024).

### 9. Mistakes and what changed in his thinking

- The original sin: buying control of Berkshire in 1965 – "a dumb decision" (Munger, 2023), "a mistake – my mistake – and one that plagued us for two decades" (2024); the 20-year textile struggle taught him that a non-controlling piece of a wonderful business beats 100% of a marginal one (2020). The 1955 merger as "death march" (2021).
- Graham to Munger: the explicit statement that Munger told him in 1965 to "abandon everything you learned from your hero, Ben Graham" and buy "wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices," which he followed "with much back-sliding" (2023). Charlie "repeatedly, jerked me back to sanity when my old habits surfaced" (2023).
- Precision Castparts: overpaid in 2016 because of over-optimism about normalized earnings; $11bn write-down; "far from my first error of that sort. But it's a big one" (2020).
- BHE/utilities: failed to "anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns" – "a costly mistake" (2023).
- BNSF: margins have slipped relative to all five peers since purchase; mis-forecast 2023 earnings (2023).
- Acquisitions generally: "more than a few were outright disasters" (2019); "a number that I should not have purchased" (2024); most capital-allocation decisions "no better than so-so" (2022).
- USAir and Salomon: "near-disasters" escaped by luck (2022).
- Railroads: "Warren and I hated railroad stocks for decades... We were slow to recognize the change" (Munger, 2022).
- The 1967 dividend: "a bad dream" (2024).
- Governance: never bringing in a critic of his own acquisitions ("include me among the guilty," 2019).
- Manager misjudgments: fidelity failures, "wandering in the wilderness" in insurance before Ajit (2023, 2024).
- Changes in thinking across the batch: (a) increasing acceptance that size caps returns – from "the next five... jewels" optimism (2020) to "we have no possibility of eye-popping performance... slightly better" (2023); (b) willingness to go abroad and use currency-matched debt (Japan, 2019 onward, discussed 2023-24); (c) heavier reliance on buybacks as the "easiest and most certain" value lever when nothing else is attractive (2020-2022); (d) a stronger, repeated emphasis on correcting mistakes fast ("thumb-sucking," 2024) and on candour as a discipline against self-deception (2024); (e) explicit shift of the letter's framing to succession – Greg Abel "ready to be CEO tomorrow" (2023), "it won't be long before Greg Abel replaces me" (2024).

### 11. Macro and market views

- Interest rates: no forecasting ability; pundits reveal "far more about themselves than they reveal about the future" (2019); at near-zero rates equities "will over time perform far better than long-term, fixed-rate debt" (2019); 10-year at 0.93% down 94% from 1981, negative yields in Germany/Japan – "Fixed-income investors worldwide... face a bleak future" (2020); low rates "push the prices of all productive investments upward" (2021); rising T-bill yields later boosted investment income (2023, 2024).
- Reaching for yield: "Risky loans... are not the answer to inadequate interest rates" – the S&L lesson (2020); lower-quality bonds and illiquid "alternatives" are "dangerous games" (2019).
- Inflation and currency: Berkshire offers "some modest protection from runaway inflation... far from perfect"; "Huge and entrenched fiscal deficits have consequences" (2022); "Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails... Fixed-coupon bonds provide no protection against runaway currency"; businesses and skilled people cope with monetary instability; plea to Uncle Sam "to maintain a stable currency" (2024); insurance claims face "monetary and the 'legal'" inflation (2023).
- Forecasting: "near-term economic and market forecasts are worse than useless" (2022); "No one knows what oil prices will do" (2023); no view on FX, hence currency-neutral yen funding (2023, 2024).
- Market timing: none; "Anything can happen to stock prices tomorrow... major drops in the market, perhaps of 50% magnitude or even greater" (2019); markets "will – unpredictably seize up or even vanish"; "We did not predict the time of an economic paralysis but we were always prepared for one" (2023).
- Market behaviour: "far more casino-like"; speed of communication "facilitate instant worldwide paralysis" (2023); bull markets breed "bloviated" adjusted earnings (2021); "Investing illusions can continue for a surprisingly long time" (2020).
- Leverage: avoid – "the individual who does not use borrowed money" (2019); Munger's "times zero" (2022); Berkshire's own structure of no material maturities and no reliance on credit markets (2023).
- America: "Never bet against America" (2020, 2021); "The American Tailwind" (2019, 2022, 2023); America would have done fine without Berkshire, not the reverse (2021, 2022, 2024); capitalism as savings deployed – "If America had consumed all that it produced, the country would have been spinning its wheels" (2024).
- Industry structure views: utilities' "fixed-but-satisfactory-return pact" breaking, possible shift to public power (2023); rail as the most efficient long-haul mode but capital-hungry with politically set wages (2023); US shale ending energy dependence (2023); climate change raising convective-storm and wildfire losses (2023, 2024).
- Taxes: Berkshire as a large and growing taxpayer ($3.6bn 2019; $3.3bn 2021; $32bn 2012-21; $26.8bn 2024, a record) framed as the fruit of 60 years of reinvestment rather than dividends (2019, 2021, 2022, 2024).
