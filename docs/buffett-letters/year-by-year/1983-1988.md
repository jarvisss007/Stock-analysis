## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 1983

**Context.** Shareholders rose from ~1,900 to ~2,900, mostly via the Blue Chip Stamps merger (now 100% owned; Wesco 80%). Book value rose 32% ($737.43 to $975.83/share); 19-year compound rate 22.6%. The "high point" was buying 90% of Nebraska Furniture Mart from Rose Blumkin. Berkshire's own insurance combined ratio was a terrible 121 vs. industry 111; GEICO (one-third owned) wrote at 96. Berkshire restates its "catechism" of owner-related business principles for the new shareholders.

**Core teachings.**
- Berkshire's form is corporate but its "attitude is partnership"; the company is "a conduit through which our shareholders own the assets." Directors "eat our own cooking" (4 of 5 have >50% of family net worth in BRK).
- Goal: "maximize the average annual rate of gain in intrinsic business value on a per-share basis" - not size. Preference is to own directly "a diversified group of businesses that generate cash and consistently earn above-average returns on capital"; second choice is partial ownership via marketable stocks.
- Accounting consequences don't drive decisions: he would rather buy "$2 of earnings that is not reportable by us under standard accounting principles than... $1 of earnings that is reportable." Unreported (look-through) earnings are expected to show up in intrinsic value via capital gains.
- Little debt, and when used, long-term fixed-rate. "We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet."
- Retained-earnings test: retention must deliver "at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained", tested on a five-year rolling basis.
- Stock issuance only "when we receive as much in business value as we give" - applies to mergers, options, convertibles and stock-for-debt swaps alike.
- They will not sell good businesses "regardless of price", and are reluctant to sell sub-par ones; warns against pouring capital into bad industries ("about as rewarding as struggling in quicksand"), yet rejects "gin rummy managerial behavior."
- Candour as policy: "the CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private." But investment ideas are not discussed because they are "rare, valuable and subject to competitive appropriation."
- The "grizzlies" test for appraising a business: ask how you would like to compete with it given ample capital and skilled people. NFM = "the ideal business - one built upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners."
- One-year results don't matter; use at least a five-year test; "Red lights should start flashing if the five-year average annual gain falls much below the return on equity earned over the period by American industry."
- Book value vs intrinsic value: "Book value tells you what has been put in; intrinsic business value estimates what can be taken out." Intrinsic value = "future cash output discounted to present value." The two-children-through-college analogy.
- Conversion away from tangible assets: taught 35 years earlier "to favor tangible assets and to shun businesses whose value depended largely upon economic Goodwill" - a bias that "caused me to make many important business mistakes of omission." Now prefers "businesses that possess large amounts of enduring Goodwill and that utilize a minimum of tangible assets."
- Annual realized capital gains are "meaningless"; aggregate realized plus unrealized gains over years are "very important."
- Newspaper franchise strength is best measured by penetration ratio; a newsrich product (50% news hole vs. high-30s typical) is "by intent."
- Price vs value: "(In candy, as in stocks, price and value can differ; price is what you give, value is what you get.)"
- Insurance's long lead-time means current results reflect decisions two or more years back; this characteristic "enabled us to make a great deal of money in GEICO; we could picture what was likely to happen well before it actually occurred."
- Stock splits: a rational stock price requires rational shareholders; "Manic-depressive personalities produce manic-depressive valuations." Shareholder self-selection via consistent communication ("opera" vs "rock concert"). "People who buy for non-value reasons are likely to sell for non-value reasons."
- Trading friction: "A hyperactive stock market is the pickpocket of enterprise." The 12%-ROE / 100% turnover / 1% commission example loses 1/6 of earnings to friction - an effective 16 2/3% tax. Markets as "invisible foot."
- Acquisition criteria (the "ad"): $5m+ after-tax earnings, demonstrated consistent earning power (no projections, no turnarounds), good ROE with little debt, management in place, simple businesses, an offering price.

**Businesses discussed.**
- *Nebraska Furniture Mart*: $100m+ sales from one 200,000 sq ft store; buys brilliantly, expense ratios "competitors dont even dream about", passes savings to customers. Bought 90% for ~$55m (per 1984 letter) with no audit. Management = Blumkin family character.
- *Buffalo News*: #1 weekday penetration among the 100 largest US papers; penetration = "best measure of the strength of its franchise." Negatives: shift from ROP linage to lower-margin preprints.
- *See's*: "valuable and solid consumer franchise"; problem is flat poundage (same-store down 8% cumulative since 1979) masked by price rises; "units sold per store" is the key metric. "We regard product quality as sacred."
- *Insurance (own)*: "absolutely terrible"; combined ratio 121; roots in his own decisions. New lines: structured settlements and loss-reserve transfers, where "premier financial strength" is the edge; no profit front-ended.
- *GEICO*: "a truly exceptional business idea and an exceptional management" (Byrne, Snyder, Lou Simpson); Berkshire's one-third share of GEICO premiums exceeds its own volume by 80%.
- Holdings table: GEICO, General Foods, R.J. Reynolds, Washington Post, Time, Affiliated Publications, Handy & Harman, Interpublic, Media General, Ogilvy & Mather.

**Mistakes admitted.** The insurance underwriting results: "I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home to roost." The decades-long "mistakes of omission" from shunning goodwill-based businesses. Early textile book value "considerably overstated intrinsic business value" - like "investment in a largely-wasted education."

**Verbatim quotes (1983).**

### 1984

**Context.** Net worth gain $152.6m (13.6%) - "mediocre" against 22.1% 20-year record. He warns that 15%/yr for a decade would need $3.9bn of profits and "a few big ideas." Hard to find equities meeting both qualitative and quantitative tests - the hardest in ten years. Big themes: proportionate redemptions (GEICO, General Foods) and the auditor dispute; a long essay on share repurchases; NFM economics vs Levitz; newspaper economics; a humbling 134 combined ratio and a full confession on loss-reserving errors; the WPPSS bond purchase as a "business"; a treatise on dividend policy. Subsequent event: agreement to buy 3m shares of Capital Cities at $172.50.

**Core teachings.**
- "Economic gains must be evaluated by comparison with the capital that produces them."
- Strategic plan = wait for big ideas to "pop up occasionally."
- Repurchases: when outstanding businesses sell far below intrinsic value, "no alternative action can benefit shareholders as surely as repurchases"; companies "often find it easy to get $2 of present value for $1," whereas acquisitions often fail to get $1 for $1. The subtle benefit: repurchases *demonstrate* pro-shareholder management, which rationally raises the price investors should pay. "The key word is demonstrated." Greenmail is "odious and repugnant."
- NFM's edge is quantified: gross margin roughly half of Levitz's 44.4%; operating expenses 16.5% of sales vs 35.6%; customers save $30m/year; that is what widens the geographic reach. Blumkin "secrets": energy; "define with extraordinary realism their area of special competence and act decisively on all matters within it"; ignore propositions outside it; behave high-grade. "Sell cheap and tell the truth."
- Trust-based deal: no audit, no inventory count, no title check - "We gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word."
- Dominant-newspaper economics: "While first-class newspapers make excellent profits, the profits of third-rate papers are as good or better - as long as either class of paper is dominant within its community." Since the market doesn't impose quality, "management must impose its own." Wasted circulation hurts because costs follow gross circulation while ad revenue follows useful circulation.
- Insurance: the "key index" to underwriting profitability is the annual % change in industry premium volume; ~10%/yr growth needed to hold the combined ratio. The buyer of insurance "receives only a promise"; its value "should be appraised against the possibility of adversity, not prosperity."
- Prefers business performance to market performance: "we would far rather have the business value of GEICO increase by X during the year, while market value decreases, than have the intrinsic value increase by only 1/2 X with market value soaring."
- GEICO's "major, sustainable competitive advantage" (low-cost auto insurance) is "a rare asset in business generally, and its almost non-existent in the field of financial services" - and GEICO failed in every endeavour outside its core.
- Loss reserving: P/C financials are "at best, only a first rough draft of earnings"; auditors can't police them; "the corpse is supposed to file the death certificate"; insurers can be "broke but flush" and the "walking dead" write business at any price.
- Business-valuation approach applied to bonds: WPPSS = $139m cost "earning" $22.7m after tax = 16.3% unleveraged, with no premium to capital, versus $250-300m for an equivalent business - but with a capped upside (face value only 48% above cost) and default risk. The 1946 1%-yield bond = buying a business earning 1% on book at book value. Graham: "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike."
- Most businesses also have limited upside unless more capital is invested, because "most businesses are unable to significantly improve their average returns on equity." A 12% bond with reinvested coupons grows like a 12% ROE business that retains everything.
- Unconventional decisions: managers avoid "the intelligent-but-with-some-chance-of-looking-like-an-idiot decision"; "no individual lemming has ever received bad press." Buffett/Munger with 47% of the stock "behave with Berkshires money as we would with our own."
- Concentration is appropriate only from "a position of exceptional financial strength." Billy Rose on over-diversification: "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well."
- Secrecy about securities: coat-tail riders would have cost ~$5m on WPPSS.
- Long-term bonds disliked because "bonds are as sound as a dollar - and we view the long-term outlook for dollars as dismal"; small chance of runaway inflation driven by deficits; an all-bond portfolio has "a small but unacceptable wipe out risk."
- Dividend policy: "all earnings are not created equal" - inflation makes some "restricted" earnings ersatz (Con Ed's "Dig We Must" gold-into-lead). Unrestricted earnings should be retained only if "for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners," i.e. incremental returns at or above those generally available. The 10%-perpetual-bond-with-reinvest-option analogy. Beware the Pro-Am effect: a great core business camouflages bad allocation elsewhere. "If earnings have been unwisely retained, it is likely that managers, too, have been unwisely retained."
- Berkshire's own three "cornerstone" businesses (textiles, department stores, trading stamps) all failed; "Only by committing available funds to much better businesses were we able to overcome these origins."

**Businesses discussed.** NFM (efficiency, trust); See's (only one good-sized competitor is profitable; refuses preservatives/freezing; 40% of volume and 75% of profit in 4 weeks before Christmas); Buffalo News (dominant-paper economics, penetration #1 weekday); own insurance (134 combined ratio, financial strength now a competitive asset in structured settlements/loss-reserve assumptions; "only a handful of companies exists with which we will reinsure our own liabilities"); GEICO (36% owned; business value rose while stock static); Exxon (new, 4th largest holding - repurchases); Washington Post, General Foods (repurchasers); WPPSS 1,2,3 bonds; Capital Cities (Murphy and Burke "tops in both ability and integrity").

**Mistakes admitted.** Loss-reserving errors "substantial and recently have always presented a better underwriting picture than was truly the case" (table: 1983 reported -$33.2m, corrected -$51.0m). Consequences: couldn't be counted on, acted with less urgency, overpaid taxes. Underestimated "the mushrooming tendency of juries and courts to make the deep pocket pay." The "rented suit" story. Original three cornerstone businesses all chosen by Buffett and Munger: "(Who says you cant lose em all?)"

**Verbatim quotes (1984).**

- "When companies with outstanding businesses and comfortable financial positions find their shares selling far below intrinsic value in the marketplace, no alternative action can benefit shareholders as surely as repurchases." (1984)

- "His heart is not listening to his mouth - and, after a while, neither will the market." (1984)

- "In GEICOs case, as in all of our investments, we look to business performance, not market performance. If we are correct in expectations regarding the business, the market eventually will follow along." (1984)
- "Insurance is different: you can be broke but flush." (1984)
- "Simply put, we feel that if we can buy small pieces of businesses with satisfactory underlying economics at a fraction of the per-share value of the entire business, something good is likely to happen to us - particularly if we own a group of such securities." (1984)

(1984)

### 1985

**Context.** Net worth +$613.6m (48.2%) - "neither [Halley's Comet nor this gain] will be seen again in my lifetime." 21-year rate 23.2%. Events: Capital Cities/ABC position (3m shares at $172.50), Scott & Fetzer acquisition (~$320m), 7% quota-share of Fireman's Fund (following Jack Byrne), sale of General Foods to Philip Morris ($338m pre-tax gain). The textile business was shut down. Market no longer offers bargains; Berkshire's discount to intrinsic value has disappeared. Insurance market tightened; premium volume tripled. Long sections on incentive compensation/stock options and on reinsurance economics.

**Core teachings.**
- Two factors cap future returns: an unattractive market and size - "an iron law of business is that growth eventually dampens exceptional economics." No company with $1bn+ of equity has kept earning 20%+ for ten years while reinvesting everything.
- Advantages: no quarterly pressure, unlimited scope, "we love our work."
- Market price vs business value: "in the end, investor gains must equal business gains," but long under/overvaluation distributes gains inequitably among owners depending on luck. Premium paid above intrinsic value must be maintained for a new owner just to match the business.
- Institutions are not a force for stability: stocks they monitor "have often been among the most inappropriately valued." Graham's "Oil discovered in hell" story on professional herd behaviour.
- Consolidated figures are useless; segment data are "equally essential for investors"; Al Capone on "a kind word and a gun."
- Realized gains in a year are "meaningless" - the college-graduation analogy. The four factors in the General Foods win: bargain price, fine economics, able shareholder-oriented management, and a buyer paying full value - "we devote our attention to attractive purchases, not to the possibility of attractive sales."
- Proportionate redemptions benefit non-sellers too when done below intrinsic value.
- Munger: study mistakes rather than successes - "All I want to know is where Im going to die so Ill never go there."
- Textile lessons: a commodity business in world oversupply; capital projects that look great individually "neutralized each other and were irrational" collectively (the parade/tiptoe analogy); Burlington spent $3bn and shareholders lost two-thirds of purchasing power. "A horse that can count to ten is a remarkable horse - not a remarkable mathematician." Returns depend "far more... [on] what business boat you get into than... how effectively you row." "When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."
- The middle ground on closing businesses: won't shut sub-normal operations "merely to add a fraction of a point", but won't fund "unending losses."
- Book value/replacement value is a poor guide: looms bought for $5,000 in 1981 sold at $26 scrap; the auction netted less than zero. "the economic goodwill attributable to two paper routes in Buffalo - or a single Sees candy store - considerably exceeds the proceeds we received from this massive collection of tangible assets."
- Earnings growth must be judged against capital added: three businesses went from $8m to $72m pre-tax with only ~$40m more capital; the average US business needs ~$5 of extra capital per $1 of extra pre-tax earnings. "When returns on capital are ordinary, an earn-more-by-putting-up-more record is no great managerial achievement" (savings account quadruples in 18 years at 8%).
- Stock options critique: fixed-price 10-year options reward mere retention of earnings; they ignore the carrying cost of capital; "Negotiating with ones self seldom produces a barroom brawl"; option holder has no downside ("Ill be happy to accept a lottery ticket as a gift - but Ill never buy one"); options are blind to individual performance ("A managerial Rip Van Winkle"). Fixes: tie rewards to the manager's own bailiwick (.350 hitter on a cellar team); build in a retained-earnings/carrying-cost factor; price options "at true business value." But don't break a working culture ("If it aint broke, dont fix it is preferable to purity at any price").
- Berkshire's system: bonuses tied to unit results, not to BRK stock price; uncapped and non-hierarchical; managers buy stock with their own money and so "truly walk in the shoes of owners."
- Insurance as commodity: profits only when "prices are fixed in some manner or when capacity is short"; in insurance capacity is "an attitudinal concept, not a physical fact" and capital "can be secured instantly," so shortages vanish quickly. The capital-raisers of 1985-86 will be at fault for the next bust.
- Reinsurance: the "lamp of experience" fails in long-tail lines ("Out with Patrick Henry and in with Pogo"); issuing companies writing small net lines kept more of the premium than the risk; reinsurers behave like "Mark Twains cat." Berkshire never played "lay-it-off-at-a-profit", so now has capacity: willing to lose $10m on a single event if price is right and risk uncorrelated.
- "Except for" must be excised from the lexicon; "the real mistake is not the act, but the actor." "the trick is to learn most lessons from the experiences of others."
- Washington Post lesson: bought in 1973 at under one-fourth of a business value everyone could estimate ($400-500m vs $100m market cap) - the edge was "attitude," learned from Graham. Academics' efficient-market theory handed Grahamites opponents "taught that thinking is a waste of energy." The "triple dip": business value up, per-share value up faster via repurchases, discount narrowed.
- Arbitrage (Beatrice) is "a parking place for money" when "we have more money than ideas," only in announced deals.
- Permanent-holding/vote-restriction agreements (Cap Cities, GEICO, WaPo) buy managerial stability: "That kind of certainty, combined with a good manager and a good business, provides excellent soil for a rich financial harvest."
- Patience: "you cant produce a baby in one month by getting nine women pregnant."
- Acquisition approach is "somewhat haphazard": no master strategy, no planners; "we simply hope that something sensible comes along - and, when it does, we act." Minimum raised to $10m after-tax.

**Businesses discussed.** General Foods (sold - four factors); textiles (closed; Burlington contrast); NFM, See's, Buffalo News ("Three Very Good Businesses"; same-store poundage still slipping at See's; ROP linage declining at News); own insurance (combined ratio 134 to 111; large-risk ad drew 600 replies/$50m premium); GEICO (38%; Byrne left for Fireman's Fund; succession by Snyder/Simpson praised); Fireman's Fund quota share (7%, four years; float held by Berkshire); Washington Post (bought at <25% of value; Kay Graham's repurchases); Beatrice (arbitrage); WPPSS ($194m amortized cost, $62m unrealized gain, $30m tax-exempt income); Capital Cities/ABC ("best [management] of any publicly-owned company in the country"; bought at "a full price... its no field for bargains"); Scott & Fetzer ("a prototype - understandable, large, well-managed, a good earner"; World Book sells 2x nearest competitor at a lower price; Ralph Schey's "operating and capital-allocation record is superb").

**Mistakes admitted.** Textiles: "I was very wrong about (4)" (modest cash returns); "I should be faulted for not quitting sooner"; Waumbec acquisition for "synergy (a term widely used in business to explain an acquisition that otherwise makes no sense)"; "I ignored Comtes advice... and believed what I preferred to believe." Loss reserving errors continued ("So far this has not been the case"). Sold Cap Cities at $43 in 1978-80, now buying at $172.50: "A little more time, please." "prior mismanagement by your Chairman" of insurance.

- "Wild swings in market prices far above and below business value do not change the final gains for owners in aggregate; in the end, investor gains must equal business gains." (1985)
- "In selecting common stocks, we devote our attention to attractive purchases, not to the possibility of attractive sales." (1985)
- "Viewed individually, each companys capital investment decision appeared cost-effective and rational; viewed collectively, the decisions neutralized each other and were irrational." (1985)
- "Likewise, a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a remarkable textile company - but not a remarkable business." (1985)
- "My conclusion from my own experiences and from much observation of other businesses is that a good managerial record (measured by economic returns) is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row." (1985)
- "Should you find yourself in a chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching leaks." (1985)
- "When returns on capital are ordinary, an earn-more-by-putting-up-more record is no great managerial achievement. You can get the same result personally while operating from your rocking chair." (1985)
- "No owner has ever escaped the burden of capital costs, whereas a holder of a fixed-price option bears no capital costs at all." (1985)

- "Our advantage, rather, was attitude: we had learned from Ben Graham that the key to successful investing was the purchase of shares in good businesses when market prices were at a large discount from underlying business values." (1985)
- "(We are enormously indebted to those academics: what could be more advantageous in an intellectual contest - whether it be bridge, chess, or stock selection than to have opponents who have been taught that thinking is a waste of energy?)" (1985)
- "capacity is an attitudinal concept, not a physical fact." (1985)

### 1986

**Context.** Net worth +$492.5m (26.1%); 22-year rate 23.3%. Scott Fetzer's first full year (World Book, Kirby, diversified manufacturing); Fechheimer Bros. acquired (84%, $55m valuation) after its chairman read the annual-report "ad." No new equity ideas - capital allocation was "to pay off debt and stockpile funds"; $700m of medium-term tax-exempts bought as "the least objectionable alternative." Insurance combined ratio 103, premium growth fastest among top 100 insurers; but reserving wrong for the third year. Long section on the Tax Reform Act of 1986. A corporate jet was bought. The appendix ("owner earnings") is referenced but only partially available (see caveat).

**Core teachings.**
- Buffett and Munger "really have only two jobs": attract and keep outstanding managers, and allocate capital. On managers: "our main contribution has been to not get in their way" (Nicklaus/Palmer analogy). Managers who "think like owners" is "the highest compliment we can pay a manager." Ogilvy: hire people bigger than you.
- Span of control: with able passionate managers "you can have a dozen or more reporting to you and still have time for an afternoon nap"; one deceitful/inept/uninterested person is "more than you can handle." Work only with people you like and admire - otherwise it is "like marrying for money... absolute madness if you are already rich."
- Capital allocation matters more at Berkshire because it earns more than average, retains everything, and its businesses need little incremental capital; a business earning 23% and retaining it all is far more affected by today's allocations than one earning 10% and paying half out.
- Unrecorded economic goodwill grows when managers raise earnings "while except in the case of insurance utilizing little additional capital."
- Reporting preference: show operating units' earnings "before purchase price adjustments" - "the earnings that would have been reported by the businesses if we had not purchased them." GAAP figures "are not necessarily the most useful ones for investors or managers."
- Acquisitions evaluated by "the economic characteristics of the business its competitive strengths and weaknesses and the quality of the people" - not plant visits (neither he nor Munger had visited Fechheimer; Chuck Huggins had never been to Omaha).
- Ideal seller profile (NFM/Fechheimer): family members who want to keep running and owning, want a buyer who won't resell, management "for as far as the eye can see."
- Insurance math: a combined ratio of 107-112 "typically produces an overall break even result" once float income is counted. Social/judicial inflation means revenues must grow ~10%/yr just to hold profitability even with 2-4% general inflation. Earnings lag prices by 6-12 months; industry will "head south in a hurry" by late 1988/89.
- Commodity pricing: calls for "statesmanlike" pricing are like a Nebraska corn grower asking the world to show statesmanship - "What's needed is not more statesmen but less corn."
- Berkshire's swings in volume come from others' "here today gone tomorrow behavior"; it stays at adequate prices; no layoffs in slowdowns because fear of layoffs makes employees "produce scads of business through thick and thin mostly thin."
- "Reserve strengthening" is "a euphemism for what should more properly be called correction of previous untruths albeit non intentional ones."
- GEICO's moat explicitly named: expense ratio 23.5% vs many competitors 15 points higher - "a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much sought after business castle"; Snyder "continually widens the moat by driving down costs."
- Lou Simpson's results "achieved in the right way": undervalued stocks "individually unlikely to present him with a permanent loss and that collectively were close to risk free."
- Five places for insurance money: long-term stocks, long-term bonds, medium-term bonds, cash equivalents, arbitrage. Fear and greed are "two super contagious diseases" whose timing is unpredictable; "we simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful."
- Mathematical law: "stocks can't outperform businesses indefinitely"; after transaction and management costs stockholders in aggregate must underperform the companies they own; "Bull markets can obscure mathematical laws but they can not repeal them."
- Bonds: aversion to long-term bonds from inflation fear; "the behavior of our currency will be determined by the behavior of our legislators." Medium-term tax-exempts viewed as a cash alternative; best outcome would be selling them at a loss to buy much cheaper equities.
- Permanent holdings doctrine: will keep Cap Cities, GEICO, WaPo "even if these securities were to appear significantly overpriced," just as See's or the News wouldn't be sold. ## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 1983-1988

### 1. What investing IS

- Investing is buying a piece of a business, evaluated exactly as one would evaluate buying the whole business: "we buy marketable stocks for our insurance companies based upon the criteria we would apply in the purchase of an entire business" (1984); "we approach the transaction as if we were buying into a private business. We look at the economic prospects of the business, the people in charge of running it, and the price we must pay. We do not have in mind any time or price for sale" (1987). "We really don't see many fundamental differences between the purchase of a controlled business and the purchase of marketable holdings" (1987).
- Self-definition: "business analysts - not... market analysts, not... macroeconomic analysts, and not even... security analysts" (1987). The approach extends to bonds: a bond is "an unusual sort of business with special advantages and disadvantages" (1984), and the 1946 1%-yield bond was a business earning 1% on book bought at book (1984).
- Graham's maxim: "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike" (1984). The Washington Post purchase required no insight, only "attitude" - buying good businesses at a large discount (1985).
- Time horizon: indefinite. Willing "to hold a stock indefinitely so long as we expect the business to increase in intrinsic value at a satisfactory rate" (1987); "favorite holding period is forever" (1988); the three permanent holdings would not be sold even if "far overpriced" (1986, 1987). A suspension of trading "would not bother us" (1987). "Eventually, our economic fate will be determined by the economic fate of the business we own" (1987).
- Success is measured by operating results, not quotations: "we let our marketable equities tell us by their operating results - not by their daily, or even yearly, price quotations - whether our investments are successful" (1987); prefers business value up X with price down over value up X/2 with price soaring (1984).
- Attention goes to buying, not selling: "we devote our attention to attractive purchases, not to the possibility of attractive sales" (1985). Reported gains in a given year are "meaningless" (1983, 1984, 1985).
- In aggregate, investors cannot out-earn the businesses they own: "stocks can't outperform businesses indefinitely"; after frictional costs they must underperform (1986); turnover is a self-imposed tax (1983).

### 2. What to look for in a business

- The economic signature: high returns on capital with little incremental capital needed. The three businesses (NFM, See's, News) grew from $8m to $72m pre-tax on ~$40m of extra capital, versus ~$5 of capital per $1 of extra pre-tax earnings for the average American business (1985). The Sainted Seven earned ~57% after-tax on historical equity (1987) and ~67% (1988) "with no benefit from financial leverage"; "because so little capital is required to run these businesses, they can grow while concurrently making almost all of their earnings available for deployment in new opportunities" (1987).
- Economic goodwill over tangible assets: strong preference "for businesses that possess large amounts of enduring Goodwill and that utilize a minimum of tangible assets" (1983); economic goodwill compounds in inflation (1985); the textile auction proved tangible assets can be worth less than nothing while "two paper routes in Buffalo - or a single Sees candy store" are worth more (1985).
- Franchise/moat language emerges here: "fortress-like business franchise... usually the key to sustained high returns" (1987); GEICO's cost advantage is "a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much sought after business castle" (1986); strong unregulated franchises keep the benefit of tax cuts (brain surgeon analogy, 1986). Newspaper franchise strength = penetration ratio (1983); dominant papers prosper whether good or bad (1984).
- Pricing power / customer value: See's "preferred... to candy costing two or three times as much" (1983) but Buffett monitors units-per-store, not dollar volume, and moderates price rises when poundage slips (1983-87). NFM wins by low cost: 16.5% operating expense vs 35.6% at Levitz (1984). Cereal companies can raise prices with "not a peep" because of their economics (1988).
- Low-cost production: GEICO's 23.5% expense ratio (1986); NFM/Borsheims' "incredibly low expenses" and rapid turnover (1988).
- Stability and predictability: "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix"; the best businesses do "something quite similar today to what they were doing five or ten years ago"; Fortune's 25 champions are mundane and little-leveraged (1987). Projections and turnarounds are "of little interest" (acquisition ad, every year).
- The "grizzlies" test: how would you compete with it given ample capital and people? (1983).
- Commodity businesses: textiles and insurance - profits only with fixed prices or shortage; capital investment in a commodity industry is collectively self-defeating (1985); insurance has "hundreds of competitors, ease of entry, and a product that cannot be differentiated" (1987). "What business boat you get into" matters more than rowing (1985); the business's reputation, not management's, remains intact (1985).
- Growth that destroys value: Burlington's $3bn of capex left shareholders with one-third the purchasing power (1985); earnings quadrupling by retention alone deserves no applause (1985); ConEd's "Dig We Must" (1984). "Most businesses are unable to significantly improve their average returns on equity" (1984).
- Simplicity and circle of competence: "simple businesses (if theres lots of technology, we wont understand it)" (ad); no insight into investment banking, hence a convertible preferred at Salomon (1987, 1988).

### 3. What to look for in management

- Honesty/integrity first: deals done on a handshake and a word with the Blumkins (1984) and Friedmans (1988) - no audit, no inventory count. Managers must be "high-grade, talented and likeable" (1987); "we try to steer clear of" self-interested managers (1985).
- Owner-orientation: "They unfailingly think like owners the highest compliment we can pay a manager" (1986); permanent-holding managers "love their businesses, they think like owners, and they exude integrity and ability" (1988). Managers who buy Berkshire stock with their own money "truly walk in the shoes of owners" (1985).
- Capital allocation skill is the rare and decisive skill: CEOs rise via marketing/production and "must make capital allocation decisions, a critical job that they may have never tackled" (1987); great core businesses camouflage bad allocation elsewhere (1984). Managers of high-return businesses who sink cash in low-return ventures "should be held to account" (1984). Ralph Schey and Kay Graham praised precisely for allocation (1985).
- Demonstrated behaviour, not rhetoric: repurchases below value demonstrate pro-shareholder leanings - "The key word is demonstrated... His heart is not listening to his mouth" (1984). Compare "our most precious asset" boilerplate (1987).
- Candour and accountability: "except for" must be excised; "the real mistake is not the act, but the actor" (1985). CEOs who paint the bullseye around the arrow; boards that don't measure themselves (1988). Managers who learn from personal experience are "destined to learn much from personal experience in the future" (1985).
- Institutional imperative (not yet named): managers "quickly add to capacity when prospects start to improve" (1985); insurance managers plug the hole "through which money is showering" (1987); CEOs avoid decisions that risk looking like an idiot - "no individual lemming has ever received bad press" (1984); "Failure seems to go to their heads" (1984); restructuring dumps businesses "not... the officers and directors who bought the businesses" (1986).
- Compensation: fixed-price options reward retained earnings and ignore capital cost; price options at business value; reward managers for results in their own bailiwick, uncapped, non-hierarchical, unrelated to Berkshire's stock price (1985). Owner-chosen charity, not director-matched (1987).
- Hands-off management of the right people: "our main contribution has been to not get in their way" (1986); "no corporate meetings, no corporate budgets, and no performance reviews"; contribution is "applause" (1987); okayed a Fechheimer acquisition "without even looking at it" (1988). No flexible budgets or yo-yo cost cutting (1987). No layoffs in insurance downturns (1986). No mandatory retirement (1987, 1988).
- Work only with people you like: "marrying for money... absolute madness if you are already rich" (1986); would take X with admired people over 110% of X otherwise (1987).

### 4. Valuation: price, intrinsic value, margin of safety, discount rates, growth

- Intrinsic business value defined as "future cash output discounted to present value"; book value "tells you what has been put in" (1983). It is "a soft number... two equally well informed observers might make judgments more than 10 [percent] apart" (1986). Book value can be totally unrelated to business value (LTV/Baldwin-United vs Belridge, 1987).
- The bond-as-business framework effectively supplies a discount-rate logic: compare the unleveraged after-tax yield of an asset (WPPSS at 16.3%, 1984; ~17% after tax, 1988) with what the same earnings would cost in a negotiated business purchase ($250-300m vs $139m). Retention test similarly compares incremental returns with "those generally available to investors" (1984); the 10%-perpetual-bond example shows retention is wise when market rates are 5% and foolish when 15% (1984).
- Margin of safety by price: WaPo bought at under one-fourth of intrinsic value (1985); GEICO at $6.67 now earning $9.01 per share (1987). "Mr. Market has proven to be a mighty good friend" (1987).
- Shift from bargain to quality at a fair price: "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price" (1987). Cap Cities bought "at a full price... its no field for bargains" because of people and properties (1985).
- Price caps future returns: at $1.8bn WaPo cannot compound as it did from $100m; "vastly-reduced potential throughout our portfolio" (1985). A new Berkshire owner paying a premium must see it maintained just to match the business (1985).
- Capped upside matters: WPPSS's face value limits gain to 48%, but most businesses also have limited upside without added capital (1984).
- Sell discipline: sell when "the market may judge a business to be more valuable than the underlying facts would indicate," or to fund something cheaper/better understood; never merely because of appreciation or holding period (1987).
- Earnings quality: judge growth against the base year and the capital required (1985); treat accounting goodwill charges and purchase-price adjustments as non-economic (1986 appendix; 1987, 1988 presentation); use owner earnings rather than "cash flow" (1986 appendix, text partially missing).
- Value vs P/E: Rockwood rose from 15 to 100 while losing money - "Sometimes there is more to stock valuation than price-earnings ratios" (1988).
- Berkshire's own price: wants it "rationally related" (1983) / "in a narrow range centered at intrinsic business value," bothered equally by over- and undervaluation (1988).

### 5. Temperament and psychology

- Mr. Market in full (1987): the manic-depressive partner who "doesn't mind being ignored"; "there to serve you, not to guide you"; the poker patsy; the need to "insulate his thoughts and behavior from the super-contagious emotions."
- Fear and greed as "super contagious diseases" with unpredictable timing; "be fearful when others are greedy and... greedy only when others are fearful" (1986). Euphoria prevailed in 1986; the 1987 crash followed.
- Patience and inactivity: "we find doing nothing the most difficult task of all"; "masterly inactivity" (1984); no ideas is better than compromised standards (1984, 1986); arbitrage "cools any temptation... to relax our standards" (1988); "you cant produce a baby in one month" (1985); big ideas "pop up occasionally" (1984).
- Concentration: "harem of forty women" (1984); "very few companies that we try to understand well"; "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful" (1988); arbitrage in a few large deals rather than 50 (1988). Concentration is justified only by financial strength (1984).
- Circle of competence: the Blumkins "define with extraordinary realism their area of special competence and act decisively on all matters within it" and ignore everything outside (1984); investments limited to "alternatives we think we understand" (1987, 1988); GEICO failed outside its core (1984).
- Willingness to look foolish: unconventional, intelligent-but-risky decisions are possible because Buffett/Munger "dont worry about being fired" (1984).
- Independence from the crowd: institutions as destabilizers (1985); Graham's oil-prospector story (1985); portfolio insurance as "Alice-in-Wonderland" (1987); volatility is an opportunity unless one is forced to sell (1987).
- Self-deception: "believed what I preferred to believe" (1985); Franklin's "reasonable creature" on the jet (1986); study mistakes rather than successes (Munger, 1985).
- Prudence inversely to others': "the less the prudence with which others conduct their affairs, the greater the prudence with which we should conduct our own" (1988).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons

- Book value vs intrinsic value (1983, 1984, 1986, 1987, 1988); book value is only a proxy at Berkshire.
- Economic vs accounting goodwill: the 1983 appendix is referenced ("explains why economic and accounting Goodwill can, and usually do, differ enormously") but its text is missing from the file; the letters state that amortization of goodwill is shown separately, not charged to businesses (1983-88), and that economic goodwill grows in inflation when earnings rise without added capital (1985, 1986).
- Purchase-price accounting and owner earnings (1986 appendix, partial): write-ups of inventory/fixed assets/goodwill create non-tax-deductible charges ($11.6m at Scott Fetzer) that change GAAP earnings without changing economics; owner earnings = (a) reported earnings + (b) depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges, less [a (c) term that is truncated in the file]; Wall Street "cash flow" = (a)+(b) without the subtraction, "meaningless" for manufacturing, retailing, extractive companies and utilities; useful only for bridge-like assets; "accountants' job is to record, not to evaluate."
- Look-through earnings (unnamed): retained earnings of investees "will be reflected in market prices and... increase our intrinsic business value on a dollar-for-dollar basis" (1983); minority holdings worth $2bn produced only $11m of reported earnings vs "well over $100 million" of underlying earnings (1987); the GAAP "schizophrenia" of market-value balance sheets but dividend-only income (1987).
- Realized gains in any year are meaningless; the college-graduation analogy (1985).
- Proportionate redemptions as dividends vs sales - the Peat Marwick dispute; form vs substance; "None of this... has any effect on intrinsic business value" (1984, 1985).
- Loss reserving: components of loss expense (paid, case, IBNR, revisions); reserves should be 50-50 to prove slightly high or low; one-year-later restatement tables (1984); "reserve strengthening" is a euphemism (1986); auditors "cannot effectively police" insurers and should say so (1984, 1987).
- Stock options as unaccounted cost and misaligned incentive (1985).
- Segment data vs consolidation: consolidated numbers "virtually ignore[d]" (1983), "never prepare them for internal use" (1985), prepared only "to meet outside requirements" (1988). GAAP is a floor; three questions test (1988). Smoothing, big baths, and outright fraud (1988).
- Deferred taxes on unrealized gains (28%/34%) do not reflect reality given no intention to sell (1988). Tax incidence depends on franchise strength (1986). Timing of tax payments affects value (1986).
- Tangible book/replacement value is a poor valuation guide (textile auction, 1985).
- Formula-type statements given: retention test ($1 market value per $1 retained, 1984); combined ratio arithmetic and 107-112 break-even with float (1986, 1987, 1988); friction tax arithmetic (1983); $5 of capital per $1 of extra pre-tax earnings for average business (1985); CEO deploys >60% of capital after ten years at 10% retention (1987).

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level

- Retained-earnings test: at least $1 of market value per $1 retained, five-year rolling basis (1983, 1984); Berkshire will distribute unrestricted earnings if prospective returns fall below that hurdle (1984).
- Dividend theory: restricted vs unrestricted earnings; retention only if incremental returns at or above those generally available; dividends should be stable and reflect long-term expectations (1984). Owner-vs-option-holder conflict on payout (1985).
- Buybacks: the surest value-creating action when price is far below value; "$2 of present value for $1"; signals management quality; greenmail condemned (1984). Benefited enormously from GEICO, WaPo, General Foods repurchases (1984, 1985).
- Acquisitions: criteria ad every year (min. $5m then $10m after-tax; consistent earning power; good ROE with little debt; management in place; simple; price stated); no unfriendly takeovers; cash preferred; stock only when value received equals value given (1983-88). "Synergy" mocked (1985). Haphazard approach: "we simply hope that something sensible comes along" (1985). Negotiated large minority blocks and convertible preferreds also sought (1985, 1987, 1988).
- Stock issuance: never below intrinsic value; this applies to options and convertibles too (1983). Shares outstanding virtually unchanged since 1965 (1983, 1986).
- No "gin rummy" disposals, but no funding of unending losses - the Adam Smith/Karl Marx middle ground (1983, 1985).
- Debt: little, long-term, fixed-rate (1983); obligations must be "certain," policies set for "extraordinarily adverse conditions" (1987); borrow in anticipation of need because cheap money and cheap assets rarely coincide (1987); S&P AAA and a promise to bondholders (1988).
- Capital allocation is the main driver at a high-ROE, full-retention company (1986); CEOs are frequently unskilled at it and staff/bankers make it worse (1987).
- Size as the enemy of returns: $3.9bn, $5.7bn, $7.2bn, $10.3bn of profits needed for 15% over the next decade (1984-88).

### 8. Insurance and float

- Float explicitly introduced: "the investment income that an insurer earns from holding on to policyholders funds the float"; combined ratios of 107-112 (1986) / 107-111 (1987, 1988) are break-even once float income is counted. Insurance is "the most important challenge" area for allocation and "will almost certainly remain our largest operation" (1987).
- Industry economics: commodity, no cartel any more, capacity is attitudinal and capital is instant (1985); ~10%/yr premium growth needed to hold the combined ratio because of social/judicial inflation (1984-88); earnings lag prices by 6-12 months (1986, 1987); the cycle timetable predicted and confirmed (1985-88).
- Berkshire's edges: financial strength ("only a promise... appraised against the possibility of adversity," 1984; "empty sack," 1987), willingness to write large net lines (1985), "total indifference to volume" and price-based-on-exposure (1987), no layoffs (1986), standby capacity (1987), float/premium ratio 3x typical (1988), personnel (Goldberg; Ajit Jain first mentioned in 1988).
- New lines: structured settlements and loss-reserve assumptions (1983, 1984), Fireman's Fund 7% quota share (1985-89), large risks (1985-86).
- Reserving errors confessed repeatedly (1984, 1985, 1986), improved (1987, 1988); the "walking dead," guaranty-fund cascades (1984).
- GEICO as the model low-cost franchise and moat; Lou Simpson's record (1983-88); Prop 103 as a threat to GEICO's economic goodwill (1988).
- Taxes: the 1986 Act "the most important economic event affecting the insurance industry over the past decade" (1987); reduced tax exemption, dividends-received deduction cuts, discounting of reserves (1986, 1987).

### 9. Mistakes and what changed in his thinking

- Explicit conversion from Graham-style tangible-asset/bargain investing to quality: "My own thinking has changed drastically from 35 years ago when I was taught to favor tangible assets" (1983); "required only 20 years to recognize how important it was to buy good businesses... I searched for 'bargains' - and had the misfortune to find some... short-line farm implement manufacturers, third-place department stores, and New England textile manufacturers" (1987); "making silk purses out of silk" (1987). Book value "as I, in my early years, did myself" was overweighted (1985).
- Textiles: closed in 1985; "I was very wrong"; "I should be faulted for not quitting sooner"; Waumbec "synergy" acquisition; the capex trap in commodity industries; Burlington as cautionary tale (1985). Earlier, the original cornerstone businesses all failed (1984).
- Insurance underwriting and reserving: "serious mistakes" (1983); combined ratios of 121 and 134; three straight years of under-reserving; Pinocchio (1984-86).
- Sold Cap Cities at $43 (1978-80), rebought at $172.50 (1985).
- Mistakes of omission from shunning goodwill businesses (1983). Didn't ask Mrs. B about her relatives (1988). Wrong forecasts on News margins (1987, 1988). Couldn't buy meaningfully in the October 1987 break. Salomon directorship cost money in RJR arbitrage (1988). Jet hypocrisy (1986).
- Changed thinking also on diversification: "Clearly, diversification has served us well" (1984) - meaning diversifying away from bad original businesses into better ones, while still concentrating the portfolio.

### 10. Graham, Munger, Fisher, Keynes and other investors

- Graham: "my friend and teacher" (1987); The Intelligent Investor "by far the best book on investing ever written" and "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike" (1984); learned from him that the key was buying good businesses at large discounts (1985); the oil-prospector-in-heaven story (1985); Mr. Market allegory and "voting machine... weighing machine" (1987); Graham-Newman arbitrage from 1926-56 averaged 20% unleveraged; "Grahamites should probably endow chairs to ensure the perpetual teaching of EMT" (1988); Rockwood cocoa arbitrage while at Graham-Newman (1988).
- David Dodd: "my friend and teacher for 38 years"; principles "simple, sound, useful, and enduring"; "Our prosperity is the fruit of their intellectual tree" (1988).
- Munger: "my partner in general management" (1984); emphasizes "the study of mistakes rather than successes" - "All I want to know is where Im going to die so Ill never go there" (1985); "Okay, at least it will keep you out of bars" on arbitrage (1988); found Harry Bottle for Dempster (1962) and K&W (1987); writes the Wesco letters on thrifts and the S&L crisis (1984, 1988). Fisher is not mentioned in these six letters.
- Keynes: "The difficulty lies not in the new ideas but in escaping from the old ones" (1983); "began as a market-timer... and converted, after much thought, to value investing" (1988).
- Lou Simpson: "the class of the field among insurance investment managers" (1983); "rare combination of temperamental and intellectual characteristics" (1984); beat the S&P every year 1980-86, "in the right way" (1986).
- Peter Lynch: "cutting the flowers and watering the weeds" (1988). Jay Pritzker (Rockwood, RJR), Ivan Boesky (jokingly), Herb Stein, Phil Graham, Kay Graham, Tom Murphy/Dan Burke ("the best of any publicly-owned company"), John Gutfreund, Billy Rose, David Ogilvy, Adam Smith/Karl Marx, Pascal, Comte, Samuel Johnson, Disraeli, Ben Franklin, Churchill, Woody Allen, Yogi Berra also quoted.

### 11. Macro and market views

- No forecasting: "we have no idea and never have had whether the market is going to go up down or sideways" (1986); "We do not have, never have had, and never will have an opinion about where the stock market, interest rates, or business activity will be a year from now" (1988); cannot forecast interest rates "and... believe that no one else can" (1987).
- Yet persistent inflation pessimism driven by fiscal deficits and the trade deficit: "we are as pessimistic as ever on that front" (1983); "substantial inflation lies ahead... a small, but not insignificant, chance of runaway inflation" (1984); currency depends on legislators (1986); "claim checks" abroad, inflation as the debtor government's "H bomb" (1987); purchasing-power stability "isnt in the cards" (1988); within five years higher taxes or higher inflation or both (1986).
- Consequence: aversion to long-term bonds ("bonds are as sound as a dollar," 1984; "no better than the currency," 1987), except mispriced special situations (WPPSS). An all-bond portfolio has a "wipe out risk" in runaway inflation, while a stock portfolio would also lose heavily (1984). Medium-term tax-exempts as a reluctant cash substitute (1986-88).
- Market conditions: bargains abundant in 1974, "180 degrees" different by 1985; euphoria in 1986; crash in 1987; "less attractive stock market" and "far more richly-priced market for the acquisition of businesses" in 1988. The best thing that could happen would be a market drop allowing a switch from bonds to equities (1986).
- Market structure: hyperactive markets are "pie shrinkers" and an "invisible foot" (1983); institutions and portfolio insurance destabilize prices (1985, 1987); EMT is wrong because the market is frequently, not always, efficient (1988).
- Leverage/takeovers: LBO and takeover excesses in 1988 - "Toto, I have a feeling were not in Kansas any more"; Berkshire steps aside; "event risk" for bondholders (1988). Tight money produces acquisition bargains; finance ahead of need (1987).
- Taxes as a macro force: 1986 Act lowers after-tax share of gains for shareholders (80 to 72 cents per dollar of business-value gain), raises corporate capital-gains rate, hits insurers; General Utilities repeal reduces sell-out values (1986); dividends-received deduction cut again in 1987 (1987).
