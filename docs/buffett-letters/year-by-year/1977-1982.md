## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 1977 (letter dated March 14, 1978)

**Context.** Operating earnings $21.9m ($22.54/share), 19% on beginning equity. Insurance (National Indemnity under Phil Liesche) far exceeded expectations in a hard market created by the 1976 industry rate increases; textiles had "a very poor year"; Illinois National Bank and the 36.5% stake in Blue Chip Stamps performed as expected. Cypress Insurance (California workers' comp) bought for cash at yearend 1977; Kansas Fire and Casualty started. Insurance premium volume had grown from $22m (1967) to $151m with no new Berkshire shares issued. Capital Cities Communications bought ($10.9m) during 1977.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Record earnings per share are not noteworthy; because equity compounds, "even a totally dormant savings account will produce steadily rising interest earnings each year." The right measure of managerial economic performance is return on equity capital (except for firms with unusual debt/equity ratios or assets carried at unrealistic values).
- He dissects his own result: EPS up 37% but beginning capital up 24%, "making the gain in earnings per share considerably less impressive than it might appear at first glance."
- Operating earnings exclude capital gains realised directly by Berkshire/insurers; but over the long term, aggregate capital gains "obviously is of significance." Single-year figures should not get too much attention.
- "The importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds": insurance allows mistakes yet a satisfactory overall result; textiles are the opposite, where "even very good management probably can average only modest results."
- Insurance is a commodity: "Insurance companies offer standardized policies which can be copied by anyone. Their only products are promises." No advantages from trademarks, patents, location, etc. Consequence: the business "magnifies the effect which individual managers have on company performance."
- Underwriting discipline: as rates become inadequate "we again will face the challenge of philosophically accepting reduced volume. Unusual managerial discipline will be required, as it runs counter to normal institutional behavior to let the other fellow take away business - even at foolish prices." (An early statement of what he later calls the institutional imperative.)
- Insurance costs rise about 1% per month from monetary inflation and "social inflation" (broadening definitions by juries of what is covered); unless rates rise comparably, margins shrink. One-year policies mean pricing mistakes can only be corrected at renewal, so effects lag.
- Reinsurance "generates unusually high funds for investment as a percentage of premium volume" (float, though the word is not used).
- Stocks are evaluated like whole businesses. The four criteria: "(1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive price."
- No attempt to buy for short-term price behaviour; lower prices on stocks owned are welcomed as a chance to buy more.
- Market-value snapshots and single-year earnings are poor scorecards; "the scorecard on our investment decisions will be provided by business results over that period, and not by prices on any given day." Equally wrong to be "mesmerized by prospective near term earnings or recent trends in earnings" when buying stocks.
- The Berkshire textile digression: in 1948 the combined mills earned $18m after tax, more than Safeway or 3M; a decade after the 1955 merger they had lost $10m aggregate on $595m of sales. "So much for single year snapshots as adequate portrayals of a business."
- Pieces of outstanding businesses in the stock market sell at "very large discounts" from negotiated whole-company prices; so bargains unavailable via acquisition can be had via stocks. He takes "very large positions" without seeking control, expecting business results to translate into market value "for owners, minority as well as majority."
- Non-control can beat control: with Capital Cities, direct ownership would cost twice as much and offer no advantage because he could not manage as well as existing management. "In effect, we can obtain a better management result through non-control than control."
- Reported operating earnings understate the economics of stock holdings: $10.9m in Capital Cities earned ~$1.3m attributable but only $40,000 of dividends appears in operating earnings.
- Low-cost operation and cost discipline as managerial virtues (Illinois National Bank earns ~3x the industry rate on assets while paying savers maximum rates and holding a low-risk, liquid asset position; no new capital since 1931 and $20m of dividends since 1969).
- See's: pre-tax operating earnings grew from $4.2m to $12.6m since 1972 "with little additional capital investment" in an industry with "practically no unit growth" - the archetype of the low-capital, high-return business.

**Businesses/investments discussed.**
- National Indemnity (Phil Liesche, Roland Miller, Bill Lyons): extraordinary underwriting in auto/general liability as competitors withdrew after 1974-75; volume plus margin plus "large sums have been made available for investment."
- Reinsurance (George Young): combined ratio 107.1, unsatisfactory but trending down; high investable funds per premium dollar.
- Home and Auto (John Seward): rescued from near extinction; now sound and growing.
- Homestate companies (John Ringwalt, Cornhusker etc.): combined ratios below 100.
- Cypress Insurance (Milt Thornton): "first-class operation for policyholders, agents, employees and owners alike."
- Textiles: large local employer, cooperative labour, straightforward management, hope of modest profits; but structurally a headwind business. Ken Chace's cash generation after 1965 funded insurance.
- Illinois National Bank (Gene Abegg): earns ~3x most large banks on assets with low risk and high liquidity.
- Blue Chip Stamps, Wesco (Louis Vincenti), See's (Chuck Huggins): See's earnings tripled with little capital.
- Capital Cities: "extraordinary properties and extraordinary management," skills "extend equally to operations and employment of corporate capital"; cheaper via stock than direct ownership.
- Holdings table: Washington Post, GEICO (convertible preferred and common), Interpublic, Knight-Ridder, Ogilvy & Mather, Kaiser Aluminum, Kaiser Industries.

**Mistakes admitted.** "We have mistakenly predicted better results [in textiles] in each of the last two years. This may say something about our forecasting abilities, the nature of the textile industry, or both." Insurance mistakes: the 1969 surety operation, Home and Auto's 1973 Miami expansion, an aviation fronting arrangement, California workers' comp. Lesson: some mistakes are survivable in a tailwind business; "One of the lessons your management has learned - and, unfortunately, sometimes re-learned - is the importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds."

**Verbatim quotes (1977).**
1. "Most companies define record earnings as a new high in earnings per share. Since businesses customarily add from year to year to their equity base, we find nothing particularly noteworthy in a management performance combining, say, a 10% increase in equity capital and a 5% increase in earnings per share. After all, even a totally dormant savings account will produce steadily rising interest earnings each year because of compounding." (1977)
2. "we believe a more appropriate measure of managerial economic performance to be return on equity capital." (1977)
3. "One of the lessons your management has learned - and, unfortunately, sometimes re-learned - is the importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds." (1977)
4. "Unusual managerial discipline will be required, as it runs counter to normal institutional behavior to let the other fellow take away business - even at foolish prices." (1977)
5. "Insurance companies offer standardized policies which can be copied by anyone. Their only products are promises." (1977)
6. "Most of our large stock positions are going to be held for many years and the scorecard on our investment decisions will be provided by business results over that period, and not by prices on any given day." (1977)
7. "We select our marketable equity securities in much the same way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety. We want the business to be (1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive price." (1977)
8. "if their business experience continues to satisfy us, we welcome lower market prices of stocks we own as an opportunity to acquire even more of a good thing at a better price." (1977)
9. "Our experience has been that pro-rata portions of truly outstanding businesses sometimes sell in the securities markets at very large discounts from the prices they would command in negotiated transactions involving entire companies." (1977)
10. "In effect, we can obtain a better management result through non-control than control. This is an unorthodox view, but one we believe to be sound." (1977)
11. "Since Sees was purchased by Blue Chip Stamps at the beginning of 1972, pre-tax operating earnings have grown from $4.2 million to $12.6 million with little additional capital investment. Sees achieved this record while operating in an industry experiencing practically no unit growth." (1977)

### 1978 (letter dated March 26, 1979)

**Context.** Diversified Retailing merged into Berkshire on December 30, 1978, lifting the Blue Chip stake to ~58% and forcing full consolidation (and restatement of 1977). Operating earnings 19.4% of beginning equity, near the 1972 record; per-share net worth had roughly doubled in three years (~25% compounded) thanks to insurance's "bonanza period" and capital gains. Phil Liesche's segment earned ~$11m underwriting profit on ~$90m premiums. Buffett warns the insurance cycle "has turned downward in 1979." Equities at insurers: cost $129.1m, market $216.5m; SAFECO added. Associated Retail Stores (Ben Rosner) acquired via the Diversified merger. Bank must be divested by end-1980.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Consolidated statements lumping businesses with "dramatically different economic characteristics" obscure reality; he reports segment by segment "from the same perspective that we view them managerially." Consolidation "is of no value to us in any management activities."
- Single-year performance should exclude capital gains, but they are "an important component of the longer term record."
- No forecasting of markets: "successfully forecasting short term stock price movements is something we think neither we nor anyone else can do." Yet long-run optimism about specific holdings because their business value exceeds cost.
- Textile economics as textbook: low capital turnover (high receivables/inventory) times low margins = inadequate returns; "producers of relatively undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses must earn inadequate returns except under conditions of tight supply or real shortage." With excess capacity, "prices tend to reflect direct operating costs rather than capital employed." Competitors pursue the same improvements, so no advantage sticks.
- Self-delusion in reserving (especially long-tail reinsurance) leads to inadequate industry rates and hurts even the disciplined: "If major factors in the market dont know their true costs, the competitive fall-out hits all."
- Margins stay steady only if rates rise as fast as loss costs; in Jan 1979 rates were up 3% vs costs up 9% - a mechanical forecast of deterioration.
- Everyone expects to outperform the industry: "someone is going to be disappointed."
- Candour about his own expansion efforts: "Some of our expansion efforts - largely initiated by your Chairman have been lackluster, others have been expensive failures." Easier to buy a good insurance business than create one.
- The four purchase criteria restated; criteria 1-3 can usually be met for a few companies, "but (4) often prevents action." In 1971 insurers held just $10.7m of stocks because prices were full.
- Pension-fund behaviour as a contrary indicator: 122% of net funds into equities in 1971 at full prices; 21% in 1974 after the crash; record-low 9% in 1978 when bargains abounded.
- 1975-78: $112m of realised and unrealised equity gains while the Dow fell from 852 to 805 - "a marvelous period for the value-oriented equity buyer." Stock-picking does not require a rising market.
- Either corporate acquirers are wrong to pay negotiated prices or Berkshire will make "considerable sums of money buying small portions of such businesses at the greatly discounted valuations prevailing in the stock market."
- Prefers that bargains not be revalued quickly, since Berkshire is a net buyer most years; "consistent attractive purchasing is likely to prove to be of more eventual benefit to us than any selling opportunities."
- Concentration: "Our policy is to concentrate holdings. We try to avoid buying a little of this or that when we are only lukewarm about the business or its price."
- SAFECO case: "the best run large property and casualty insurance company" bought "substantially under book value" - less than 100 cents on the dollar for the best, while mediocre companies fetch more than 100 cents in takeovers, and a start-up can never be had below 100 cents.
- Passive ownership of excellent management is no loss: "if one controlled a company run as well as SAFECO, the proper policy also would be to sit back and let management do its job."
- Retained earnings of investees are "just as real" as dividends; those of well-run companies with good reinvestment opportunities may be worth "greater than 100 cents on the dollar." The retention test cuts both ways: in low-capital-need industries or where management has plowed capital into low-return projects, "earnings should be paid out or used to repurchase shares - often by far the most attractive option for capital utilization."
- Cost culture: "the manager of an already high-cost operation frequently is uncommonly resourceful in finding new ways to add to overhead, while the manager of a tightly-run operation usually continues to find additional methods to curtail costs."
- Owner-like managers (Rosner, Abegg, Vincenti, all 73-81) who "instinctively and unerringly think like owners."

**Businesses/investments discussed.**
- National Indemnity / Phil Liesche: ~$11m underwriting profit on ~$90m; credit also to founder Jack Ringwalt's "operating philosophy."
- Cypress (Milt Thornton): outstanding first year; workers' comp dangerous when inflation meets "changing social concepts."
- Frank DeNardo hired to fix National Indemnity's California workers' comp ("a disaster"); volume cut to 25%.
- Reinsurance (George Young): large investable funds, underwriting still inadequate; willing to shrink volume.
- Homestate: disappointing; Kansas Fire (Floyd Taylor) a bright spot.
- Textiles: $1.3m on $17m capital; structurally poor returns; kept for employment/community, management candour, labour cooperation, modest cash returns.
- SAFECO: superb underwriting, conservative reserving, sensible investment policy; bought below book.
- Illinois National Bank: 2.1% on assets, ~3x major banks, lower risk; to be spun off.
- Associated Retail Stores (Ben Rosner, Leo Simon): no growth, adverse demographics, yet 20%+ after-tax returns on capital through merchandising, real estate and cost control.
- Holdings: ABC, GEICO common and convertible preferred, Interpublic, Kaiser Aluminum, Knight-Ridder, SAFECO, Washington Post.

**Mistakes admitted.** Insurance expansion efforts "lackluster" or "expensive failures," largely initiated by the Chairman; the original 1967 purchase remains by far the best part. Lesson: "it is easier to buy one than create one." Homestate underwriting disappointing; National Indemnity's California comp a "disaster."

**Verbatim quotes (1978).**
1. "Such a grouping of Balance Sheet and Earnings items - some wholly owned, some partly owned - tends to obscure economic reality more than illuminate it." (1978)
2. "We make no attempt to predict how security markets will behave; successfully forecasting short term stock price movements is something we think neither we nor anyone else can do." (1978)
3. "The textile industry illustrates in textbook style how producers of relatively undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses must earn inadequate returns except under conditions of tight supply or real shortage. As long as excess productive capacity exists, prices tend to reflect direct operating costs rather than capital employed." (1978)
4. "We hope we dont get into too many more businesses with such tough economic characteristics." (1978)
5. "It is not easy to buy a good insurance business, but our experience has been that it is easier to buy one than create one." (1978)
6. "We get excited enough to commit a big percentage of insurance company net worth to equities only when we find (1) businesses we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) priced very attractively. We usually can identify a small number of potential investments meeting requirements (1), (2) and (3), but (4) often prevents action." (1978)
7. "We continue to find for our insurance portfolios small portions of really outstanding businesses that are available, through the auction pricing mechanism of security markets, at prices dramatically cheaper than the valuations inferior businesses command on negotiated sales." (1978)
8. "We are not concerned with whether the market quickly revalues upward securities that we believe are selling at bargain prices. In fact, we prefer just the opposite since, in most years, we expect to have funds available to be a net buyer of securities." (1978)
9. "Our policy is to concentrate holdings. We try to avoid buying a little of this or that when we are only lukewarm about the business or its price. When we are convinced as to attractiveness, we believe in buying worthwhile amounts." (1978)
10. "We paid less than 100 cents on the dollar for the best company in the business, when far more than 100 cents on the dollar is being paid for mediocre companies in corporate transactions. And there is no way to start a new operation - with necessarily uncertain prospects - at less than 100 cents on the dollar." (1978)
11. "(This proposition cuts the other way, of course, in industries with low capital requirements, or if management has a record of plowing capital into projects of low profitability; then earnings should be paid out or used to repurchase shares - often by far the most attractive option for capital utilization.)" (1978)
12. "It is a real pleasure to work with managers who enjoy coming to work each morning and, once there, instinctively and unerringly think like owners." (1978)

### 1979 (letter dated March 3, 1980)

**Context.** New accounting rule: insurers' equities carried at market, boosting stated net worth. Operating earnings 18.6% of beginning equity (securities at cost), down from 19.4%; EPS up ~20% but he calls that "an improper figure upon which to focus." Book value since 1964: $19.46 to $335.85, 20.5% compounded, with low leverage and no significant share issuance. Industry combined ratio rose from 97.4 to 100.7 as predicted; Berkshire's improved to 97.1. Waumbec Mills admitted as a mistake. Long bond losses discussed at length. Bank sale forced by Fed stance on spin-off. NASDAQ quotation begun. Equities at insurers: cost $185m, market $337m; GEICO common 5.7m shares now the largest holding.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Measure single-year performance as operating earnings / beginning equity with securities at cost; measuring against market-value equity distorts (bad markets flatter, good investing penalises).
- EPS growth is a trap: "even a stopped clock can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low."
- "The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share."
- Long-term performance should include all capital gains, realised and unrealised, at market; their short-run impact is "capricious" but over years they are "fully as important" as operating earnings.
- 1964 book value "somewhat overstated the intrinsic value" since assets were not worth 100 cents on the dollar (an early explicit book-value vs intrinsic-value distinction).
- Inflation plus taxes = the "investors misery index"; when it exceeds the business's ROE, the investor's real capital shrinks even if he consumes nothing. At 14% inflation a 20% compounder yields roughly zero after-tax purchasing-power gain. "We have no corporate solution to this problem; high inflation rates will not help us earn higher rates of return on equity."
- The gold comparison: 1964 book value bought half an ounce of gold; so does 1979 book value after fifteen years of plowing back everything.
- Easy businesses beat hard ones even at fancy prices: a network TV station earns extraordinary returns on tangible capital "virtually impossible to avoid" and sells at "one thousand cents or more on the dollar"; "Despite a fancy price tag, the easy business may be the better route to go." Corollary on the bank sale: "You simply cant buy high quality businesses at the sort of price/earnings multiple likely to prevail on our bank sale."
- Waumbec lesson: "turnarounds seldom turn," and "the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price." Statistical bargains (bought below working capital, machinery "for less than nothing") still fail if the economics are bad.
- Underwriting discipline in practice: Phil Liesche shrinks volume when prices are wrong; Berkshire does not lay off staff because of voluntary volume swings - "We would rather have some slack in the organization from time to time than keep everyone terribly busy writing business on which we are going to lose money."
- Reinsurance's long tail lets "catastrophic current loss experience ... fester undetected for many years"; capital influx will produce disasters; Berkshire's reinsurance volume may decline.
- High interest rates will create "a new threshold of tolerance for underwriting losses" (cash-flow underwriting), so combined ratios will average higher than in the past; forecast of ~105 industry average over five years. Serendipity (fewer accidents from higher gas prices) "wont last indefinitely."
- Insurance "tends to magnify, to an unusual degree, human managerial talent - or the lack of it."
- Retained earnings of investees: "We have faith that the managements of these companies ... will translate a dollar retained by them into a dollar or more of subsequent market value for us." No portfolio changes to suit the market of a single year, even though he expects underperformance in 1980.
- Bonds: amortised-cost accounting "may have contributed in a major way to the losses" by hiding them. Insurers moved to six-month policies because they could not price a year ahead, then "sold the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years." The long bond is "the last major fixed price contract of extended duration still regularly initiated in an inflation-ridden world."
- His own bond mistake: never bought 30-40 year bonds, but bought 15-year bonds and, worse, did not sell them when views crystallised. "You do not adequately protect yourself by being half awake while others are sleeping." Convertibles preferred because conversion rights give a shorter effective life. Policy: "Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be."
- Acknowledges the analysis may be too negative: "Inflation is man-made; perhaps it can be man-mastered."
- Shareholder communication: owners deserve to hear directly from the CEO with "the general scope, balance, and level of candor" he expects from his own managers; no narrative in quarterly reports because nothing meaningful changes quarterly for long-horizon owners.
- Companies get the shareholders they deserve: short-term focus attracts short-term holders; cynicism is returned. Phil Fisher's restaurant analogy; a company "cant be all things to all men"; seeking high trading activity means wanting existing owners to leave.
- Organisation: "centralization of financial decisions at the top ... and rather extreme delegation of operating authority"; headquarters fits in 1,500 square feet; this costs an occasional mistake but eliminates cost layers, speeds decisions, and attracts managers who "simply cant be hired in the normal course of events."

**Businesses/investments discussed.**
- Associated Retail (Ben Rosner): large earnings relative to capital, "realized in cash and not in increased receivables and inventories," in a no-growth segment.
- Textiles/Waumbec: cash at a low rate on capital; the industry, not the managers, is the problem; Waumbec operations partially useful for the decorator line ("our strongest franchise").
- National Indemnity (Liesche): $8.4m underwriting profit on ~$82m, volume deliberately down.
- Home and Auto (Seward), general liability expansion with cautious managers.
- Reinsurance (Young): tough and getting tougher.
- Homestate: Cornhusker poor; data-processing reorganisation mistakes admitted.
- Workers' comp: Cypress "an absolute gem"; Thornton sticks to business he "understands and wants, without giving consideration to the impact on volume." DeNardo fixed LA.
- Surety reinsurance started under Chet Noble, seeking long-term partner clients.
- SAFECO and GEICO as "two truly outstanding management groups."
- Bank (Abegg, Jeffrey): 2.3% on assets, over three times the average major bank.
- Holdings: Affiliated Publications, Amerada Hess, ABC, GEICO, General Foods, Handy & Harman, Interpublic, Kaiser Aluminum, Media General, Ogilvy & Mather, SAFECO, Washington Post, Woolworth.

**Mistakes admitted.** (1) Waumbec Mills: a statistical bargain that was "a mistake"; "our original rationale did not prove out." (2) Fifteen-year bonds bought and not sold at a loss when views changed; "our mistakes should be viewed less charitably than the mistakes of those who went about their business unmindful of the developing problems." (3) Homestate data-processing reorganisation. (4) Textile lesson generalised to bonds: futile "trying to be very clever ... in an area where the tide was running heavily against us."

**Verbatim quotes (1979).**
1. "Thus, even a stopped clock can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low." (1979)
2. "The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share." (1979)
3. "It probably also is fair to say that the quoted book value in 1964 somewhat overstated the intrinsic value of the enterprise, since the assets owned at that time on either a going concern basis or a liquidating value basis were not worth 100 cents on the dollar." (1979)
4. "That combination - the inflation rate plus the percentage of capital that must be paid by the owner to transfer into his own pocket the annual earnings achieved by the business (i.e., ordinary income tax on dividends and capital gains tax on retained earnings) - can be thought of as an investors misery index. When this index exceeds the rate of return earned on equity by the business, the investors purchasing power (real capital) shrinks even though he consumes nothing at all." (1979)
5. "In some businesses - a network TV station, for example - it is virtually impossible to avoid earning extraordinary returns on tangible capital employed in the business. And assets in such businesses sell at equally extraordinary prices, one thousand cents or more on the dollar, a valuation reflecting the splendid, almost unavoidable, economic results obtainable. Despite a fancy price tag, the easy business may be the better route to go." (1979)
6. "Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that turnarounds seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price." (1979)
7. "We hear a great many insurance managers talk about being willing to reduce volume in order to underwrite profitably, but we find that very few actually do so. Phil Liesche is an exception: if business makes sense, he writes it; if it doesnt, he rejects it." (1979)
8. "Nevertheless, we believe that insurance can be a very good business. It tends to magnify, to an unusual degree, human managerial talent - or the lack of it." (1979)
9. "You do not adequately protect yourself by being half awake while others are sleeping. It was a mistake to buy fifteen-year bonds, and yet we did; we made an even more serious mistake in not selling them (at losses, if necessary) when our present views began to crystallize." (1979)
10. "Overall, we opt for Polonius (slightly restated): Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be." (1979)
11. "In large part, companies obtain the shareholder constituency that they seek and deserve. If they focus their thinking and communications on short-term results or short-term stock market consequences they will, in large part, attract shareholders who focus on the same factors." (1979)
12. "Your company is run on the principle of centralization of financial decisions at the top (the very top, it might be added), and rather extreme delegation of operating authority to a number of key managers at the individual company or business unit level." (1979)

### 1980 (letter dated February 27, 1981)

**Context.** Operating earnings $41.9m, 17.8% on beginning equity (down from 18.6%). Book value $400.80, 20.5% compounded over 16 years. The big theme is "non-controlled ownership earnings": retained earnings of investees now exceed Berkshire's total reported operating earnings. GEICO stake raised to 7.2m shares (~33%) at total cost $47m. Illinois National Bank divested December 31, 1980 by a proportional share exchange. Waumbec closed; New Bedford looms cut by a third. $60m of 12.75% notes due 2005 sold in August. Industry combined ratio rose to ~103.5; insurers' bond losses analysed. Gene Abegg died.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Three accounting categories for ownership (>50% consolidated; 20-50% equity method one-line; <20% dividends only). Because Berkshire's assets concentrate in the third category, "conventional accounting only allows less than half of our earnings iceberg to appear above the surface." "our economic well-being is determined by their earnings, not their dividends."
- The value of retained earnings is determined "by the use to which they are put and the subsequent level of earnings produced by that usage," not by ownership percentage or by whether they appear in reported earnings: "If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we dont record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree."
- He has owned 100% of businesses whose reported earnings were not worth 100 cents on the dollar (reinvestment was compelled just to avoid asset deterioration, at sub-market returns), and small fractions of businesses whose retained earnings were worth far more than 100 cents. He prefers earnings without accounting credit used well by a 10%-owned company over earnings with credit "put into projects of more dubious potential by another management - even if we are that management."
- Buybacks: when "a fine business is selling in the market place for far less than intrinsic value, what more certain or more profitable utilization of capital can there be than significant enlargement of the interests of all owners at that bargain price?" Auction markets let companies buy their own earning power at under 50% of negotiated-acquisition cost.
- Translation of retained earnings into market value is "highly uneven," "unpredictable as to timing"; "a silly purchase price for a block of stock in a corporation can negate the effects of a decade of earnings retention"; but at sensible prices recognition "almost certainly will occur," sometimes with "frosting on the cake." Confidence quantified: swapping equities for tax-free bonds would raise reported operating earnings by over $30m; "Such a shift tempts us not at all."
- Hamburger test for real returns: forgo ten hamburgers, get back ten after tax = no real income "no matter how much it appreciated in dollars. You may feel richer, but you wont eat richer." Inflation as a tax on capital that raises the hurdle ROE; at 12% inflation a 20% ROE paid out to a 50% bracket owner loses purchasing power. Explicit taxes alone can never make a positive corporate return negative for owners; the inflation tax can, because it "is not limited by reported income." Most individual owners in medium/high brackets "should expect no real long-term return from the average American corporation."
- Indexing definition: capital is truly indexed only if ROE rises so earnings rise with the price level "without any need for the business to add to capital - including working capital - employed. (Increased earnings produced by increased investment dont count.)" Very few businesses qualify, "And Berkshire Hathaway isnt one of them." Retention-driven EPS growth is not indexing (the thrifty wage-earner analogy).
- Look-through economics by industry: 3% of Kaiser Aluminum and 1.25% of Alcoa give Berkshire ~$13m of share of earnings, a larger economic interest in aluminum than in most controlled businesses.
- GEICO as "the best of all investment worlds - the coupling of a very important and very hard to duplicate business advantage with an extraordinary management whose skills in operations are matched by skills in capital allocation." $47m cost, ~$20m of earning power; equivalent negotiated purchase would cost $200m+. Control confers options (leverage, change managers, direct cash flow, sell) but Berkshire is satisfied buying minority portions of well-run businesses "at prices far below our share of the total value of the entire business."
- GEICO buybacks reduced share equivalents from 34.2m to 21.6m in two years "in a business that simply cant be replicated."
- Turnaround rule: "when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."
- GEICO and American Express (1964) distinguished from true turnarounds: "extraordinary business franchises with a localized excisable cancer (needing, to be sure, a skilled surgeon)." GEICO's advantage: "designed to be the low-cost operation in an enormous marketplace (auto insurance) populated largely by companies whose marketing structures restricted adaptation."

### 1981 (letter dated February 26, 1982)

**Context.** Operating earnings $39.7m, 15.2% of beginning equity (from 17.8%). Net worth up $124m (31%), over half from GEICO's market gain; book value $526.02, 21.1% compounded over 17 years. Industry combined ratio 105.7 and worsening; Berkshire sacrificed volume to keep underwriting superiority. A major acquisition was nearly made but the price "would have left our owners worse off." Shareholder-designated charitable contributions program launched (Munger's idea), 95.6% participation. Long bond yields exceeded 16%, prompting the "equity value-added" analysis. Holdings now include GEICO ($199.8m market on $47m cost), General Foods, R. J. Reynolds, Alcoa, Arcata, GATX, Pinkerton's.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Non-controlled ownership earnings will translate into value "just as surely as if subsidiaries we control had earned, retained - and reported - similar earnings," but irregularly: "While market values track business values quite well over long periods, in any given year the relationship can gyrate capriciously." Recognition is low or negative where earnings are used unproductively, more than dollar-for-dollar where returns on augmented capital are high.
- Non-controlled holdings have better economics than controlled ones because "the area of choice has been far wider": small pieces of exceptional businesses are available at reasonable prices; whole ones only rarely and at high prices. Avoid small commitments: "If somethings not worth doing at all, its not worth doing well."
- Acquisitions aim "at maximizing real economic benefits, not at maximizing either managerial domain or reported numbers"; "managements stressing accounting appearance over economic substance usually achieve little of either."
- "we would rather buy 10% of Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X per share. Most corporate managers prefer just the reverse."
- Three unspoken motives for high-premium takeovers: animal spirits; size as the yardstick of measurement and compensation (the Fortune 500 sales-rank point); and the toad-kissing delusion. "investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads"; "Weve observed many kisses but very few miracles."
- Two acquisition records that work: Category 1, businesses adapted to inflation with "(1) an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume, and (2) an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases in business ... with only minor additional investment of capital." Category 2, managerial superstars (Heineman, Singleton, Zaban, Tom Murphy) who can see a prince in a toad - and who have made few deals recently and often found buybacks "the most sensible employment of corporate capital." Self-assessment: he does not qualify for Category 2 and under-executed Category 1: "Our preaching was better than our performance. (We neglected the Noah principle: predicting rain doesnt count, building arks does.)"
- Price discipline: willing to "pay a fairly fancy price for a Category 1 business if we are reasonably confident of what we are getting," but will not pay for what Berkshire brings "for we find that we ordinarily dont bring a lot." Walked away from a deal: "The empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been poorer."
- Where value is found now: "open-market purchase of fractional positions in companies with excellent business franchises and competent, honest managements. We never expect to run these companies, but we do expect to profit from them."
- Three sources of investment error: "(1) the management we have elected to join; (2) the future economics of the business; or (3) the price we have paid." Category (2) is the most common.
- Marketable securities allow faster correction of mistakes than controlled subsidiaries: "Lack of control, in effect, often has turned out to be an economic plus." Single-year ROE now has diminished utility because unrecorded earnings exceed reported ones.
- Inflation: "Like virginity, a stable price level seems capable of maintenance, but not of restoration."
- Equity value-added: decades ago a 10% ROE made a good business because long bonds yielded 5% taxable / 3% tax-exempt, so a dollar reinvested was worth more than a dollar and stocks sold at 150% of book. With long tax-exempts at 14% and business ROE at 14%, "a typical American business is no longer worth one hundred cents on the dollar to owners who are individuals." A perpetual 7% tax-exempt equivalent "might be worth fifty cents on the dollar." Hence: "Unless passive rates fall, companies achieving 14% per year gains in earnings per share while paying no cash dividend are an economic failure for their individual shareholders." Investors cling to "historic p/e ratios" when "yesterdays assumptions can be retained only at great cost."
- The crossbar metaphor: businesses are "jumping somewhat higher" but "the crossbar of passive return has been elevated much faster." If inflation is tempered, passive returns fall and equity values improve.
- Dividend/retention rule: "a company with historic and prospective high returns on equity should retain much or all of its earnings ... Conversely, low returns on corporate equity would suggest a very high dividend payout so that owners could direct capital toward more attractive areas." (Parable of the talents, Matthew 25:14-30.) The bondholder analogy: nobody reinvests 5% coupons into 5% bonds at par when similar bonds sell at 40 cents.
- Inflation inverts this: "the bad business must retain every nickel that it can," because "inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm" consuming receivables, inventory and fixed-asset dollars just to hold unit volume. Beware dividend-reinvestment plans and companies that "sell newly issued shares to Peter in order to pay dividends to Paul." Berkshire retains earnings "for offensive, not defensive or obligatory, reasons" and clears the after-tax passive crossbar "but barely."

Table of premium growth vs combined ratio: double-digit premium growth precedes good results; ~10% annual volume growth needed just to stabilise losses since incurred losses rise ~10%.
- "underwriting experience has been getting worse in spite of good luck, not because of bad luck."
- Insurers with bonds at "nonsensically high prices" must sell policies at "nonsensically low prices"; everyone must price near the most threatened competitor; nobody will let cash flow turn negative; "worship of size over profitability" and fear of lost market share. Rejects the idea of a regular cycle: large underwriting losses "will be the norm." Financial strength as an option: "should fear ever prevail throughout the industry, our financial strength could become an operational asset of immense value."

- Beware yardstick-switching: "Yardsticks seldom are discarded while yielding favorable readings. Undistributed earnings of GEICO, General Foods, Washington Post and R. J. ## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 1977-1982

### 1. What investing IS, according to these letters

- A stock is a fractional ownership of a business and is evaluated exactly as a whole-business acquisition would be: "We select our marketable equity securities in much the same way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety" (1977), restated almost word for word in 1978. The four tests - understandable, favourable long-term prospects, honest and competent management, very attractive price - appear in both years.
- The time horizon is many years. The scorecard is "business results over that period, and not by prices on any given day" (1977). Market-value snapshots at a single date are explicitly discounted (the $74m unrealised gain in 1977 vs the $17m loss in 1974). Quarterly reports carry no narrative because nothing of long-term significance changes quarterly (1979).
- Investing is about buying earning power cheaply, with the central mechanism being retained earnings in well-run businesses that get translated, irregularly but eventually, into market value (1978, 1980, 1981, 1982). "If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us ... we still own part of the tree" (1980). The investor's job is to pick businesses whose economics let "each dollar of retained earnings to be translated eventually into at least a dollar of market value" (1982).
- The stock market's "auction pricing mechanism" (1978) regularly offers pieces of outstanding businesses "at very large discounts" from negotiated whole-company prices (1977, 1978, 1980, 1981). Hence "we would rather buy 10% of Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X" (1981). Minority ownership of a superbly run business is not a handicap; "we can obtain a better management result through non-control than control" (1977, Capital Cities; 1978, SAFECO; 1980, GEICO).
- Investing is not forecasting markets: "successfully forecasting short term stock price movements is something we think neither we nor anyone else can do" (1978); forecasts "tell you a great deal about the forecaster; they tell you nothing about the future" (1980).
- Real investing results are purchasing-power gains after inflation and taxes, not nominal dollars ("You may feel richer, but you wont eat richer," 1980; the "investors misery index," 1979).
- Being a net buyer, he prefers that bargains stay cheap (1978) and will add to holdings when prices fall (1977, 1982).

### 2. What to look for in a business

- Return on equity / return on tangible capital without leverage is the master metric (1977, 1979). The ideal is a business that earns extraordinary returns almost unavoidably, like a network TV station, and it is worth paying "one thousand cents or more on the dollar" for such a thing: "Despite a fancy price tag, the easy business may be the better route to go" (1979).
- Low capital intensity and growth without capital: See's grew pre-tax earnings from $4.2m to $12.6m "with little additional capital investment" in an industry with "practically no unit growth" (1977). Associated Retail earns 20%+ after tax with earnings "realized in cash and not in increased receivables and inventories" (1979, 1980). Preference for "businesses that generate cash, not those that consume it," and suspicion of pretty earnings that never become "no-strings-attached cash" (1980).
- The inflation-proof business, defined formally in 1981: pricing power - the "ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume" - plus the ability to handle large dollar-volume increases "with only minor additional investment of capital." The 1980 "truly indexed" definition is the same idea: ROE must rise with the price level without adding capital.
- The anti-model is the commodity business in a capital-intensive, excess-capacity industry: textiles in 1978 ("prices tend to reflect direct operating costs rather than capital employed") and generalised in 1982 ("persistent over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability"). Differentiation works for candy bars, not sugar (1982). Only a producer with "a cost advantage that is both wide and sustainable" escapes (1982, GEICO).
- Tailwinds vs headwinds: choose industries where mistakes are survivable and even average management does well (insurance 1977), not ones where "even very good management probably can average only modest results" (textiles 1977, 1979).
- Franchise language: "extraordinary business franchises with a localized excisable cancer" (GEICO, American Express, 1980); "companies with excellent business franchises and competent, honest managements" (1981); the decorator line is "our strongest franchise" (1979). GEICO's "very important and very hard to duplicate business advantage" that "simply cant be replicated" (1980).
- Predictability: "demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us, nor are turn-around situations)" and "simple businesses" (1982 acquisition criteria). Pay for the past, not the future: buy "at prices that will make sense, even should the future of the acquired enterprise develop much along the lines of its past" (1981).
- Growth that destroys value: retention-driven EPS growth at flat ROE is a "stopped clock" (1979); a business compounding EPS at 14% with no dividends is "an economic failure" when passive rates are 14% (1981); low-return businesses forced to retain by the inflation "tapeworm" (1981).
- Supply dynamics: profitability in commodity industries is set by "the ratio of supply-tight to supply-ample years," and "nothing fails like success" because recoveries breed over-capacity (1982).

### 3. What to look for in management

- Honesty and candour first: the 1977/1978 criterion "operated by honest and competent people"; Gene Abegg "shot straight 100% of the time," laid negatives "face up on the table" and later volunteered undiscussed items of value (1980). Sellers who stay on reveal their character (1980). The textile managers are praised for being "straightforward in reporting on problems" (1978).
- Owner-orientation: Rosner, Abegg and Vincenti "instinctively and unerringly think like owners" (1978); Liesche and Rosner ran businesses "with every bit of the care and drive that they would have exhibited had they personally owned 100%" and "No rules were necessary" (1982); Thornton and Taylor show a "passionately proprietary attitude" (1982).
- Capital allocation as a separate skill: Capital Cities' skills "extend equally to operations and employment of corporate capital" (1977); GEICO's "skills in operations are matched by skills in capital allocation" (1980); Tom Murphy is "a real managerial twofer" (1981). The superstars (Singleton, Heineman, Zaban, Murphy) have made few recent deals and favour buybacks (1981).
- Discipline against the institutional imperative (the phrase is not yet used): letting "the other fellow take away business - even at foolish prices" "runs counter to normal institutional behavior" (1977); most insurance managers talk about shrinking volume but "very few actually do so" (1979); National Indemnity's discipline "if suspended, probably could not be fully regained" (1980); managers are measured and paid "by the yardstick of size" (1981); "worship of size over profitability" (1981); CEOs' "cravings outpace cash and credit resources (certainly mine always have)" (1982).
- Cost consciousness: the manager of a tight operation keeps cutting costs even when already below competitors (1978, Abegg); Berkshire's headquarters is 1,500 sq ft (1979), five people (1982), "managing the business rather than managing each other."
- Managers who stay in their circle: Liesche and Thornton stick to business they understand "without giving consideration to the impact on volume" (1979); GEICO's Byrne and Snyder achieve "keeping things simple and remembering what you set out to do" (1982).
- Candour in reporting to owners is a duty: owners should hear directly from the CEO, not a PR specialist, with the same "scope, balance, and level of candor" he demands of his managers (1979). "Let Jack Do It" as a creed (1982).
- Beware yardstick-switchers and accounting cosmetics: managers who move the bullseye (1982); "weak accounting" from weak managements (1982); managements "stressing accounting appearance over economic substance usually achieve little of either" (1981).
- Delegation: "rather extreme delegation of operating authority" with financial decisions centralised at the very top (1979); managers who cannot be hired normally join because Berkshire is "almost identical to running their own show" (1979).
- Age is no bar: 73-82 year old managers "regularly achieve more each year" (1978, 1979).

### 4. Valuation: price, intrinsic value, margin of safety, discount rates, growth

- Price is the binding constraint: criteria 1-3 are often satisfied "but (4) often prevents action" (1978). In 1971 and 1972 good businesses were plentiful but prices "looked absurd," so equities were 15% of insurance investments vs 80% in 1982 (1978, 1982).
- Intrinsic value is distinguished from book value as early as 1979 ("the quoted book value in 1964 somewhat overstated the intrinsic value"). The term "intrinsic business value" becomes central in 1982's share-issuance essay: never issue shares unless "we receive as much intrinsic business value as we give"; dilution should be computed in intrinsic value, not EPS.
- Overpaying destroys even great businesses: "a silly purchase price for a block of stock ... can negate the effects of a decade of earnings retention" (1980); "a too-high purchase price for the stock of an excellent company can undo the effects of a subsequent decade of favorable business developments" (1982). Conversely, a cheap price cannot rescue bad economics (Waumbec, 1979).
- Bargain benchmarks used: SAFECO bought "substantially under book value" when mediocre companies fetch over 100 cents in takeovers (1978); GEICO's $20m of earning power for $47m vs $200m+ via negotiated purchase (1980); buybacks at "under 50%" of negotiated acquisition cost (1980); SAFECO's and others' retained earnings worth "greater than 100 cents on the dollar" (1978).
- Discount rates / passive returns as the hurdle (1981): a business is good only if a dollar reinvested is worth more than a dollar, which depends on the passive rate. At 5%/3% bond yields a 10% ROE was a good business and stocks sold at 150% of book; at 16%/14% yields a 14% ROE business "is no longer worth one hundred cents on the dollar to owners who are individuals," and a perpetual 7% tax-exempt equivalent "might be worth fifty cents on the dollar." The "crossbar of passive return" is the hurdle every business must clear, and falling inflation would lower it and raise equity values. This is the earliest version of interest rates acting on all asset values.
- Growth is valuable only at high returns on incremental capital; growth from retention at low ROE is worthless or worse (1979, 1980, 1981). High-ROE businesses should retain; low-ROE should pay out (1981).
- Market recognition of value is uneven and unpredictable in timing but reliable over years: "While market values track business values quite well over long periods, in any given year the relationship can gyrate capriciously" (1981); GEICO's price rose faster than its intrinsic value in 1981-82, which he expects to reverse at times (1982).
- Taxes matter: realisation of look-through gains is "subject to very substantial, but indeterminate, taxation," perhaps 25% as a rough haircut (1980, 1982).
- Pay for what you get, not what you bring: "we will not normally pay a lot in any purchase for what we are supposed to bring to the party" (1981).

### 5. Temperament and psychology

- Mr. Market is foreshadowed: market prices set by "an army of manic-depressive lemmings" (1982); pension managers chasing tops and fleeing bottoms (122% in 1971, 21% in 1974, 9% in 1978) (1978).
- Welcome falling prices in businesses you own (1977, 1982); don't want quick revaluation when you're a net buyer (1978).
- Patience and inactivity: no attempt to attune the portfolio to a specific year's market (1979); Pascal's "unable to stay quietly in one room" (1982); relief rather than envy at others' 1982 acquisitions; "two blank pages" as the year's best achievement (1982).
- Concentration: "Our policy is to concentrate holdings" (1978); avoid small commitments, "If somethings not worth doing at all, its not worth doing well" (1981); 90% of shareholders have Berkshire as their largest holding (1979).
- Circle of competence: "businesses we can understand" (1977, 1978); "simple businesses (if theres lots of technology, we wont understand it)" (1982); the "nostalgia" joke about Washington Post and GEICO being companies known since ages 13 and 20 (1982).
- Discipline against adrenaline: "managerial intellect wilted in competition with managerial adrenaline" (1982); "the corporate pulse never beats faster than when an acquisition is in prospect" (1981); "Dont ask the barber whether you need a haircut" (1982).
- Honesty with oneself: "It is very easy to fool yourself regarding underwriting results" (1978); "Yardsticks seldom are discarded while yielding favorable readings" (1982); "facts do not cease to exist, either because they are unpleasant or because they are ignored" (1981).
- Fear as opportunity: financial strength "could become an operational asset of immense value" "should fear ever prevail throughout the industry" (1981); borrow before you need it because the best opportunities come when credit is "extremely expensive - or even unavailable" (1980).
- Stable, long-term shareholders are cultivated deliberately (98% retention; the Fisher restaurant analogy, 1979).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons

- Return on equity, not EPS, is the measure; EPS rises on a "dormant savings account" (1977, 1979). Measure single-year ROE with securities at cost (to avoid market-value distortion of the denominator) but long-term results at market including all gains (1979, 1980).
- Consolidation obscures economics; segment reporting mirrors how management thinks (1978). Pooling restatement "pretends" merger dates (1978). Two identical securities can be carried at two different values on one balance sheet (1979).
- Look-through / "non-controlled ownership earnings" (the term used in 1980 and 1981; "Non-Reported Ownership Earnings" and "economic earnings" in 1982): the three accounting categories (>50%, 20-50%, <20%); undistributed investee earnings exceeded total reported operating earnings in 1980-82; the "earnings iceberg" (1980); GEICO's $23m undistributed vs $3.5m dividends (1982). GEICO's 33-35% stake is treated as <20% because voting rights were assigned (1980, 1982).
- Retained earnings' value depends on use, not ownership percentage or accounting treatment (1980, 1982); 100%-owned capital-intensive businesses can produce fully-credited retained earnings worth "minor or zero economic value" (1982).
- Insurance bond accounting at amortised cost hides losses, immobilises investment and underwriting decisions, and causes "asset maintenance underwriting" (1979, 1980, 1981). Mark-to-market would have focused attention sooner (1979).
- Insurance earnings are malleable: long-tail lines, past reserving, rapid growth let an insurer "show almost any profit number it wishes" (1982); reserving self-delusion feeds inadequate rates (1978); combined ratio defined (1981, 1982).
- Goodwill/intangibles: amortisation of purchase intangibles for See's, Mutual and Buffalo Evening News appears in "Other" (footnote, 1981, 1982) - noted but not yet argued at length.
- Inflation accounting: reported earnings overstate real earnings; the "tapeworm" consumes receivables, inventory and fixed-asset dollars (1981); the hamburger test (1980); "you pay income taxes on what would be deficits if returns to stockholders were measured in constant dollars" (1980).
- Book value vs intrinsic value: 1964 book overstated intrinsic value (1979); accounting numbers are "the beginning, not the end, of business valuation" (1982).
- EPS "dilution" in mergers is the wrong test; intrinsic-value dilution is what counts; later equity issues must be counted in deal maths (1982).
- Dividend-reinvestment plans and issuing shares "to Peter in order to pay dividends to Paul" disguise an inability to pay real dividends (1981).
- Operating earnings exclude realised gains and one-offs (branch sale, 1980) so that single-year performance is not flattered (1977-1982).

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level

- Retained-earnings test: retain where returns on incremental capital are high; pay out or repurchase where they are low or capital needs are small (1978, 1981, with the parable of the talents). The bondholder analogy (1981). Inflation perversely forces bad businesses to retain (1981).
- Buybacks: "often by far the most attractive option for capital utilization" (1978); the most certain use of capital when a fine business sells far below intrinsic value (1980); GEICO's share count cut from 34.2m to 21.6m (1980); superstar allocators favour them (1981); damage-repair buybacks after stock-financed deals (1982).
- Acquisitions: the toad/princess analysis and the two winning categories (1981); walked away when the price would leave "the citizenry ... poorer" (1981); the 1982 blown deal celebrated; published criteria in 1982 (size, consistent earnings, high ROE with little debt, management in place, simplicity, a price); only friendly deals; cash preferred.
- Issuing stock: the full 1982 essay - issue only for equal intrinsic value; stock issuance is a partial sale of the company; the 120-acre farm; the three legitimate routes; the double whammy. Historically Berkshire issued shares only in the 1978 Diversified merger, and grew insurance 600% with "No additional shares" (1977, 1982).
- Dividends: Berkshire retains "for offensive, not defensive or obligatory, reasons" (1981) and because retention minimises explicit taxes on owners (1980).
- Leverage/debt: results achieved "while utilizing a low amount of leverage" (1979); the $60m 12.75% notes raised ahead of need for "financial firepower" (1980); "plenty of liquidity, with debt that is moderate in size and properly structured" even though ROE is "penalized somewhat" (1980); "Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be" (1979).
- Capital in controlled vs non-controlled businesses: non-controlled holdings have better economics and errors are easier to reverse (1981); yet 100% purchases "really make us dance" (1982).
- Spin-off/divestiture done so all shareholders kept proportional interest in the bank, with Buffett taking less (1980).

### 8. Insurance and float

- Float, without the name: reinsurance "generates unusually high funds for investment as a percentage of premium volume" (1977, 1978); policyholder money is "temporarily on deposit with the insurer" (1980); premium growth drove the rise of insurance investments from $134.6m to $252.8m (1977). Insurers' liquidity needs "mandate major investments in marketable securities" (1981).
- Economics of the industry: a commodity product ("Their only products are promises," 1977), instant capacity creation ("capital plus an underwriters willingness to sign his name," 1982), historically profitable only through regulator-fostered administered pricing (1982); therefore, underwriting magnifies managerial talent (1977, 1979).
- Cycle mechanics: one-year policies delay the earnings effect of rate changes (1977, 1981); written premium sets half of next year's earned premium (1981); ~10% annual volume growth needed just to hold the loss ratio because loss costs rise ~10% (1981, 1982); monetary and "social" inflation (1977, 1981).
- Cash-flow / asset-maintenance underwriting driven by high interest rates and hidden bond losses (1979, 1980, 1981); the cycle may not turn without a "megadisaster" that curtails supply (1982); losses will be "the norm" (1981). Forecast of ~105 combined ratios over five years (1979).
- Reinsurance: ease of entry, cash up front, long tail, "amateur night" (1980); festering losses (1979).
- Underwriting discipline: shrink volume rather than write at a loss, keep staff anyway (1979, 1980); discipline once lost cannot be regained (1980); Berkshire slipped below average in 1982 because its traditional lines were priced to lose.
- GEICO as the low-cost exception with a wide, sustainable cost advantage; 35% stake equals ~$250m of premium, more than Berkshire's own direct volume (1980, 1981, 1982).
- Financial strength as a competitive option when fear arrives (1980, 1981, 1982).

### 9. Mistakes and what changed in his thinking over these years

- Textiles and Waumbec: the clearest evolution from Graham-style statistical bargains to quality. Waumbec was bought below working capital, "got very substantial amounts of machinery and real estate for less than nothing," and "was a mistake"; "turnarounds seldom turn" and talent is better spent on "a good business purchased at a fair price than ... a poor business purchased at a bargain price" (1979). He was slow to close it ("costly mistake in not facing the realities ... sooner," 1980). By 1981 he admits "our kisses fell flat" on toads and that success came from "princes when purchased" or fractional stakes in "easily-identifiable princes at toad-like prices." By 1982, "turn-around situations" are explicitly excluded from acquisition criteria.
- Insurance expansion failures: surety (1969), Miami auto, aviation fronting, California comp (1977); "lackluster" and "expensive failures" initiated by the Chairman (1978); Insurance Company of Iowa closed after losses every year (1980); homestate data-processing mistakes (1979). Lesson: easier to buy a good insurer than build one (1978).
- Long bonds: bought 15-year bonds and failed to sell when views changed; "talking when he should have been acting" (1979, 1980). Lesson: don't be clever in an area where the tide runs against you; convertibles only.
- Forecasting: repeated wrong textile forecasts (1977, mocked again 1981 via Goldwyn); the aluminum opinion reversed "approximately 180 degrees" (1981). Lesson: errors about "the future economics of the business" are the most common kind (1981).
- Acquisition temperament: "Our preaching was better than our performance" on Category 1 businesses (1981); nearly starring in the "Acquisition Follies of 1982." Lesson: stay in the room.
- Yardstick change: abandoning ROE-on-operating-earnings for Berkshire in 1982, with explicit self-suspicion.
- Shift in emphasis across the batch: from single-year ROE (1977-79) to look-through earnings (1980-82); from textiles-as-salvageable (1977-78) to textiles-as-textbook-failure (1979-82); from bargain-price language (1977-78) to franchise, pricing power and "fancy price for a Category 1 business" (1979-81); from the hope that the insurance cycle turns to the view that structural over-capacity makes losses the norm (1979-82). Buffett handed insurance oversight to Mike Goldberg (1982).

### 10. Mentions of Graham, Munger, Fisher, Keynes and other investors

- Phil Fisher (1979): "Phil Fisher, a respected investor and author, once likened the policies of the corporation in attracting shareholders to those of a restaurant attracting potential customers," used to argue that a company gets the shareholders it deserves and should not vacillate "between French cuisine and take-out chicken."
- Charlie Munger: praised with Louie Vincenti for "the superb job ... in repositioning Mutual Savings and Loan" (1980); the shareholder-designated contributions idea "along with many other ideas that have turned out well for us, was conceived by Charlie Munger, Vice Chairman of Berkshire and Chairman of Blue Chip. Irrespective of titles, Charlie and I work as partners in managing all controlled companies" (1981); "Charlie and I are interchangeable in business decisions. Distance impedes us not at all: weve always found a telephone call to be more productive than a half-day committee meeting" (1982).
- Jack Ringwalt (founder of National Indemnity): his "operating philosophy remains etched upon the company" (1978); instilled the underwriting discipline (1979, 1980); a "prime mover" (1982).
- Jack Byrne (GEICO): supplied "managerial brilliance" "in abundance" (1980); "Let Jack Do It" (1982). Lou Simpson: "the best investment manager in the property-casualty business" (1982).
- Managerial superstars named (1981): Ben Heineman (Northwest Industries), Henry Singleton (Teledyne), Erwin Zaban (National Service Industries), Tom Murphy (Capital Cities).
- Barbara Stewart of Chubb (1980): her October 1980 paper on property-casualty competitive dynamics; Chubb's report "the most insightful, candid and well-written."
- Arthur Okun's "St. Offset" (1982); Pascal (1982); Sam Goldwyn (1981); Pogo (1981); Yogi Berra (1982); Polonius (1979); Matthew 25 (1981); Alice in Wonderland (1981).
- Graham and Keynes are not named in these six letters. The Graham influence is implicit in the rejected Waumbec "statistical" bargain and in the 1978 "value-oriented equity buyer" language; the shift towards Fisher/Munger quality thinking is visible in 1979-81 (easy businesses at fancy prices; franchises; pricing power).

### 11. Macro and market views

- Inflation is the dominant macro theme every year. It raises insurance loss costs ~1%/month (1977); it creates an implicit tax on capital that can turn positive corporate returns into negative owner returns (1979, 1980); it forces bad businesses to retain earnings (1981); "Berkshire has no corporate solution to the problem" (1979, 1980, repeated). Government "is unable to print gold or create oil" (1979). "Inflation is man-made; perhaps it can be man-mastered" (1979). Volcker applauded, yet "Like virginity, a stable price level seems capable of maintenance, but not of restoration" (1981).
- Interest rates as the hurdle for all equity values: the 1981 "crossbar of passive return" analysis - when long tax-exempts yield 14%, a 14% ROE business is worth under book, and if inflation and passive rates fall "the intrinsic position of American equity capital should significantly improve." High interest rates also drive cash-flow underwriting (1979, 1980).
- Long-term bonds: "the last major fixed price contract of extended duration still regularly initiated in an inflation-ridden world" (1979); possibly "obsolete instruments"; the dollar may have "too many structural weaknesses to appropriately serve as a unit of long term commercial reference" (1979). Insurers carry bonds at 2-3x net worth at amortised cost; some have negative market-value net worth (1980).
- Market timing rejected: no forecasting of short-term prices (1978, 1980); portfolio not adjusted for the expected market of a single year (1979). But valuation-level awareness is strong: 1972's two-tier mania vs 1982; a high stock market "would hurt our long-term business prospects," and "early traces" were visible (1982).
- Macro indicators used as contrary signals: pension-fund equity allocations (1978); corporate merger premiums vs stock prices (1978, 1981, 1982).
- Leverage: keep debt moderate and liquidity ample; borrow ahead of need because credit vanishes when opportunities are best (1980).
- Forecasting humility: "Forecasts, said Sam Goldwyn, are dangerous, particularly those about the future" (1981); yet mechanical forecasts about insurance (rates vs loss costs; written vs earned premium) are made confidently because they follow from contract terms rather than from guessing (1978, 1979, 1981, 1982).
- Derivatives, EBITDA, stock options, pensions, restructuring charges: not discussed in these six letters.
