## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 1995 letter (dated March 1, 1996)

**Context.** Net worth rose $5.3 billion (45.0%); per-share book value +43.1% because stock was issued for two acquisitions; book value per share has compounded at 23.6% for 31 years ($19 to $14,426). Buffett dismisses the year: "any fool could make a bundle in the stock market." Three acquisitions "of exactly the type we desire": Helzberg's Diamond Shops and R.C. Willey (both for stock, tax-free exchanges) and 100% of GEICO ($2.3 billion for the half not already owned, closed January 2, 1996). These roughly double revenues while headquarters staff grew from 11 to 12. The Cap Cities/ABC-Disney merger was agreed (Berkshire sought Disney stock). A Class B recapitalization was proposed to pre-empt expense-laden "Berkshire clone" unit trusts. Buffett states flatly that at $36,000 Berkshire is not undervalued and "the stock... has outperformed the business."

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- The "double-barrelled approach": negotiated purchase of 100% of a business at a fair price, or purchase of "a modest percentage of an outstanding business at a pro-rata price well below what it would take to buy 100%" via the stock market. Having both options is an advantage over capital allocators who stick to one course.
- Two-column intrinsic-value table introduced (marketable securities per share; pre-tax operating earnings per share excluding all investment income). 1965-95 growth: 33.4% and 14.7% respectively. "These results have not sprung from some master plan."
- Size is "the giant disadvantage": "In the early years, we needed only good ideas, but now we need good big ideas."
- Skepticism about acquisitions generally: "most deals do damage to the shareholders of the acquiring company." Never look at seller projections; sellers know more and pick the time of sale (the limping-horse story). Drucker: "Dealmaking beats working."
- Berkshire's edge in acquiring: no strategic plan (so no "ordained direction... leading almost invariably to silly purchase prices"); every contemplated move is mentally compared against dozens of alternatives, including small pieces of the best businesses via the stock market. Also: Berkshire stock is attractive to sellers who want to defer taxes, and Berkshire buys to keep with autonomy for managers.
- Diversification anecdote: a company got 100% of earnings from its original business; after a decade of consultant-driven acquisitions, 150%.
- Retailing is a "have-to-be-smart-every-day business" (shooting-star phenomenon, competitors copy and top you) versus the "have-to-be-smart-once business" (buy a network TV station early and "a shiftless and backward nephew" could run it). Helzberg's would not have been bought without Jeff Comment to run it.
- Management philosophy: managers operate autonomously; "Our basic goal as an owner is to behave with our managers as we like our owners to behave with us." Number of direct reports is irrelevant if managers are good; "If I have one person reporting to me and he is a lemon, that's one too many."
- GEICO's economics: direct marketing gives "an enormous cost advantage over competitors that sold through agents, a form of distribution so ingrained... that it was impossible for them to give it up." Underwriting and loss-adjustment expenses fell to 23.6% of premiums. "In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats.'" The business "remains exceptional for precisely the same reasons that prevailed in 1951."
- Lesson from selling GEICO in 1952 to buy a stock at ~1x earnings: the stock sold grew to $1.3 million, "a lesson about the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company." Same lesson repeated with Disney (bought 1966 at 31 split-adjusted, sold 1967 at 48; now $66) and Cap Cities (sold one-third for $635 million two years earlier versus $1.27 billion in the Disney deal).
- Float defined and valued: float is money held but not owned; cost of float = underwriting loss; the business is profitable "if its cost of float is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds" and has "negative value" if higher than market rates. Float that costs nothing "has in effect served as equity." Berkshire's float grew at 20.7% compounded since 1967.
- Three determinants of any company's profitability: "(1) what its assets earn; (2) what its liabilities cost; and (3) its utilization of 'leverage.'" Leverage is only an advantage if liabilities are cheap.
- Super-cat: large profits most years, occasional huge loss; prefer "a lumpy 15% to a smooth 12%"; monitor aggregate exposure so the worst case is tolerable; never knowingly write business at inadequate rates (the early-1970s reinsurance mistakes still send bills 20 years later). Interests of those who write business must be aligned, downside and upside, with those providing capital (Lloyd's).
- Reporting: Charlie's dictum "Just tell me the bad news; the good news will take care of itself." Supply the information "we would wish you to give us if our positions were reversed." No investor relations department, no earnings guidance.
- Industry trend candour: shoe business cyclical not secular; newspapers "lost still another notch"; World Book threatened by CD-ROM and on-line.
- Convertible preferreds post-mortem: initially valued for fixed-income characteristics with the conversion as a "kicker"; as a group (ex-Gillette) they returned no more than medium-term fixed-income would have.
- Recapitalization: market price should ideally track intrinsic value so every shareholder benefits in proportion to the business's progress; wants shareholders who are "knowledgeable... share our objectives and long-term perspective, and are aware of our limitations." Goal: "to double Berkshire's per-share intrinsic value every five years, and we may well fall far short."

**Businesses/investments and why.**
- Helzberg's: per-store sales ~$2 million, "far more than competitors," which is "the key to Helzberg's excellent profits"; manager Jeff Comment essential.
- R.C. Willey: >50% of Utah furniture business; Bill Child built it from $250,000 to $257 million.
- GEICO: low-cost direct marketer; Tony Nicely ("brains, energy, integrity and focus") and Lou Simpson (22.8% vs 15.7% S&P 1980-95, "conservative, concentrated approach"); Simpson also provides succession insurance.
- Super-cat/Ajit Jain: unmatched financial strength, fastest quotes, largest limits; competitors who lay off risk lose flexibility. Insured Mike Tyson's life, Lloyd's names' mortality, Chinese satellites.
- Gillette: "a superior business"; First Empire (Bob Wilmers, "outstanding banker"); Champion: no conviction about the paper industry, sold back to company; Salomon: "no special insights" into investment banking, conversion option "very poor" decision; USAir: weak credit, "your guess may be better than mine."
- Disney: "unique assets and outstanding management."
- Tom Murphy: "as fine an executive as I have ever seen"; "If Murph should elect to run another business, don't bother to study its value - just buy the stock."

**Mistakes admitted.** Selling GEICO (1952), Disney (1967), one-third of Cap Cities (1993). Taking Gillette preferred instead of common cost ~$625 million less ~$70 million of extra dividends ("I was far too clever"). Salomon: no insight, conversion option worthless so far. USAir: a poor investment. The claim that "under almost any conditions, we expect these preferreds to return us our money plus dividends" is "one I would like to have back." Early-1970s reinsurance written at inadequate rates: "Berkshire paid a heavy tuition for my education."

**Verbatim quotes (1995).**
- "This was a year in which any fool could make a bundle in the stock market. And we did. To paraphrase President Kennedy, a rising tide lifts all yachts." (1995)
- "Charlie and I haven't lost our skepticism: We believe most deals do damage to the shareholders of the acquiring company." (1995)
- "In the world of mergers and acquisitions, that horse would be peddled as Secretariat." (1995)
- "Buying a retailer without good management is like buying the Eiffel Tower without an elevator." (1995)
- "In retailing, to coast is to fail." (1995)
- "But in the next 20 years, the GEICO stock I sold grew in value to about $1.3 million, which taught me a lesson about the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company." (1995)
- "In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats.'" (1995)
- "Float is money we hold but don't own." (1995)
- "An insurance business is profitable over time if its cost of float is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds. But the business has a negative value if the cost of its float is higher than market rates for money." (1995)
- "In other words, we prefer a lumpy 15% to a smooth 12%. Since most managers opt for smoothness, we are left with a competitive advantage that we try to maximize." (1995)
- "No matter what others may do, we will not knowingly write business at inadequate rates." (1995)
- "A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit." (1995)
- "Berkshire is selling at a price at which Charlie and I would not consider buying it." (1995)
- "The stock, in other words, has outperformed the business. That kind of market overperformance cannot persist indefinitely, neither for Berkshire nor any other stock." (1995)

### 1996 letter (dated February 28, 1997)

**Context.** Net worth +$6.2 billion (36.1%); per-share book value +31.8% ($19,011; 23.8% compounded over 32 years), diluted by the Class B sale ($565 million, May 1996) and the FlightSafety stock/cash acquisition (~$1.5 billion). GEICO became a wholly-owned subsidiary (step-acquisition accounting forced a $478.4 million write-down of the original 51%). Kansas Bankers Surety bought for $75 million. The Owner's Manual was issued and reprinted. Berkshire's stock "underperformed the business" in 1996, which Buffett regards as a more appropriate price/value relationship. Huge super-cat contracts were written with Allstate (Florida hurricanes) and the California Earthquake Authority. Taxes paid: $860 million. Berkshire reports go on the Internet. This letter contains the "Inevitables" and "circle of competence" passages.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Intrinsic value, not book value, is what counts; the two-column table is the guide (investments per share $28,500; operating earnings per share $421.39).
- Costs matter: mutual-fund expenses of ~100 basis points "cut the returns their investors earn by 10% or more over time." Berkshire's after-tax headquarters cost is under two basis points of net worth. "We are here to make money with you, not off you."
- Fairness between shareholders: "Over time, the aggregate gains made by Berkshire shareholders must of necessity match the business gains of the company." When price and value diverge, "the sophisticated have an edge over the innocents." Managers should foster price/value alignment through policies and communication; long holding periods make entry/exit premiums irrelevant.
- Acquisition criteria: "excellent business economics and an outstanding manager." The manager thinks of the business as "his" (Don Towle at KBS). FlightSafety is explicitly flagged as capital intensive, unlike most Berkshire operations.
- Age and managers: "It's difficult to teach a new dog old tricks."
- Float is "a major component of Berkshire's intrinsic value that is not reflected in book value." With underwriting profits "we have been paid for holding money." Float grew 22.3% compounded since 1967 to $6.7 billion.
- Super-cat: "a truly terrible year in the super-cat business is not a possibility - it's a certainty." Don't own Berkshire (or stocks generally) if scary news would make you panic-sell. Robert Woodruff on selling Coke: "I don't know. I've never sold any."
- Volatility is a product Berkshire sells; "we would much rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%," and this works best with shareholder/partners who accept volatility.
- Three super-cat advantages: certainty of payment under adverse conditions (Berkshire never lays off risk partly because of doubts about collecting from others); capacity after a catastrophe when others have none (stand-by fees); unmatched size of coverages.
- Distrust of models: "we believe the precision they project is a chimera... such models can lull decision-makers into a false sense of security." Portfolio insurance in 1987 as example. But imprecision does not prevent sensible underwriting: "you need not know a man's precise age to know that he is old enough to vote." Price so that ~90% of premiums are eventually paid out. "The super-cat business is just like the investment business in that it often takes a long time to find out whether you knew what you were doing."
- Inversion and avoiding ruin: Charlie's "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there." "If we can't tolerate a possible consequence, remote though it may be, we steer clear of planting its seeds. That is why we don't borrow big amounts."
- GEICO's virtuous circle: low costs permit low prices, which attract and retain good policyholders, who refer friends (over 1 million referrals a year, more than half of new business), which lowers acquisition cost further. The goal is "not to widen our profit margin but rather to enlarge the price advantage we offer customers." Economies of scale rise with geographic penetration. Voluntary policies only; assigned-risk growth is "most unwelcome."
- Incentive-compensation principles: goals should be "(1) tailored to the economics of the specific operating business; (2) simple in character so that the degree to which they are being realized can be easily measured; and (3) directly related to the daily activities of plan participants." Shun "lottery ticket" arrangements such as options on Berkshire shares whose value is out of the recipient's control. GEICO bonuses depend on only two variables: growth in voluntary auto policies and underwriting profit on seasoned business. "At Berkshire, we never greet good work by raising the bar."
- Pay each unit for its own results: Lou Simpson is paid on four-year investment performance, not underwriting; "If you bat .350 at Berkshire, you can be sure you will get paid commensurately even if the rest of the team bats .200."
- Look-through earnings defined: operating earnings + Berkshire's share of investees' retained operating earnings - hypothetical 14% tax. Retained earnings at investees are more valuable than dividends because investees "often have the opportunity to reinvest earnings at high rates of return."
- Inactivity: "We continue to make more money when snoring than when active. Inactivity strikes us as intelligent behavior." The art of investing in public companies is "little different from the art of successfully acquiring subsidiaries. In each case you simply want to acquire, at a sensible price, a business with excellent economics and able, honest management. Thereafter, you need only monitor whether these qualities are being preserved."
- Concentration naturally results from this approach; don't trim winners because they dominate (Michael Jordan analogy).
- Preference for businesses "unlikely to experience major change" because the goal is near-certainty of "enormous competitive strength ten or twenty years from now." "A fast-changing industry environment may offer the chance for huge wins, but it precludes the certainty we seek." Change is welcomed as citizens but "we applaud the endeavor but prefer to skip the ride."
- See's: the reasons people buy boxed chocolates, and buy them from See's, are "virtually unchanged from what they were in the 1920s" and unlikely to change for 20 or 50 years.
- "The Inevitables" (Coca-Cola, Gillette): no sensible observer doubts they "will dominate their fields worldwide for an investment lifetime." But "I would rather be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one." Leadership alone is no guarantee (GM, IBM, Sears); "for every Inevitable, there are dozens of Impostors"; Berkshire can't find a "Nifty Fifty or even a Twinkling Twenty," so adds "Highly Probables."
- Price discipline: "You can, of course, pay too much for even the best of businesses," and overpayment risk "may now be quite high for the purchasers of virtually all stocks, The Inevitables included."
- Biggest risk to a great business is management losing focus (Coke once grew shrimp, Gillette explored for oil); "value stagnate[s] in the presence of hubris or of boredom."
- Advice to investors: most should use a low-fee index fund. Those who pick stocks need only "the ability to correctly evaluate selected businesses." Circle of competence: "The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital." You need not understand beta, efficient markets, MPT, option pricing or emerging markets; only two courses matter: "How to Value a Business, and How to Think About Market Prices."
- Investment goal stated: "purchase, at a rational price, a part interest in an easily-understandable business whose earnings are virtually certain to be materially higher five, ten and twenty years from now." Buy "a meaningful amount" when one qualifies. "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes."
- USAir diagnosis: beguiled by a long profitable history and the seniority of the security; missed that revenues faced an unregulated market while costs were "a holdover from the days when regulation protected profits." "If history supplied all of the answers, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." Labor cost cuts usually require credible bankruptcy threats.
- B-share offering design: 1.5% underwriting commission (lowest seen) and open-ended size to repel flippers; aimed at long-term holders. Unit trusts would have created "a speculative bubble in our stock" that was "self-validating" for a time. "In the securities business, whatever can be sold will be sold."
- Exchangeable notes on Salomon shares: ~1.1% interest cost before exchange. Investment-banking fees are fine when "commensurate with performance."

**Businesses/investments and why.**
- GEICO: low-cost operator, virtuous circle, scale economies; "GEICO's sustainable cost advantage is what attracted me to the company way back in 1951."
- FlightSafety: world leader in pilot training; capital intensive (simulators up to $19 million); Al Ueltschi "exactly our kind of manager."
- Kansas Bankers Surety: "extraordinary underwriting record"; owner-minded manager.
- Coca-Cola and Gillette: Inevitables; Goizueta "rethought and improved every aspect of the company" while fundamentals stayed constant; expanding market share.
- See's: unchanged consumer motivation; predictability.
- Super-cat exposures: Florida (Allstate) and CEA (~$1 billion layer above ~$5 billion of losses); worst case ~$600 million after tax, <3% of book value.
- USAir: cost structure problem; penalty-dividend clause was the one thing done right; Stephen Wolf credited.
- Salomon: did "an absolutely first-class job" on the B offering, against its own fee interest.

**Mistakes admitted.** USAir: "my analysis of USAir's business was both superficial and wrong"; twice tried to sell at 50% of face/ $335 million and luckily failed ("If you're so rich, why aren't you smart?"). Reinsurance "disastrous propositions" embraced in the 1970s. GEICO's foolish early-1980s reinsurance contracts noted.

**Verbatim quotes (1996).**

- "Selling fine businesses on 'scary' news is usually a bad decision." (1996)

### 1997 letter

**Context.** Net worth +$8.0 billion; per-share book value +34.1% ($25,488; 24.1% compounded over 33 years), but the S&P rose almost as fast: "Our appraisal of 1997's performance, then: Quack." Berkshire paid/accrued $4.2 billion of federal income tax. Prices "are high for both businesses and stocks" so Buffett invokes Ted Williams' strike-zone discipline. Three unconventional positions disclosed: oil derivative contracts (14 million barrels left of 45.7 million), 111.2 million ounces of silver, and $4.6 billion of long-term zero-coupon Treasuries. Acquisitions: Star Furniture and International Dairy Queen. Roberto Goizueta died. Salomon merged into Travelers; USAir recovered and the preferred was called. The "Confession" that stock-financed mergers have cost shareholders money appears here.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Measure performance against the S&P, net of Berkshire's corporate-tax handicap; expect only "a modest advantage over the Index" over time. Don't be the preening duck after a rainstorm.
- Intrinsic value "far exceeds book value" but moved roughly in tandem in 1997; two-column table (investments $38,043/share equivalent, +33.5%; operating earnings +70.3%, flattered by luck in super-cat and GEICO).
- Scale kills returns: "For anyone deploying capital, nothing recedes like success." In 1951 a $10,000 gain added 100 percentage points to his year; now $500 million adds one point. "We weren't smarter then, just smaller."
- Discipline when prices are high: Ted Williams' 77 cells; swing only at fat pitches; today's pitches "are just catching the lower outside corner" and swinging would lock in low returns. But no guarantee better pitches come: "Perhaps the attractive prices of the past were the aberrations."
- Hierarchy of commitments: favourite is "a well-run and sensibly-priced business with fine economics"; otherwise short-term high-quality instruments; occasionally "alternative commitments" (oil, silver, zeros) where profit is likely but not certain. "Finding that kind of opportunity [a wonderful business at an attractive price], we know that we are going to make money - the only question being when."
- Macro bets "never [have] anything close to a 100% probability"; "you pay Charlie and me to use our best judgment - not to avoid embarrassment." Silver was bought on supply/demand, with inflation expectations playing "no part."
- How to think about market fluctuations: the hamburger quiz. Net savers (including Berkshire shareholders, since Berkshire retains everything) should want lower stock prices. Investee buybacks benefit Berkshire more at low prices. "Disinvestors lose as market falls - but investors gain." "Markets that then were hostile to investment transients were friendly to those taking up permanent residence."
- Insurance: float definition repeated; "the business is a lemon if its cost of float is higher than market rates for money." New caution: because loss costs are estimated, "insurers have enormous latitude in figuring their underwriting results," reserving errors "can be huge," and "virtually all surprises in insurance are unpleasant ones." Float has had value "greater than an equal amount of net worth would have had."
- Catastrophe bonds: "Orwellian misnomer"; the dice example (1-in-36 risk of a $50 million payout insured for $1 million a year looks like easy money, 75.4% chance of a decade without paying, but "eventually... you would go broke"); experts paid up front who put no money on the table are "less reprehensible - but more dangerous." When prices deteriorate, Berkshire writes less; it would raise worst-case exposure (~$600 million after tax) if prices were right.
- Ajit Jain: "both the discipline to walk away from business that is inadequately priced and the imagination to then find other opportunities."
- GEICO: measured by voluntary policy growth (16% in 1997) and seasoned-business profitability; underwriting profit of 8.1% was "higher than we wish it to be," target ~4% with the rest passed to customers; "a tough market helps the low-cost operator"; rates set to reflect each driver's loss expectancy. Market share only 3% of a $115 billion industry.
- Compensation: "We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets: In none of Berkshire's subsidiaries do we relate compensation to our stock price, which our associates cannot affect in any meaningful way."
- Subsidiaries pay 100% of earnings to the parent (News, See's and Scott Fetzer have sent $1.8 billion), so their reported growth understates their value versus public peers that retain two-thirds of earnings.
- Acquisitions: criteria "understandable; possess excellent economics; and are run by outstanding people." Ask new partners "Are there any more at home like you?" Star deal done in two hours with no need to check leases: "I knew I was dealing with a man of integrity and that's what counted." Sellers who share proceeds with employees signal good partners.
- Cash versus stock: Dairy Queen offered cash or slightly less valuable stock to tilt sellers toward cash. The Confession: aggregating all stock-only mergers (ex-affiliates), "our shareholders are slightly worse off"; "when I've issued stock, I've cost you money." Reason: trading away pieces of a marvelous collection is like trading a .380 hitter for a .350 hitter. Cash acquisitions (National Indemnity, See's, Buffalo News, Scott Fetzer, GEICO) have been far better. Future stock issuance will be reluctant and paired with a cash option.
- General merger calculus: paying a premium makes sense only if "a) its stock is overvalued relative to the acquiree's or b) the two enterprises will earn more combined than they would separately." Acquirers claim synergy because few admit overvaluation; "voracious buyers... are tacitly conceding that point" and often run "Wall Street's version of a chain-letter scheme." "Only in fairy tales are emperors told that they are naked."
- Options: ignoring their cost is "dead wrong"; Berkshire replaces option plans with economically equivalent cash plans so true compensation cost is "brought out of the closet."
- Market valuation framework: at the 1997 meeting (Dow 7,071, long Treasuries 6.89%) the market was not overvalued if 1) rates stayed or fell and 2) ROE stayed remarkably high. Lower rates make every dollar of earnings more valuable, but ROE may not persist, and "Today's price levels, though, have materially eroded the 'margin of safety' that Ben Graham identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing." 1979 Forbes: "You pay a very high price in the stock market for a cheery consensus."
- Stock sales (~5% of portfolio) partly to shift the bond-stock ratio "in response to the relative values that we saw in each market."
- Goizueta model of a CEO: clear strategic vision aimed at shareholders, urgency, "what she refers to as an obsession, you call focus," and seamless succession planning.
- Equal, simultaneous disclosure of information to all shareholders via the Internet when markets are closed.

**Businesses/investments and why.**
- GEICO: low cost, growth, customer pricing; Tony Nicely.
- Star Furniture: integrity of Melvyn Wolff; excellent, accelerating growth; room to multiply in size.
- International Dairy Queen: 5,792 stores, franchise model, understandable.
- Coca-Cola: "steamroller" under Goizueta and Ivester.
- Gillette ($600m to $4.8bn) and First Empire ($40m to $236m).
- Salomon: Maughan and Denham "like, trust and admire."
- USAir: "a record that is unblemished by success"; Stephen Wolf's turnaround.
- American Express: Harvey Golub; Frank Olson convinced him "Amex's corporate card was a terrific franchise"; $3 billion gain, "110% luck."
- Zeros, silver, oil: judgment-based macro commitments, explicitly not core.

**Mistakes admitted.** Stock-financed mergers cost shareholders money. USAir: "I was wrong in originally purchasing the stock, and I was wrong later, in repeatedly trying to unload our holdings at 50 cents on the dollar." Nearly sold Amex in 1994. Salomon 1991-92: "I had an unfortunate seat. It faced the stage."

### 1998 letter (punctuation stripped in source)

**Context.** Net worth +$25.9 billion; per-share book value +48.3% ($37,801; 24.7% over 34 years), but "not as good as it looks" because most of the gain came from issuing shares above book value in the General Re ($22 billion, all stock, closed December 21) and Executive Jet ($725 million, half cash half stock) acquisitions, which raise book value per share without raising intrinsic value. The equity portfolio lagged the S&P as several large investees had operating shortfalls. Net worth $57.4 billion, the largest of any US corporation; Buffett sets a 15% intrinsic-value growth goal "at the very peak of possible outcomes." Over $15 billion in cash at year-end. Two long accounting sections attack stock-option accounting and restructuring/merger-accounting manipulation; SEC Chairman Levitt's "earnings management" speech is endorsed. Berkshire paid $2.7 billion in federal income tax plus a record $30 million SEC fee. Look-through earnings were omitted this year because of the Gen Re timing.

**Core teachings in this letter.**

### 1999 letter (punctuation stripped in source)

**Context.** Net worth +$358 million; per-share book value +0.5% ($37,987; 24.0% over 35 years): "the worst absolute performance of my tenure and compared to the S P the worst relative performance as well." Buffett grades himself "D" in capital allocation. Large investees (Coke, Gillette, etc.) lagged on disappointing operating results; Berkshire's stock fell more than the business. General Re had a $1.4 billion underwriting loss; consolidated float cost 5.8%. Acquisitions for cash: Jordan's Furniture and ~76% of MidAmerican Energy (~$2 billion, structured around the Public Utility Holding Company Act). Buffett encloses his Fortune article arguing the S&P will do far worse in the next decade or two than since 1982. The letter includes the tech/circle-of-competence passage, a long section on share repurchases (A shares had fallen below $45,000), and the pooling-versus-purchase accounting debate.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- "Relative results are what concern us Over time bad relative numbers will produce unsatisfactory absolute results."
- Expectation reset: large superiorities over the S&P "are a thing of the past" (prices higher, capital base larger); and the S&P itself will do "far less well in the next decade or two."
- Cash is "the method of acquisition that Charlie and I vastly prefer."
- Goodwill amortization (~$500 million a year) is "an accounting matter having nothing to do with true economic goodwill which increases in most years" and persistently widens the gap between intrinsic value and book value. See's and Buffalo News "are now worth fifteen to twenty times the value at which they are carried on our books."
- Managers: mostly independently wealthy, no contracts, no budgets to approve, no show-and-tell; asked to run companies "as if these are the sole asset of their families and will remain so for the next century." The Bill Child/Boise story: he built the store with his own $9 million, sold it to Berkshire at cost only after it succeeded, and refused interest.
- Insurance economics restated: amount of float, its cost, and "most critical of all the long term outlook for both." "Growth of float is important but its cost is what's vital." Reserve adjustments at other insurers in 1999 "made a mockery of the earnings that investors had relied on." $400 million of the 1999 loss was "enthusiastically welcomed" because it brings exceptional float for a decade.
- Ajit Jain's four virtues: "the intelligence to properly rate most risks the realism to forget about those he can't evaluate the courage to write huge policies when the premium is appropriate and the discipline to reject even the smallest risk when the premium is inadequate."
- Gen Re's incentive plans now tied to "float growth and cost of float the same variables that determine value for owners."
- GEICO: spending rose to $242 million; "we would happily commit 1 billion annually to marketing if we knew we could handle the business smoothly and if we expected the last dollar spent to produce new business at an attractive cost." Inquiry costs are rising (media rates, diminishing returns) but closure ratio improving; renewal operating costs "are the lowest among broad based national auto insurers... Others may copy our model but they will be unable to replicate our economics." Retention decline explained by mix shift and new-customer share. Industry margins to fall ~3 points in 2000; reserve releases that flattered earnings "has now largely dried up." Compensation: "we will foot the bill for new business" so associates are rewarded for initially unprofitable growth plus seasoned-book costs. Could hold policy count with $50 million of ads, so the extra $300-350 million is an optional outlay to build the brand; each household relationship sends ~$1,100 a year, "cash is pouring in rather than going out no Internet economics here."
- Contrast of economic characteristics within aviation: FlightSafety "must lay out huge amounts of capital" (simulators up to $15 million; 222 of them; one trainee at a time), so "Operating margins must therefore also be high if we are to earn a reasonable return on capital." EJA: "the customer owns the equipment," so "modest pre tax margins in this business can produce good returns on equity." Scale is vital: "Buy a fraction get a fleet." Multi-supplier advantage over manufacturer-owned competitors.
- Acquisitions: investment-banker books are Superman comics; projected earnings have false precision while the banker won't forecast his own firm's next month. Scott Fetzer 1985 story: Charlie "I'll pay 2.5 million not to read it." "our carefully crafted acquisition strategy is simply to wait for the phone to ring." Referrals from previous sellers are the main source.
- Jordan's: "highest sales per square foot of any major furniture operation in the country"; the Tatelmans paid $9 million of their own money to employees.
- Acquisition accounting: goodwill amortization charges "are usually spurious"; economic goodwill "is much like land" (value fluctuates but is not ordained to fall; See's goodwill has grown for 78 years), unlike depreciation, inventory obsolescence, bad debts and warranty accruals which "reflect true costs." Pooling is a fiction ("ask employees severed from their jobs which company was the conqueror"). Proposal: record the purchase at fair value, carry goodwill without amortization, write it down only when impaired, applied retroactively; managements would then "structure acquisitions more sensibly deciding whether to use cash or stock based on the real consequences for their shareholders." Tax: buying assets for cash (Jordan's) allows 15-year tax amortization of goodwill, which "materially increases the amount of cash delivered"; stock-for-stock is "the worst deal of all" economically. "We have never done an inefficiently structured deal however in order to make our figures look better."

- See's: record 24% operating margin; $857 million pre-tax earned since the 1972 $25 million purchase "and despite its growth the business has required very little additional capital." Chuck Huggins' "fanatical insistence on both product quality and friendly service."

No expertise in patents, manufacturing processes or geological prospects either. "If we have a strength it is in recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of competence and when we are approaching the perimeter." Others' claims validated by stock prices are neither envied nor emulated; straying would only be inadvertent, "not because we got restless and substituted hope for rationality."
- Price versus quality: "we feel much better about the businesses than their stocks." "If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price or a comfortable business at a questionable price we much prefer the latter What really gets our attention however is a comfortable business at a comfortable price."

"Fools give you reasons wise men never try."

Business needs are maintenance spending (Helzberg's remodeling) and growth outlays expected to produce more than a dollar of value per dollar spent (R.C. Willey's Idaho expansion). In the 1970s repurchasers were a tipoff of undervaluation and shareholder-oriented management; now buybacks are "all the rage" to "pump or support the stock price." "Buying dollar bills for 1.10 is not good business for those who stick around." Intrinsic value can be estimated for only a portion of equities and only as a range, never a "pseudo precise figure." Buying back to offset option exercises is "buy high sell low"; each decision "should stand on its own feet." Buffett admits having erred by not repurchasing in the past, though a 2% buyback at a 25% discount adds only ~0.5% to per-share value. Continuing holders should hope the stock falls. Berkshire will never buy "with the intention of stemming a decline" and has never told anyone to buy or sell its shares.

### 2000 letter (punctuation stripped in source)

**Context.** Net worth +$3.96 billion; per-share book value +6.5% ($40,442; 23.6% over 36 years), beating the S&P 500 (which fell). Eight acquisitions totalling ~$8 billion, 97% cash: MidAmerican (76%), CORT (via Wesco, $386 million), U.S. Liability (half stock), Ben Bridge Jeweler (half stock), Justin Industries ($570 million), Shaw Industries (87.3%, ~$4 billion sales, the largest non-insurance business), Benjamin Moore ($1 billion), Johns Manville (~$1.8 billion). No debt incurred; shares up only 1/3 of 1%. GEICO's policy growth "slowed to a halt" and Buffett says "I was wrong" about advertising returns. Underwriting loss $1.6 billion, float cost 6%, including large retroactive reinsurance deals (Ajit's $2.4 billion premium from a UK company). Nearly all Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae sold; 15 mid-sized positions and some high-yield bonds bought. The letter contains the Aesop "bird in the bush" valuation discussion and the dot-com bubble post-mortem, the Dexter goodwill write-off, the EBITDA/tooth-fairy and growth-prediction passages, and the 50th-anniversary tribute to Ben Graham.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Goal: per-share value growth that "will modestly exceed the gain from owning the S P 500"; "a small annual advantage in our favor can if sustained produce an anything but small long term advantage." Requires adding a few good businesses a year, existing businesses gaining value, and avoiding "any material increase in our outstanding shares."
- Managers: at Berkshire "our all stars have exactly the jobs they want" and never leave ("never had a manager of a significant subsidiary voluntarily leave"). Franklin: "A small leak can sink a great ship" (headquarters now 13.8 people).
- Acquisition technique: "We answer the phone." CORT: "a fine though unglamorous business an outstanding manager and a price going by that on the failed deal that made sense." Deals made by phone before meeting (Ben Bridge, U.S. Liability). Promises to sellers (headquarters stays, no merger with another jeweler) "can take those promises to the bank."
- Never write an uncapped policy: Shaw's merger partner wanted insurance for all future asbestos costs; "we would never issue a policy that lacked a cap." Conversely Berkshire made an all-cash, no-financing-contingency offer for Johns Manville within days of the LBO collapsing.
- Earnings bumps don't matter: "Only in the sales presentations of investment banks do earnings move forever upward We don't care about the bumps what matters are the overall results."
- Why 2000 was fertile: sellers foresaw slowdowns, and the junk-bond market dried up so LBO buyers "became unable to borrow all they thought they needed"; "Because we analyze purchases on an all equity basis our evaluations did not change which means we became considerably more competitive."
- Seller quality as a signal: an owner who loves his company signals "honest accounting pride of product respect for customers and a loyal group of associates." Auctioned businesses "dressed up for sale particularly when the seller is a financial owner." The Rembrandt analogy: the painter should choose the permanent home.
- Insurance economics restated; new warning: "Both the income statements and balance sheets of insurers can be minefields"; "there is nothing symmetrical about surprises in the insurance business They almost always are unpleasant." Cost of float history: close to zero overall, 19% in the terrible 1984, 6% in 2000.
- Two unusual float sources: (1) policies that penalize current earnings but deliver float for years ("pain today gain tomorrow"), $482 million of the 2000 loss; (2) retroactive reinsurance of past losses, always capped; GAAP sets up a "deferred charges applicable to assumed reinsurance" asset amortized into underwriting losses "often stretching into decades," offset by "lots of" float. Net: cost of float will be "very attractive" but "rarely to return to a no cost mode." "the ultimate benefits that we derive from float will depend not only on its cost but fully as important how effectively we deploy it."
- Risk appetite: Grab.com $1 billion prize ($170 million present value) insured; A-Rod disability. "Charlie and I however like any proposition that makes compelling mathematical sense regardless of its effect on reported earnings."
- GEICO post-mortem: "Agonizing over errors is a mistake But acknowledging and analyzing them can be useful though that practice is rare in corporate boardrooms." The Washington Post Co. reviews acquisitions three years later; elsewhere "CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth" and dump blunders into "nonrecurring" charges. Four causes of higher acquisition cost: ad frequency overstepped (diminishing returns per medium); low-hanging fruit picked (older Americans resist direct purchase); stricter underwriting and rate rises as frequency/severity rose ("some prospects will switch to save 200 per year but will not switch to save 50"); State Farm (19% share) slow to raise prices despite an 18% underwriting loss, implying a ~23% float cost versus GEICO's 6.1%.
- Commodity economics: "when a company is selling a product with commodity like economic characteristics being the low cost producer is all important." GEICO "has much the better business model" and "will inevitably increase its market share significantly while simultaneously achieving excellent profits."
- State Farm as counter-evidence to business-school dogma (mutual, no stock options, no capital markets, yet tangible net worth exceeding all but four US businesses): "Studying counter evidence is a highly useful activity."
- Tony Nicely: "He never engages in wishful thinking or otherwise distorts reality as so many managers do when the unexpected happens"; cut non-cost-effective ads, filed rate increases, "looks at the loss reports every day."
- Preference for businesses over stocks (Owner's Manual principle 4): personal (working with owner-oriented managers) and tax: with 80%+ ownership "the government is our partner twice when we own part of a business through a stock investment but only once when we own at least 80."
- Valuation (Aesop): the formula for valuing all assets bought for financial gain is "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush," requiring three answers: "How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush When will they emerge and how many will there be What is the risk free interest rate which we consider to be the yield on long term U.S. bonds." That gives "the maximum value of the bush." It "applies to outlays for farms oil royalties bonds stocks lottery tickets and manufacturing plants" and was not changed by the steam engine, electricity, the automobile or the Internet. "Common yardsticks such as dividend yield the ratio of price to earnings or to book value and even growth rates have nothing to do with valuation except to the extent they provide clues to the amount and timing of cash flows." "growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs in the early years of a project or enterprise that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later years." Growth-versus-value styles reflect "ignorance not their sophistication"; "Growth is simply a component usually a plus sometimes a minus in the value equation."
- Precision: "Using precise numbers is in fact foolish working with a range of possibilities is the better approach Usually the range must be so wide that no useful conclusion can be reached." Occasionally conservative estimates show a price "startlingly low in relation to value" ("IBT Inefficient Bush Theory"). The investor needs "some general understanding of business economics as well as the ability to think independently" but "not brilliance nor blinding insights."
- Investment versus speculation: when no conviction can be mustered even with a broad range (typical of "new businesses and rapidly changing industries"), "any capital commitment must be labeled speculative." Speculation focuses "not on what an asset will produce but rather on what the next fellow will pay"; it's "neither illegal immoral nor un American" but "We bring nothing to the party so why should we expect to take anything home."
- Bubble psychology: "Nothing sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money"; Cinderella at the ball planning to leave "just seconds before midnight" but "dancing in a room in which the clocks have no hands." The December 1999 Paine Webber-Gallup survey: investors expected 19% a year for the decade, "an irrational expectation." Valuations put on businesses "almost certain to end up being of modest or no value"; a "virus" decoupled stock values from business values.
- Value creation versus wealth transfer: "value is destroyed not created by any business that loses money over its lifetime no matter how high its interim valuation may get." Promoters "merchandising birdless bushes" moved billions "from the pockets of the public to their own purses." "a bubble market has allowed the creation of bubble companies entities designed more with an eye to making money off investors rather than for them Too often an IPO not profits was the primary goal." Chain letter with "fee hungry investment bankers" as postmen. "But a pin lies in wait for every bubble." Two old lessons: Wall Street "will sell investors anything they will buy" and "speculation is most dangerous when it looks easiest."

Keep estimates conservative, "focus on industries where business surprises are unlikely to wreak havoc on owners." Admits still making mistakes: "I'mthe fellow remember who thought he understood the future economics of trading stamps textiles shoes and second tier department stores."

- Dexter: "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993 Furthermore I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." All remaining accounting goodwill written off; "we clearly have none at present."
- Depreciation is real: FlightSafety spent $272 million on simulators; "Anyone who thinks that the annual charges for depreciation don't reflect a real cost every bit as real as payroll or raw materials should get an internship at a simulator company Every year we spend amounts equal to our depreciation charge simply to stay in the same economic place and then spend additional sums to grow."

## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 1995-2000

### 1. What investing IS, according to these letters
- A stock is a part interest in a business, and the art of buying stocks is "little different from the art of successfully acquiring subsidiaries": at a sensible price, buy a business with excellent economics and able, honest management, then merely monitor that these are preserved (1996). The same "double-barrelled" logic lets Berkshire compare whole-company purchases against "small pieces of the best businesses in the world via the stock market" (1995).
- The explicit goal: "purchase, at a rational price, a part interest in an easily-understandable business whose earnings are virtually certain to be materially higher five, ten and twenty years from now," buy a meaningful amount when one qualifies, and hold ten years or not ten minutes (1996). A portfolio whose aggregate earnings march upward will see its market value follow; that is "the exact approach that has produced gains for Berkshire shareholders" via look-through earnings (1996).
- The universal valuation formula is Aesop's: estimate how many birds are in the bush, when they emerge, and discount at the long-term Treasury rate; this is "immutable" across farms, royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets and factories, and the Internet changed it not "one iota" (2000).
- Investment versus speculation: speculation focuses on what the next fellow will pay rather than what the asset produces; when no conviction about the birds is possible even using a wide range, the commitment is speculative (2000). "We bring nothing to the party" in that game (2000).
- Time horizon: Berkshire's shareholders are savers ("Berkshire 'saves' for them by retaining all earnings"), so like hamburger buyers they should want lower prices (1997). Managers are told to run businesses as if they cannot be sold for a century (1998, 1999). Markets hostile to "transients" are friendly to "permanent residents" (1997).
- Only two courses matter: "How to Value a Business, and How to Think About Market Prices"; beta, EMT, MPT and option pricing are unnecessary (1996).
- Relative results against the S&P (after Berkshire's corporate-tax drag) are the yardstick; bad relative numbers eventually produce bad absolute ones (1997, 1999).

### 2. What to look for in a business
- Predictability and absence of major change: "we favor businesses and industries unlikely to experience major change" because the aim is near-certainty of "enormous competitive strength ten or twenty years from now"; fast-changing industries "preclude[] the certainty we seek" (1996). The "Inevitables" (Coke, Gillette) will dominate "for an investment lifetime," but there are "dozens of Impostors" for every Inevitable and leadership alone (GM, IBM, Sears) is no guarantee (1996). Prefer "certain of a good result" over "hopeful of a great one" (1996).
- Durable low-cost position in a commodity-like product: GEICO's direct-marketing cost advantage, the same in 2000 as in 1951, creates a virtuous circle of low prices, good policyholders and referrals; "when a company is selling a product with commodity like economic characteristics being the low cost producer is all important" (1995, 1996, 2000). "Others may copy our model but they will be unable to replicate our economics" (1999).
- Moats: "economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats'"; GEICO's moat widened as costs fell (1995); scale economies that widen with penetration (1996); Acme's 75% unaided brand recall in Texas (2000); Benjamin Moore's dealer network (2000); EJA's "armada" that improves service while cutting dead-head cost (1998, 1999).
- Capital intensity and return on capital: See's earned $857 million pre-tax on a $25 million purchase while requiring "very little additional capital" (1999); Scott Fetzer earned $96.5 million after tax on $112 million of net worth with no leverage (1998) and distributed $1.03 billion against a $230 million cost (2000). FlightSafety is explicitly flagged as capital intensive, so it needs high margins to earn a reasonable return, whereas EJA's customer-owned fleet lets modest margins produce good ROE (1996, 1999). Depreciation is a real cost; FSI spends an amount equal to depreciation "simply to stay in the same economic place" (2000).
- "Have-to-be-smart-once" businesses (network TV stations) versus "have-to-be-smart-every-day" businesses like retailing, where "to coast is to fail" and management is indispensable (1995).
- Growth creates value only when it produces more than a dollar of value per dollar spent (GEICO advertising, 1998; the repurchase test, 1999); "growth can destroy value" when early cash inputs exceed the discounted later cash flows, and value is destroyed by any business that loses money over its lifetime whatever its interim valuation (2000).
- Industry structure warnings: deregulated revenues with regulated-era costs (USAir, 1996); imports making domestic shoe manufacturing uncompetitive (Dexter, 1999-2000); newspapers losing notches and encyclopedias facing CD-ROMs (1995); reinsurance underpricing (1999-2000).
- "Few property casualty companies are outstanding businesses" (2000); retailing is a shooting-star field (1995).

### 3. What to look for in management
- Integrity first: deals with Star and others closed without checking leases because "I knew I was dealing with a man of integrity and that's what counted" (1997); sellers who share proceeds with employees (Helzberg, Star, Jordan's, Ben Bridge) signal partners who will treat the buyer right (1995, 1997, 1999, 2000); Tom Murphy "unfailingly favored the owners" (1995); an owner who loves his business signals "honest accounting pride of product respect for customers" (2000).
- Owner-orientation and autonomy: managers run businesses as if they own 100%, as the family's only asset, unsaleable for a century; no budgets, no show-and-tell, no second-guessing (1995, 1998, 1999). Most are independently wealthy and work for love of the business; none has ever left (1995, 1999, 2000). Age is irrelevant ("difficult to teach a new dog old tricks," 1996; Ralph Schey hired at 61, 2000).
- Focus: loss of focus is "what most worries Charlie and me" about great businesses (Coke's shrimp, Gillette's oil, 1996); Goizueta's "obsession... you call focus" (1997); Tony Nicely's "business focus never wavers" and he "never engages in wishful thinking" (1996, 2000).
- The institutional imperative in acquisitions: CEOs enthused about a foolish deal get whatever projections they need (1997); bankers' books are Superman comics (1999); Drucker's "Dealmaking beats working" (1995); CEOs "invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth" rather than conduct post-mortems, unlike the Washington Post Co. (2000).
- Compensation: tailored to the unit's economics, simple and measurable, tied to what participants control; no "lottery ticket" options on Berkshire stock; never raise the bar after good work; pay the .350 hitter even if the team bats .200 (1996). "Merit badges, not lottery tickets" (1997). GEICO's two variables exclude first-year acquisition costs so growth is rewarded (1998, 1999); Gen Re's plan is tied to float growth and cost (1999). Option plans are replaced with equivalent cash plans so the cost is counted (1997, 1998).
- Candour and reporting: tell the bad news (1995); give owners what you would want if positions were reversed, simultaneously (every year); CEOs who woo investors with growth forecasts, who use vague accounting or EBITDA, or whose letters are written by PR departments are red flags (2000). The premise that a CEO's job is the highest possible stock price is one "with which we adamantly disagree" (1998).

### 4. Valuation: price, intrinsic value, margin of safety, discount rates, growth
- Intrinsic value, not book value, is what counts; Berkshire publishes a two-column guide (investments per share plus pre-tax operating earnings per share) to help shareholders estimate it (1995-1999). Issuing shares above book inflates book value but not intrinsic value (1998); goodwill amortization widens the gap mechanically (1999).
- The discounted-cash-flow definition via Aesop: certainty, timing and amount of cash, discounted at the long-term government bond yield; P/E, P/B, dividend yield and growth rates matter only as clues to cash flows; growth is "a component usually a plus sometimes a minus" (2000). The same formula applies to stocks and whole businesses, differing only in taxes (2000).
- Imprecision: work with a range, not a "pseudo precise figure"; usually the range is too wide to act; occasionally even conservative estimates show a "startlingly low" price (1999, 2000). Insurance pricing and investing both take years to reveal whether you knew what you were doing (1996).
- Margin of safety: by 1997 price levels "materially eroded" Graham's margin of safety; the market was defensible only if rates stayed low and ROE stayed exceptionally high (1997). Overpayment risk applies even to the Inevitables, and catching up to a high price can take an extended period (1996).
- Price versus business quality: "a comfortable business at a questionable price" is preferred to "a questionable business at a comfortable price," but what gets attention is "a comfortable business at a comfortable price" (1999). Berkshire said its own stock was not undervalued at $36,000 (1995), considered repurchases below $45,000 (1999), and would buy back only "well below intrinsic value conservatively calculated" (1999).
- Discount-rate sensitivity: lower rates make "every dollar of earnings... more valuable" (1997); zero-coupon Treasuries chosen as a rate bet (1997); the risk-free rate is the third Aesop variable (2000).
- Expected returns: profits tied to ~5% nominal GDP growth imply equity returns "dramatically less" than investors expect; a 19% expectation is irrational (1999, 2000).
- Buybacks are valuation decisions: repurchase only below conservative intrinsic value with surplus funds and informed shareholders; buying "dollar bills for 1.10" hurts continuing holders; option-offset buybacks are "buy high sell low" (1999).

### 5. Temperament and psychology
- Inactivity: "we make more money when snoring than when active" (1996); "activity does not correlate with achievement" and Berkshire "mainly just wait[s] for the phone to ring" (1998, 1999).
- Patience with no called strikes: Ted Williams' 77 cells; standing with the bat on the shoulder is unpleasant but swinging at low outside pitches locks in low returns (1997).
- Mr. Market logic: net savers should welcome falling prices; "Disinvestors lose as market falls - but investors gain" (1997). Don't sell fine businesses on scary news; if a crash or a super-cat loss would make you panic, don't own stocks (1996).
- Concentration: a sound strategy naturally produces a few dominant holdings; don't trade Michael Jordan because he's become important (1996). Buffett had 65% of his net worth in GEICO at 21 (1995); the Buffetts keep 99% in Berkshire (1997-1999).
- Circle of competence: size doesn't matter, knowing the boundary does (1996); Berkshire can't identify durable tech advantages "by studying up," and its strength is recognizing when it nears the perimeter; straying would be "substitut[ing] hope for rationality" (1999). Known blind spots: investment banking, paper, patents, manufacturing processes, geology (1995, 1999).
- Avoiding ruin: invert ("where I'm going to die"), never borrow big, cap every insurance policy, keep worst-case losses tolerable relative to intrinsic value (1996, 2000).
- Bubble psychology: effortless money sedates rationality; Cinderella dancers plan to leave before midnight but the clocks have no hands; speculation is most dangerous when it looks easiest (2000). Don't try to pick the winners from "an ocean of unproven enterprises" (2000).
- Honesty about errors and avoiding regret: "Agonizing over errors is a mistake But acknowledging and analyzing them can be useful" (2000); 1999's grade of D; "Quack" in 1997.
- Distrust of precision and models: computer models' precision is "a chimera" that invites huge mistakes (1996); expert risk assessors with no money on the table are dangerous (1997).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons
- Look-through earnings: operating earnings + Berkshire's share of investees' retained earnings - a 14% hypothetical tax; retained earnings reinvested at high returns are worth more than dividends (1996, 1997, 1999, 2000).
- Purchase-accounting adjustments are shown separately so businesses can be judged as if never acquired (every year). Goodwill amortization charges are "usually spurious" because economic goodwill, like land, is not ordained to decline and at See's has grown for 78 years; depreciation, obsolescence, bad debts and warranties by contrast "reflect true costs." Proposed rule: carry goodwill at cost, write down only on impairment (1999); FASB's 2000 move in that direction is welcomed and "will have [no] effect on Berkshire's intrinsic value" (2000). Pooling is a fiction (1999). Tax-deductible 15-year goodwill from asset purchases is economically superior; stock-for-stock deals are the worst (1999).
- Stock options are compensation and an expense; Berkshire subtracts the value at which equivalent options could be sold publicly, cutting reported EPS by 5-10%; the three unanswered questions (1998); option plans are swapped for cash plans when companies are acquired (1997, 1998).
- Restructuring charges and merger accounting as manipulation: the golf-scorecard and big-bath analogies; acquisition reserve boosts in P&C deals; 1,369 special charges of $72.1 billion in 1998; "Bad accounting drives out good"; auditors sing the song of whoever feeds them (1998). Earnings targets corrode behaviour "from fudging into fraud" (2000).
- EBITDA: "does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures"; depreciation at a simulator company is as real as payroll (2000).
- Insurance accounting: float computed as loss and LAE reserves + funds held + unearned premiums - agents' balances/receivables, prepaid acquisition costs, prepaid taxes and deferred charges (every year); reserving latitude makes cost of float hard to verify, auditors bless amazing numbers, and surprises are asymmetric (1997-2000). Retroactive reinsurance creates a deferred-charge asset amortized into underwriting losses for decades (2000). Step-acquisition write-downs (GEICO) are accounting, not economics (1996).
- Book value versus intrinsic value: issuing stock above book raises book but not value (1998); See's and Buffalo News carried at 1/15-1/20 of worth (1999).
- Mark-to-market: commodity positions and zeros are carried at market, so unrealized gains flow into reported figures (1997).
- Corporate tax nuances: no capital-gains preference at 35%; insurers' dividend exclusion 59.5% (1998); the "government is our partner twice" when owning stocks versus once with 80%+ subsidiaries (2000).

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level
- The retained-earnings test applied to GEICO growth spending and to subsidiaries' optional outlays: spend when it creates more than a dollar of value per dollar (1998, 1999).
- Issuing stock: stock-only mergers have made shareholders slightly worse off ("when I've issued stock, I've cost you money"); trading .380 hitters for .350 hitters; future deals will push cash options (1997). 97% of 2000's $8 billion was cash and shares rose only 1/3 of 1% (2000). Dexter's share payment "compounded" the mistake (2000). Takeover premiums are rational only with an overvalued currency or real synergy; serial issuers run chain letters (1997).
- Buybacks: the only valid combination is surplus funds plus a price below conservative intrinsic value with fully informed shareholders; never to prop the price; past failure to repurchase admitted (1999). Investee buybacks at low prices (Coke, Post, Wells) benefited Berkshire more than today's at high prices (1997).
- Dividends: none; Berkshire retains everything because investees and subsidiaries reinvest at high returns, and subsidiaries upstream 100% of earnings for redeployment ($1.8 billion from News, See's, Scott Fetzer; $1.03 billion from Scott Fetzer alone) (1997, 2000).
- Leverage: "we don't borrow big amounts" (1996); float is cheap leverage only if its cost is low, otherwise "leverage becomes a disadvantage" (1995); exchangeable notes at ~1.1% (1996); purchases analysed "on an all equity basis," which is an advantage when LBO credit dries up (2000).
- Acquisitions: no strategic plan, compare every deal to passive alternatives, never read seller projections, pay for cash flows not synergies, prefer negotiated deals with owners who care, avoid auctions (1995, 1999, 2000). Negotiated deals yield only "reasonable returns" unless capital markets are constrained (2000). Cash is held reluctantly but readily ($15 billion in 1998).
- Capital allocation is Buffett's "one subject," and 1999's D grade shows he treats it as the central managerial job (1999).

### 8. Insurance and float
- Definitions and the test, repeated each year: float is money held but not owned; its cost is the underwriting loss; the business is valuable if cost of float is below the cost of funds otherwise obtainable and a "lemon"/"albatross"/"negative value" if above market rates (1995-2000).
- Three determinants of an insurer's value: amount of float, its cost, and above all the long-term outlook for both (1998-2000). Cost is what's vital; cheap float "outranks net worth in value"; expensive float makes growth "a curse" (1998, 1999).
- History: float grew from $17 million (1967) to $27.9 billion (2000); cost was negative in most years, 19% in 1984, 5.8% in 1999 and 6% in 2000 after Gen Re's underpricing and retroactive deals (1995-2000).
- Super-cat: a terrible year is a certainty; price so ~90% of premium is eventually paid out; refuse inadequate rates even at the cost of volume; a lumpy 15% beats a smooth 12%; never lay off risk (collectability doubts); three advantages are certainty of payment, post-catastrophe capacity, and size (1995-1997). Catastrophe bonds and expert-blessed mispricing (1997).
- General Re rationale: public reinsurers are perversely graded on smoothness; Berkshire's tolerance for volatility and "Fort Knox" capital let it retain more business; compensation retargeted to float growth and cost (1998, 1999).
- GEICO economics: low cost, pass savings to customers (4% target margin), referrals, acquisition cost excluded from bonuses, growth measured by voluntary policies and seasoned profitability, marketing as a value-creating investment, then the 2000 post-mortem on rising acquisition costs and State Farm's irrational pricing (1995-2000).
- Risks: reserving latitude and asymmetric surprises; alignment of underwriters' incentives with capital providers (Lloyd's); the hell of a bad reinsurance contract; always cap exposure; retroactive reinsurance as "pain today gain tomorrow" (1995-2000).

### 9. Mistakes and what changed in his thinking
- Selling wonderful businesses too early: GEICO 1952, Disney 1967, a third of Cap Cities 1993 (1995); McDonald's 1998 (1998). Lesson repeated: don't sell "a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company."
- Structure errors: Gillette preferred instead of common (~$555 million foregone) (1995); Salomon preferred bought with "no special insights" (1995, 1997); the "almost impossible to lose money" claim on preferreds (1995).
- USAir: superficial analysis seduced by history and seniority; tried twice to sell at a loss; saved by luck and Stephen Wolf (1995-1997).
- Stock issuance: aggregate stock-only mergers hurt shareholders (1997); Dexter was overpaid for and paid in shares, goodwill written off (2000). This hardens into an explicit cash preference (1999, 2000).
- Reinsurance written at inadequate rates in the 1970s (1995, 1996); Gen Re's underpriced book after acquisition (1999, 2000).
- GEICO advertising: "I was wrong" that extra dollars would pay (2000).
- Not repurchasing Berkshire stock when it was cheap (1999).
- Capital allocation grade of D for 1999; "we feel much better about the businesses than their stocks" (1999).
- Self-described pattern: "I'mthe fellow remember who thought he understood the future economics of trading stamps textiles shoes and second tier department stores" (2000).
- Evolution over the period: (a) from Graham-style cheap statistics (the 1x-earnings Western Insurance switch) to paying "a steep price" for GEICO because of its moat (1995); (b) formalisation of "Inevitables" and change-avoidance (1996), then an explicit refusal to buy tech during the bubble because durable advantage there is unknowable (1999, 2000); (c) a steadily more sober view of market returns, from "not overvalued if rates and ROE hold" (1997) to "wildly optimistic" expectations (1999) and the bubble post-mortem (2000); (d) a growing emphasis on whole-business acquisitions for cash over stocks, driven by size, taxes and the lack of bargains (1997-2000); (e) accounting activism (options, restructurings, goodwill) sharpened after General Re (1998-2000).

### 11. Macro and market views
- Interest rates as the valuation anchor: the market was not overvalued in 1997 if rates stayed or fell and ROE stayed high; lower rates make each earnings dollar worth more (1997); the long-term government bond yield is the risk-free rate in Aesop's formula (2000); zero-coupon Treasuries bought as a rate judgment with "nothing close to a 100% probability" of success (1997).
- Forecasting: "we have absolutely no view" on whether prices will fall (1997); "We have never attempted to forecast what the stock market is going to do in the next month or the next year" (1999); but Berkshire does "try, in a very rough way, to value it" (1997) and expects "the virtual certainty" that the S&P will do far worse in the next decade or two than since 1982 (1999, Fortune article enclosed).
- Expected return arithmetic: profits track GDP (~3% real + ~2% inflation per TIPS), so valuations can't compound much faster than ~5%; 19% expectations are irrational and the adjustment will be severe where speculation concentrated (1999, 2000).
- Inflation: explicitly "play[s] no part" in the silver thesis (1997); 2% assumed because TIPS imply it, with no conviction (1999).
- Bubble commentary: unit trusts would have created a self-validating bubble in Berkshire stock (1996); the "cheery consensus" (1997); "the truly manic portions of this enchanted market" (1999); the full post-mortem on bubble companies, IPO-as-goal, chain letters, wealth transfer and the inevitable pin (2000).
- Derivatives and alternative assets: oil contracts and silver taken on fundamental supply/demand views and carried at market (1997); explicit warning that macro bets can look foolish; subsequent policy of not disclosing unconventional positions (1998).
- Leverage and credit cycles: junk-bond laxity in 1998-99 produced ballooning defaults and starved LBO buyers in 2000, which benefited an all-equity buyer (2000); Berkshire's refusal to "borrow big amounts" (1996).
- Catastrophe and financial contagion: a mega-catastrophe could trigger a financial panic that impairs other reinsurers; Berkshire plans so it can pay regardless (1996).
- Reinsurance and auto-insurance cycles: soft reinsurance market after capital influx and cat bonds (1995, 1997); auto margins would compress after unusual drops in accident frequency and the exhaustion of reserve releases (1997, 1999); State Farm's willingness to run a 23% float cost suppresses the industry (2000).
- Corporate America's ROE "remarkable" but "not a sure thing to remain" (1997); growth-rate predictions of 15% are achievable by fewer than 10 of the 200 largest earners (2000).
