## Part 1 — 1983 Appendix: Goodwill and its Amortization: The Rules and The Realities

### 1.1 Economic goodwill vs. accounting goodwill

- Scope: the appendix "deals only with economic and accounting Goodwill — not the goodwill of everyday usage." Being liked by customers is irrelevant: AT&T before the breakup was well regarded but "possessed not a dime of economic Goodwill"; a disliked business can have "substantial, and growing, economic Goodwill."
- **Accounting goodwill** = a purchase-price residual. When a business is bought, the price is first assigned to the fair value of identifiable assets (net of liabilities); any excess goes to an asset account called "excess of cost over equity in net assets acquired" — which he shortens to "Goodwill." It is a bookkeeping artifact of a specific purchase date and price, and it declines mechanically through amortization.
- **Economic goodwill** = the capitalized value of excess returns. "businesses logically are worth far more than net tangible assets when they can be expected to produce earnings on such assets considerably in excess of market rates of return. The capitalized value of this excess return is economic Goodwill."
- Sources of economic goodwill: a consumer franchise ("a pervasive favorable reputation with consumers based upon countless pleasant experiences they have had with both product and personnel"), which "allows the value of the product to the purchaser, rather than its production cost, to be the major determinant of selling price"; also "governmental franchises not subject to profit regulation, such as television stations, and an enduring position as the low cost producer in an industry."
- The two move in opposite directions: "while accounting Goodwill regularly decreased from the moment of purchase, economic Goodwill increased in irregular but very substantial fashion."
- "Spurious accounting Goodwill" exists too: when management overpays, "the silliness ends up in the Goodwill account" — he suggests it "might better be labeled 'No-Will'." Accounting goodwill therefore can be either real economic goodwill or capitalized "adrenalin."

### 1.2 The See's Candies example (actual numbers)

- Purchase: Blue Chip Stamps bought See's **early in 1972 for $25 million**.
- Net tangible assets at purchase: **about $8 million** (receivables counted as tangible assets; adequate to run the business without debt except seasonally).
- After-tax earnings at purchase: **about $2 million**, "conservatively representative of future earning power in constant 1972 dollars."
- Return on net tangible assets: **25% after tax**, "with conservative accounting and no financial leverage." Few businesses in 1972 (or 1983) could do this consistently.
- Implied economic goodwill at purchase: $25M − $8M = **$17 million** paid over net tangible assets.
- Accounting treatment of that $17M: Goodwill asset on Blue Chip's books, amortized at **$425,000 per year for 40 years**. By 1983 (after 11 years) it was down to **about $12.5 million**; Berkshire's 60% share of Blue Chip meant its balance sheet carried **about $7.5 million** of See's goodwill.
- 1983 Blue Chip merger (purchase accounting): Berkshire "paid" **$51.7 million** more than net identifiable assets for the remaining 40% of Blue Chip, assigned **$28.4 million to See's** and **$23.3 million to Buffalo Evening News**. Post-merger See's goodwill has two pieces: $7.5M (1972 purchase) + $28.4M (1983). New amortization: **about $1.0 million/year for 28 years, then $0.7 million/year for 12 years (2002–2013)**. "different purchase dates and prices have given us vastly different asset values and amortization charges for two pieces of the same asset."
- See's in 1983 ("last year"): **$13 million after tax on about $20 million of net tangible assets** (~65% return) — "a performance indicating the existence of economic Goodwill far larger than the total original cost of our accounting Goodwill." Hence the amortization charges taken since 1972 "were not true economic costs."
- Berkshire totals, yearend 1983: net accounting goodwill $62 million ($79M stated asset less $17M negative goodwill offset against Mutual Savings and Loan); "net economic Goodwill far exceeds the $62 million accounting number."

### 1.3 Why economic goodwill grows with inflation while asset-heavy businesses suffer

The See's vs. "mundane business" thought experiment:

| | See's | Mundane business |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings (1972) | $2M | $2M |
| Net tangible assets required | $8M | $18M |
| Return on tangible assets | 25% | 11% |
| Economic goodwill | Large | "little or no" |
| Plausible price | $25M (paid) | $18M (= tangible assets) |

Then assume the price level doubles:
- Both need nominal earnings to double to **$4M** just to stay even. Selling the same units at double prices with unchanged margins achieves this.
- But both "probably would have to double their nominal investment in net tangible assets" — more dollars immediately in receivables and inventories, fixed assets "more slowly ... but probably just as surely." This investment "will produce no improvement in rate of return. The motivation for this investment is the survival of the business, not the prosperity of the owner."
- See's must add **$8M**; the mundane business must add **$18M** ("a burden over twice as large").
- Result: mundane business earning $4M is still worth its tangible assets, **$36M** — "only a dollar of nominal value for every new dollar invested," the same as a savings account. See's earning $4M, valued on the same basis as in 1972, is worth **$50M** — a $25M nominal gain on $8M of added capital, "over $3 of nominal value gained for each $1 invested."
- Mechanism: "true economic Goodwill tends to rise in nominal value proportionally with inflation," because the franchise lets price track inflation while the capital base needed to support it is small.
- Caveat: inflation hurts *all* unleveraged businesses that need tangible assets ("and almost all do"); See's still had to ante up $8M just to stay even in real terms. "Businesses needing little in the way of tangible assets simply are hurt the least."

### 1.4 Implication: what to own in an inflationary world

- Rejects the "In Goods We Trust" traditional wisdom that asset-heavy businesses (natural resources, plants, machinery) are inflation hedges: "It doesn't work that way." Asset-heavy businesses "generally earn low rates of return — rates that often barely provide enough capital to fund the inflationary needs of the existing business, with nothing left over for real growth, for distribution to owners, or for acquisition of new businesses."
- Own instead businesses "that combined intangibles of lasting value with relatively minor requirements for tangible assets" — the great inflation-era fortunes came from these (communications businesses especially: little tangible investment, enduring franchises). Their nominal earnings "bounded upward" and were "largely available for the acquisition of additional businesses."
- "During inflation, Goodwill is the gift that keeps giving." But only *true economic* goodwill — not the spurious accounting kind.

### 1.5 Accounting treatment (amortization) and why to ignore it in valuation

- Rules: pre-November-1970 goodwill can stay on the balance sheet unamortized. Post-November-1970 goodwill must be amortized over not more than 40 years in equal annual charges; managements (including Berkshire) nearly always elect 40. The charge is **not tax-deductible**, so its after-tax effect "is roughly double that of most other expenses."
- Why the charges are not economic costs: See's economic goodwill rose while accounting goodwill fell; future economic goodwill "won't shrink in even decrements or anything remotely resembling them" — more likely it rises in current dollars because of inflation.
- The $100-per-share thought experiment: a company with $20/share tangible net worth earning $5/share (25%) via an internally developed franchise or FCC-granted TV stations might sell for $100/share. A buyer at $100 effectively pays $80/share for goodwill. Should he impute a $2/share annual amortization charge ($80/40 years) and treat "true" earnings as $3/share? The implied answer: no — which shows accounting goodwill is not "the best measure of economic reality."
- Two-perspective rule for intangibles:
  1. **Operating analysis** — amortization charges "should be ignored." The best guide to a business's economic attractiveness, and to the current value of its economic goodwill, is "What a business can be expected to earn on unleveraged net tangible assets, excluding any charges against earnings for amortization of Goodwill."
  2. **Acquisition analysis** — amortization charges "should be ignored also. They should be deducted neither from earnings nor from the cost of the business." Purchased goodwill is viewed "forever ... at its full cost, before any amortization," and cost should include "the full intrinsic business value — not just the recorded accounting value — of all consideration given" (e.g., Berkshire truly paid more than the $51.7M booked in the Blue Chip merger because Berkshire shares were worth more than market).
- "A good business is not always a good purchase — although it's a good place to look for one." Berkshire seeks businesses with excellent economics on (1) and reasonable returns on (2): "Accounting consequences will be totally ignored."
- Disclaimer he repeats: "we have no better accounting system to suggest. The problems to be dealt with are mind boggling and require arbitrary rules."

## Part 2 — 1986 Appendix: Purchase-Price Accounting Adjustments and the "Cash Flow" Fallacy

### 2.1 The Company O vs. Company N quiz

- Quiz: two abbreviated 1986 income statements; "Which business is the more valuable?" Both have revenues of $677,240K, identical historical COGS ($341,170K), identical S&A ($260,286K), identical other income ($4,135K), identical historical tax ($31,387K). Company O shows net income **$40,231K**; Company N shows **$28,605K**.
- Reveal: "Companies O and N are the same business — Scott Fetzer." O ("old") = what 1986 GAAP earnings would have been had Berkshire not bought it; N ("new") = Scott Fetzer's GAAP earnings as actually reported by Berkshire.
- "the two columns depict identical economics — i.e., the same sales, wages, taxes, etc. And both 'companies' generate the same amount of cash for owners. Only the accounting is different."
- Source of the gap: Berkshire paid **$315 million** for net assets carried at **$172.4 million** — a **$142.6 million premium** that GAAP requires be allocated via "purchase-price adjustments":
  - $37.3M to write inventory up to current value (there was a $22.9M LIFO reserve);
  - $68.0M added to fixed assets and $13.0M removed from deferred tax liabilities ($81.0M net);
  - residual **$24.3M to Goodwill** ("excess of cost over the fair value of net assets acquired").
  - Balance sheet effect: equity $172,377K (O) vs. $314,996K (N); inventories $77,489K vs. $114,764K; PP&E $80,967K vs. $148,960K; other assets incl. goodwill $9,836K vs. $34,210K; deferred taxes $17,052K vs. $4,075K.
- The written-up assets produce four extra non-cash charges in column N, totaling **$11.6 million**:
  1. $4,979,000 non-cash inventory costs (from 1986 inventory reductions; "apt to be small or non-existent in future years");
  2. $5,054,000 extra depreciation from the fixed-asset write-up (total depreciation $13,355K vs. $8,301K; ~12 more years);
  3. $595,000 goodwill amortization (39 more years, slightly larger since 1986 covered 98% of the year);
  4. $998,000 "deferred-tax acrobatics" (~12 more years).
- Crucially, "none of these newly-created accounting costs, totaling $11.6 million, are deductible for income tax purposes. The 'new' Scott Fetzer pays exactly the same tax as the 'old' Scott Fetzer would have."
- What it shows: the same economic engine can report very different GAAP earnings depending purely on who owns it and what they paid. If value is a multiple of GAAP earnings, Scott Fetzer would absurdly have been "worth considerably more the day before we bought it than it was worth the following day." So GAAP earnings are not the valuation input; a measure that is invariant to purchase accounting is needed — owner earnings.

### 2.2 The definition of owner earnings (verbatim)

> "If we think through these questions, we can gain some insights about what may be called 'owner earnings.' These represent (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges such as Company N's items (1) and (4) less ( c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume. (If the business requires additional working capital to maintain its competitive position and unit volume, the increment also should be included in ( c) . However, businesses following the LIFO inventory method usually do not require additional working capital if unit volume does not change.)"

Components:
- **(a)** reported earnings
- **(b)** depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges (e.g., Company N's items (1) non-cash inventory costs and (4) deferred-tax adjustment)
- **(c)** "the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume" — i.e., *maintenance* capex, not growth capex, averaged over time, not a single year.
- **Working-capital caveat**: any increment of working capital required to maintain competitive position and unit volume is also included in (c); LIFO businesses "usually do not require additional working capital if unit volume does not change."

Key properties:
- (c) "must be a guess — and one sometimes very difficult to make," so owner earnings "does not yield the deceptively precise figures provided by GAAP." Nonetheless it is "the relevant item for valuation purposes — both for investors in buying stocks and for managers in buying entire businesses." Keynes: "I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong."
- Owner earnings are identical for O and N "because the sum of (a) and (b) is the same in both columns O and N, and because ( c) is necessarily the same in both cases." Valuations are therefore identical, "just as common sense would tell you should be the case."
- For Scott Fetzer specifically: (c) "is very close to the 'old' company's (b) number of $8.3 million and much below the 'new' company's (b) number of $19.9 million." So owner earnings are best approximated by column O's reported earnings and are "considerably larger than the GAAP figures that we report."
- Summary rule for Berkshire's businesses: (b) **on a historical-cost basis** (excluding amortization of intangibles and other purchase-price adjustments) "is quite close in amount to ( c)." Not identical: at See's, capitalized expenditures exceed depreciation by **$500,000 to $1 million** per year "simply to hold our ground competitively." This is why Berkshire shows amortization and purchase-price adjustments separately in its segment table and treats the pre-adjustment business earnings as "much more closely approximating owner earnings than the GAAP figures."

### 2.3 Why "cash flow" (earnings + D&A) is misleading, and who uses it

- Wall Street "cash flow" numbers "routinely include (a) plus (b) — but do not subtract ( c) ." Investment-banker sales brochures do the same, implying "the business being offered is the commercial counterpart of the Pyramids — forever state-of-the-art, never needing to be replaced, improved or refurbished." If every U.S. corporation's sales brochure were believed, "governmental projections of national plant and equipment spending would have to be slashed by 90%."
- Where "cash flow" is a usable shorthand: real estate or other businesses with "huge initial outlays and only tiny outlays thereafter" — "A company whose only holding is a bridge or an extremely long-lived gas field."
- Where it is "meaningless": "manufacturing, retailing, extractive companies, and utilities because, for them, ( c) is always significant." Capex can be deferred in a given year, "But over a five- or ten-year period, they must make the investment — or the business decays."
- Who uses it and why: "we believe these numbers are frequently used by marketers of businesses and securities in attempts to justify the unjustifiable (and thereby to sell what should be the unsalable)." When (a) alone "looks by itself inadequate to service debt of a junk bond or justify a foolish stock price, how convenient it becomes for salesmen to focus on (a) + (b)."
- Consequence: anyone measuring debt-service capacity or equity value by (a)+(b) while ignoring (c) "is headed for certain trouble."

### 2.4 Why most businesses' (c) exceeds (b)

- Scott Fetzer was a "happy state of affairs" because (c) ≈ (b) on a historical basis; "calculations of this sort usually do not provide such pleasant news."
- "Most managers probably will acknowledge that they need to spend something more than (b) on their businesses over the longer term just to hold their ground in terms of both unit volume and competitive position." Depreciation is based on historical cost; replacing assets at current (inflated, and technologically upgraded) prices costs more, and merely holding competitive position requires ongoing improvement.
- "When this imperative exists — that is, when ( c) exceeds (b) — GAAP earnings overstate owner earnings. Frequently this overstatement is substantial."
- Example: the oil industry — "Had most major oil companies spent only (b) each year, they would have guaranteed their shrinkage in real terms."
- Even at See's, a low-capital business, capex runs $0.5–1.0M above depreciation annually just to hold ground.

### 2.5 GAAP vs. economic reality

- GAAP earnings are purchase-accounting-dependent: identical cash generation, identical taxes, two very different net income figures ($40.2M vs. $28.6M). The $11.6M of extra charges are not tax-deductible and are not an economic cost; they are an allocation of the purchase premium that will mostly amortize away until the two balance sheets converge (except higher land and most inventory values, which persist).
- GAAP's precision is "deceptively precise"; owner earnings require a judgment call on (c) but are the right target. "I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong."
- Division of labor: "the accountants' job is to record, not to evaluate. The evaluation job falls to investors and managers." Questioning GAAP "may seem impious" but is the investor's responsibility.
- Accounting numbers remain indispensable as the starting point ("Charlie and I would be lost without these numbers") but "accounting is but an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it."
- Practical application for Berkshire: show amortization and purchase-price adjustments as separate line items so readers can see business earnings before such charges, which better approximate owner earnings.

## Cross-appendix takeaways for the valuation framework

- 1983 and 1986 are two halves of one rule: strip out purchase-accounting artifacts (goodwill amortization, asset write-up depreciation, deferred-tax adjustments) from the *income statement* when judging a business, but keep the *full* purchase price (at intrinsic value of consideration) when judging the acquisition.
- Measure business quality as return on unleveraged net tangible assets before intangible amortization (1983); measure cash available to owners as owner earnings = (a) + (b) − (c), where (c) is maintenance capex plus required working-capital increments (1986).
- Prefer businesses with high returns on small tangible-asset bases: they need little (c), their economic goodwill compounds with inflation, and their reported "cash flow" is closest to true owner earnings. For asset-heavy businesses, (c) typically exceeds (b), so GAAP earnings and especially "cash flow" overstate what owners actually get.
