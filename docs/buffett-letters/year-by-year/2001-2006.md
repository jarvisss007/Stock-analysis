Source files: `letters/2001.md` ... `2006.md`. All six were read in full. Note on text quality: the 2001, 2002 and 2003 files have had most punctuation (commas, quotation marks, apostrophes in some contractions, dollar and percent signs) stripped by extraction; quotes from those years are reproduced as they appear in the file. The 2004-2006 files are clean. One caveat on the brief: the 2006 letter as supplied does NOT mention "Fortune's Formula" or the Kelly criterion (grep confirmed); its hedge-fund commentary is the "2-and-20" / Gotrocks-redux passage plus the Walter Schloss / efficient-market-theory section, both covered below.

## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 2001

**Context.** Berkshire's net worth fell $3.77 billion (book value per share down 6.2%), the first decline under present management; 37-year compound rate 22.6%. September 11 produced the industry's largest insured loss ever, and Berkshire's float cost came in at "a staggering 12.8%" because General Re had taken on terrorism exposure without charging for it. Acquisitions closed or agreed: Shaw and Johns Manville (agreed 2000), MiTek, XTRA, Larson-Juhl, and an offer for bankrupt Fruit of the Loom. The NASDAQ bubble had peaked March 10, 2000 ("The Great Bubble"), and Buffett says equity prices still "presage only moderate returns." He also admits his own equity-portfolio results "were poor just as they have been for several years."

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Relative performance is the right yardstick: from the 1956 Ground Rules, "Whether we do a good job or a poor job is to be measured against the general experience in securities." Small annual advantages over the S&P compound into large rewards for long-term investors, even if absolute returns are variable (the See's seasonal-business analogy).
- Managers should eat their own cooking: he and Munger take no cash compensation, restricted stock or options that would make their results differ from shareholders'; he keeps 99%+ of his net worth in Berkshire. He is "disgusted" by CEOs who urged investors to buy while dumping their own shares.
- Size is now the enemy: a $10 million idea that triples no longer moves the needle; "We need elephants to make significant gains now and they are hard to find." Future results will not approach the past record.
- Ownership versus options: at MiTek, 55 managers put up at least $100,000 each in cash (many borrowed) to buy 10% of the company. "They face the downside of decisions as well as the upside. They incur a cost of capital. And they can't reprice their stakes." This is contrasted with option holders.
- Entry strategy: businesses "with economic characteristics ranging from good to superb," run by "smart seasoned and trustworthy CEOs," bought for cash so existing owners give up nothing; shares issued "only grudgingly."
- Buy the manager with the brand: at Fruit of the Loom the key assets are "John and the brand"; the offer carried no financing conditions but insisted John Holland stay as CEO.
- The Horace quotation from the frontispiece of Security Analysis ("Many shall be restored that now are fallen and many shall fall that are now in honor") is offered as a lifelong lesson about the impermanence of business advantage (Acme Boot, Fruit of the Loom).
- How to value an insurer: three determinants, (1) amount of float, (2) its cost, (3) "most critical of all the long term outlook for both." Float is "money we hold but don't own." An insurer "has value if its cost of float over time is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds."
- With short rates under 2%, every insurance unit (except retroactive reinsurance) must earn an underwriting profit to be a good business; a 4% cost of float that was tolerable when bonds yielded 8% is no longer.
- Three underwriting principles: accept only risks you can evaluate "staying within their circle of competence" with an expectancy of profit after weighing remote scenarios, ignoring market share; limit aggregation so no single or correlated event threatens solvency; avoid moral risk ("trying to write good contracts with bad people doesn't work").
- Experience vs. exposure: pricing from past loss experience is dangerous when exposure has changed (terrorism; D&O late in a bull market, where the exposure-based rate "might well... be five or more times the premium dictated by experience").
- The Noah rule: "Predicting rain doesn't count building arks does." He had foreseen the terrorism risk but did not act.
- Long-term results come from avoiding dumb decisions: "Just as is the case in investing insurers produce outstanding long term results primarily by avoiding dumb decisions rather than by making brilliant ones."
- Not knowing your costs (under-reserving) is "dynamite" in long-tail reinsurance; it misprices the product, overpays incentive compensation and prepays taxes.
- "Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking." EBITDA and pro forma are devices to make you "unthinkingly accept concepts that are dangerously flawed." "Loss development" and "reserve strengthening" are euphemisms: "The losses didn't develop they were there all along."
- Insurance accounting is "a self graded exam"; discounting reserves would be a new tool for those inclined to fudge.
- Goodwill amortization ending under GAAP "will increase our reported earnings though not our true economic earnings."
- Junk bonds: Berkshire has "never purchased a newly issued junk bond"; distressed debt is a 50-year specialty but losses will occur; it is not suitable for the general public.
- "Debt is a four letter word around Berkshire."
- Stress-test every participant in a reinsurance chain under an unfavorable economic environment: "you only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out."

**Businesses discussed and why.**
- *MiTek* (truss connector plates): "our kind of business," a world leader, with an entrepreneurial management that bought in with its own money.
- *XTRA* (trailer leasing): cyclical but "should earn decent returns over time."
- *Larson-Juhl* (custom picture frames): U.S. leader serving 18,000 framing shops; owner was "straightforward... cared about who bought it and was realistic as to price."
- *Fruit of the Loom*: bankrupt from "too much debt and poor management"; John Holland and the brand are the assets. Berkshire bought the senior debt at ~50% of face first.
- *R.C. Willey*: closed-on-Sunday policy succeeding in Boise and Las Vegas against Buffett's skepticism; Bill Child's willingness to fund the Boise store personally.
- *GEICO*: paid $221 million underwriting profit to hold $4.25 billion of float; low costs "far below those of our major competitors," attractive prices, cost-free float; disappointment at flat policy count.
- *General Re*: broke all three underwriting rules; under-reserved by $800 million; overly competitive for volume. New CEO Joe Brandon.
- *Ajit Jain's reinsurance*: never broke a rule in 15 years; speed and financial strength are competitive advantages post-9/11; Berkshire retains its risks "and depend on no one."
- *FlightSafety*: 2002 capex of $162 million vs. depreciation of $95 million, a rebuke to EBITDA believers.
- *NetJets*: scale advantages (300+ planes, lower positioning cost) but under competitive conditions "at best modest profits."
- *Dexter*: three mistakes (see below).
- *Equities*: Coke, Amex, Gillette, Wells Fargo, Moody's, H&R Block, Washington Post. "We do not believe Berkshire's equity holdings as a group are undervalued."
- *FINOVA/Berkadia*: distressed-debt purchase that became a prepackaged bankruptcy financing with Leucadia.
- *Lou Simpson*: runs $2 billion at GEICO independently; "Lou's performance has been far better than mine."

**Mistakes admitted and lessons.**
- Letting General Re assume terrorism risk without premium: "I violated the Noah rule." Lesson: convert thought into action; price exposure, not experience.
- Dexter: "1 buying it in the first place 2 paying for it with stock and 3 procrastinating when the need for changes in its operations was obvious." He thought Dexter could cope with low-cost foreign competition and "was wrong."
- His equity results had been poor for several years.

**Verbatim quotes (2001).**
- "Whether we do a good job or a poor job is to be measured against the general experience in securities."
- "We need elephants to make significant gains now and they are hard to find."
- "An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds. But the business is a lemon if its cost of float is higher than market rates for money."
- "No matter what the rate trying to write good contracts with bad people doesn't work."
- "I violated the Noah rule Predicting rain doesn't count building arks does."
- "Just as is the case in investing insurers produce outstanding long term results primarily by avoiding dumb decisions rather than by making brilliant ones."
- "After all you only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out."
- "Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking. When companies or investment professionals use terms such as EBITDA and pro forma they want you to unthinkingly accept concepts that are dangerously flawed."
- "The losses didn't develop they were there all along. What developed was management's understanding of the losses or in the instances of chicanery management's willingness to finally fess up."
- "Those who believe that EBITDA is in any way equivalent to true earnings are welcome to pick up the tab."
- "Debt is a four letter word around Berkshire."
- "If winning however is equated with market share rather than profits trouble awaits. No must be an important part of any underwriter's vocabulary."

### 2002

**Context.** A "banner year": net worth up $6.1 billion (10.0%), beating the S&P by 32.1 points, which Buffett calls "aberrational" and partly due to no mega-cat and favorable bond-market conditions; on a true pro forma basis earnings would have been ~$500 million lower. Float reached $41.2 billion at a cost of only 1%. Acquisitions: Larson-Juhl and Fruit of the Loom closed; CTB, Garan, The Pampered Chef bought; MidAmerican bought the Kern River and Northern Natural pipelines (the latter, ironically, the Omaha company Ken Lay had turned into Enron). Junk-bond holdings sextupled to $8.3 billion. The letter contains the famous derivatives section ("financial weapons of mass destruction") and a long corporate-governance section written in the wake of Enron and the option-expensing fight.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Three legs of the entrance strategy: good-to-great economic characteristics, great managers, and "a sensible purchase price." "Unlike LBO operators and private equity firms we have no exit strategy we buy to keep."
- Honest pro forma: "except for" losses (mega-cats) "will forever be part of the insurance business"; Berkshire normalizes downward when it has been lucky.
- "To be a winner work with winners" (Eddie Bennett the batboy). Buffett's job is "to stay out of their way and allocate whatever excess capital their businesses generate."
- Falling interest rates have moved insurers with tolerable underwriting losses "deeply into the lemon category." "There's nothing automatic about cheap float."
- Three insurance requirements: underwrite with unwavering discipline, reserve conservatively, avoid aggregations that let "a supposedly impossible incident" threaten solvency. Size "simply doesn't count."
- He was "dead wrong" about Gen Re's culture at merger time; the WTC attack alone "would have threatened the company's existence" had it been independent.
- Most casualty managers tend to under-reserve; overcoming this takes a mindset that "has nothing to do with actuarial expertise."
- "Cheap reinsurance is a fool's bargain" (GEICO's $94 million loss on $72,000 of retained premium, 90% from deadbeat reinsurers). Deal only with the strongest.
- Lumpy vs. smooth: "we would rather earn a lumpy 15 over time than a smooth 12."
- Retroactive reinsurance mechanics explained: premium received up front is less than expected losses; the difference is a "deferred charges reinsurance assumed" asset amortized into intentional underwriting losses; worthwhile because payments are capped and the float is used for years.
- Derivatives: "time bombs"; mark-to-model becomes "mark to myth"; reinsurance and derivatives are "Like Hell... easy to enter and almost impossible to exit"; both produce overstated reported earnings because today's earnings rest on estimates whose inaccuracy may not surface for years; marking errors "have not been symmetrical"; collateral triggers on downgrades create a "spiral"; daisy-chain counterparty risk; no central bank protects derivatives dealers; LTCM in 1998 and total-return swaps that "make a joke of margin requirements." Conclusion: "financial weapons of mass destruction."
- Equity hurdle rate: "Unless however we see a very high probability of at least 10 pre tax returns which translate to 6 7 after corporate tax we will sit on the sidelines." "Occasionally successful investing requires inactivity."
- In stocks "we expect every commitment to work out well because we concentrate on conservatively financed businesses with strong competitive strengths run by able and honest people"; realized gains have exceeded losses ~100 to 1. Junk bonds are different: marginal, leveraged, low-return businesses with sometimes questionable managers, so occasional large losses are expected.
- Governance: directors should behave "as if there was a single absentee owner"; three director qualities, business savvy, interest, and shareholder orientation; "boardroom atmosphere" not inadequate law explains failure; outside directors should meet without the CEO; the mutual-fund case study of 62 years of "independent" directors who never fire a manager; "If you can't tell whose side someone is on they are not on yours."
- Berkshire directors will be chosen for "huge and true ownership interests" bought in the market, paid a pittance, with no D&O insurance, so their decisions are driven by "the effect their decisions will have on their family's net worth."
- "Audit committees can't audit." Four questions to put auditors on the spot, asked at least a week before earnings release; "Haste is the enemy of accuracy."
- Options are an expense; the 1993-94 Senate vote (88-9) castrating FASB "launched" a "new era of anything goes earnings reports."
- Three suggestions for investors: beware weak accounting ("There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen"), EBITDA is "particularly pernicious" (depreciation is a real, pre-paid expense), unintelligible footnotes mean untrustworthy management, and be suspicious of companies that trumpet earnings projections: "Managers that always promise to make the numbers will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers."

**Businesses discussed and why.**
- *The Pampered Chef*: founded with $3,000 borrowed against a life policy, "all the money ever injected into the company," now $700 million of sales through 67,000 consultants; proprietary products, enthusiastic sales force; ten seconds to decide on the managers.
- *CTB* and *Garan*: "industries characterized by tough economics" but with competitive strengths enabling "decent returns on capital."
- *MidAmerican / pipelines*: Kern River and Northern Natural will carry ~8% of U.S. gas; "Berkshire stands ready to inject massive amounts of money into MEHC."
- *HomeServices*: cyclical but liked; acquisition-driven growth.
- *General Re*: restored discipline; $1.31 billion reserve charge for prior years; now the only AAA-across-the-board giant reinsurer, which matters because reinsurance is a promise tested decades later.
- *GEICO*: saves policyholders ~$1 billion a year vs. average insurers; premiums grew from $2.9 billion (1996) to $6.9 billion; internet new business up 75%.
- *Ajit Jain*: $13.4 billion of float from a standing start in 1986 with 20 people, with underwriting profits.
- *Shaw*: productivity and expense control lifted earnings from $292 to $424 million on 1% price increases. "We cherish cost consciousness at Berkshire."
- *NetJets*: 75% industry share; still losing money in Europe; "airplanes are costly to operate," which over time favors the scale leader.
- *Gen Re Securities*: lost $173 million; being terminated; 14,384 contracts with 672 counterparties.
- *Equities*: comfortable with holdings because earnings rose while valuations fell, but "we don't yet believe their shares are undervalued."

**Mistakes admitted and lessons.**
- "I was dead wrong" about Gen Re's standards at the 1998 merger; should have detected slippage earlier. Lesson: cultures change; a disaster "of that scope was highly improbable of course but it is up to insurers to limit their risks in a manner that leaves their finances rock solid if the impossible happens."
- As a director, "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the interests of shareholders. In those cases collegiality trumped independence."
- California workers' comp reserving "severely missed the mark."

**Verbatim quotes (2002).**
- "Unlike LBO operators and private equity firms we have no exit strategy we buy to keep."
- "It's simple to be a winner work with winners."
- "Not knowing your costs will cause problems in any business" (2001) is echoed: "any insurer that has no idea what its costs are is heading for big trouble."
- "Cheap reinsurance is a fool's bargain."
- "At Berkshire we would rather earn a lumpy 15 over time than a smooth 12."
- "We view them as time bombs both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system."
- "In fact the reinsurance and derivatives businesses are similar Like Hell both are easy to enter and almost impossible to exit."
- "In extreme cases mark to model degenerates into what I would call mark to myth."
- "History teaches us that a crisis often causes problems to correlate in a manner undreamed of in more tranquil times."
- "In our view however derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction carrying dangers that while now latent are potentially lethal."
- "Unless however we see a very high probability of at least 10 pre tax returns which translate to 6 7 after corporate tax we will sit on the sidelines... But occasionally successful investing requires inactivity."
- "If you can't tell whose side someone is on they are not on yours."
- "There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen."
- "Managers that always promise to make the numbers will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers."
- "CEOs must embrace stewardship as a way of life and treat their owners as partners not patsies."

### 2003

**Context.** Net worth up $13.6 billion (21%), though the S&P returned 28.7%; 39-year rate 22.2%. Equities fell to ~50% of net worth (from 114% in the 1980s), so market moves affect Berkshire less. Acquisitions: Clayton Homes (sourced via University of Tennessee students and a book), McLane from Wal-Mart (a two-hour meeting, a handshake, "We did no due diligence"). Berkshire paid $3.3 billion of federal tax, about 2% of all corporate income tax. Junk bonds, bought at ~$8 billion in 2002, were "now decidedly unattractive." Berkshire entered foreign-currency contracts for the first time ($12 billion across five currencies), bearish on the dollar because of the trade deficit. The letter also discusses the mutual-fund scandals and Berkshire's new board.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Intrinsic value, not book, is what counts; book is "a slightly understated gauge" of long-run intrinsic growth at Berkshire. The S&P is available to anyone via a low-cost index fund, so "Unless we achieve gains in per share intrinsic value in the future that outdo the S P's performance Charlie and I will be adding nothing to what you can accomplish on your own."
- Freedom from institutional constraints (industry history, Wall Street pressure) means Berkshire's mistakes are "unforced errors."
- "When valuations are similar we strongly prefer owning businesses to owning stocks"; the tilt to equities in earlier decades was because stocks were cheaper.
- Asset size hurts performance: "Investment managers often profit far more from piling up assets than from handling those assets well. So when one tells you that increased funds won't hurt his investment performance step back His nose is about to grow."
- Acquisition criteria restated: "1 have favorable and enduring economic charac teristics 2 are run by talented and honest managers and 3 are available at a sensible price."
- Beware growth-rate presentations: base-year and terminal-year selection can make mediocre records look sensational (he shows Berkshire's earnings growth from multiple base years).
- Capital allocation menu: buy stocks aggressively if cheaper than whole businesses; load up on bonds when attractive (2002); buy qualifying businesses in any climate, "the bigger the better." Underutilized capital "is a painful condition to be in but not as painful as doing something stupid. I speak from experience."
- Lending discipline lesson from manufactured housing: volume came from "buyers who shouldn't have bought financed by lenders who shouldn't have lent"; securitization distanced the funder from the loan. A proper model needs "significant down payments and shorter term loans."
- Insurers sell "a non proprietary piece of paper containing a non proprietary promise"; no installed base, patents, real estate or resource position protects them; "The critical variables therefore are managerial brains discipline and integrity."
- A long-tail insurer "can report almost any earnings that the CEO desires" by misstating liabilities; auditors and actuaries have usually failed to catch it.
- View Berkshire "as an unfolding movie not as a still photograph."
- "Every tub on its own bottom": subsidiaries that lend must pay an appropriate rate for funds; "having a rich daddy can lead to sloppy decisions." Berkshire re-lends to Clayton at cost plus 1%.
- Holding receivables beats securitization economically even though GAAP front-ends securitization income; "We are in no hurry to record income."
- Derivatives disclosure is unknowable: "No matter how financially sophisticated you are you can't possibly learn from reading the disclosure documents of a derivatives intensive company what risks lurk in its positions." Darwin: "Ignorance more frequently begets confidence than does knowledge."
- Correlation of problems: a derivatives business needing capital would "commandeer the capital and credit of Berkshire at just the time we could otherwise deploy those resources to huge advantage" (1974 fraud example).
- Realized gains "are meaningless for analytical purposes."
- Macro forecasting: "the cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters"; yet the trade deficit is "force feeding huge amounts of claims on and ownership in America to the rest of the world."
- Default position is Treasuries; never reach for yield by dropping credit standards or extending maturities: "Charlie and I detest taking even small risks unless we feel we are being adequately compensated for doing so."
- Governance: honest negotiation requires equal intensity on both sides; CEO pay negotiations have "a play money quality"; "independence" defined by statute is "nonsense"; directors should be chosen from "high grade people whose interests are in line with those of rank and file shareholders and are in line in a very big way." "Owner capitalism." "After all who ever washes a rental car."
- Business savvy is the rarest director quality; without it, interest and shareholder orientation "are of little help."
- Low-cost index funds such as Vanguard's "are investor friendly by definition and are the best selection for most of those who wish to own equities."
- Mrs. B's wisdom: "If you have the lowest price customers will find you at the bottom of a river."

**Businesses discussed and why.**
- *Clayton Homes*: "the class act" of manufactured housing; behaved better than competitors in financing; needed Berkshire's balance sheet because securitization had dried up; offer based on Jim Clayton's book, Kevin Clayton, the public financials and the Oakwood lesson.
- *McLane*: $23 billion sales, ~1% pre-tax margin, "a good business but one not in the mainstream of Wal Mart's future"; retailers who shunned it as a Wal-Mart unit will return.
- *MidAmerican*: UK utilities, Iowa, pipelines (7.8% of U.S. gas), HomeServices; debt appropriate because diversified, stable regulated earnings; UK employee count fell from 6,800 to 2,539 after privatization ("profit motivated managers... will find efficiencies that government never will").
- *General Re*: "fixed"; now AAA along with National Indemnity, the only two among major reinsurers.
- *Ajit Jain*: PepsiCo $1 billion prize policy ($250 million present-value exposure).
- *GEICO*: premiums $2.2 billion to $8.1 billion under Tony Nicely; share 2.1% to 5.0%.
- *Fruit of the Loom*: "three major assets a 148 year old universally recognized brand a low cost manufacturing operation and John Holland."
- *Manufacturing/service/retail group*: 20.7% on tangible net worth, but only 9.2% on carrying value because of goodwill paid.
- *FlightSafety*: simulators cost $1.2 billion, triple the nearest competitor.
- *NetJets*: lost $41 million; Europe painful but necessary for first-tier status.
- *Equities*: "We own pieces of excellent businesses... but their current prices reflect their excellence."
- *Gen Re Securities*: 23,218 tickets with 884 counterparties at start of liquidation; still 7,580 two years later; losses $173 million and $99 million.

**Mistakes admitted and lessons.**
- Buying Oakwood Homes junk debt without understanding "how atrocious consumer financing practices had become."
- "I made a big mistake in not selling several of our larger holdings during The Great Bubble."
- Gen Re Securities: "I'm sure I could have saved you 100 million or so pre tax if I had acted more promptly... Charlie would have moved swiftly... I however dithered."
- Not spotting Gen Re's under-reserving, which also meant paying taxes early: "Aaarrrggghh."
- As a director with huge stakes he "remained silent as questionable proposals were rubber stamped."

**Verbatim quotes (2003).**
- "Unless we achieve gains in per share intrinsic value in the future that outdo the S P's performance Charlie and I will be adding nothing to what you can accomplish on your own."
- "When valuations are similar we strongly prefer owning businesses to owning stocks."
- "So when one tells you that increased funds won't hurt his investment performance step back His nose is about to grow."
- "It's a painful condition to be in but not as painful as doing something stupid. I speak from experience."
- "Insurers sell a non proprietary piece of paper containing a non proprietary promise... The critical variables therefore are managerial brains discipline and integrity."
- "When analyzing Berkshire be sure to remember that the company should be viewed as an unfolding movie not as a still photograph."
- "Otherwise having a rich daddy can lead to sloppy decisions."
- "Yesterday's weeds are today being priced as flowers."
- "I should note that the cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters."
- "Charlie and I detest taking even small risks unless we feel we are being adequately compensated for doing so."
- "After all who ever washes a rental car."
- "The bottom line for our directors You win they win big you lose they lose big. Our approach might be called owner capitalism."
- "Many people who are smart articulate and admired have no real understanding of business. That's no sin they may shine elsewhere. But they don't belong on corporate boards."

### 2004

**Context.** Net worth up $8.3 billion (10.5%), slightly behind the S&P's 10.9%; 40-year rate 21.9%. Buffett "struck out" on multi-billion acquisitions and found few attractive securities, ending with $43 billion of cash. Four Florida hurricanes cost $1.25 billion but float was still better than costless ($1.55 billion underwriting profit on $45 billion average float). Clayton bought Oakwood; Berkshire's loans to Clayton reached $7.35 billion. Foreign-exchange contracts grew to $21.4 billion across 12 currencies. The letter includes the long-term investor-returns discussion, the National Indemnity "disciplined underwriter" table, the GEICO/State Farm history, Lou Simpson's record, and a long trade-deficit essay.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Managers' instruction: "Run your business as if it were the only asset your family will own over the next hundred years."
- Investor returns: over 35 years the S&P averaged 11.2% including dividends ("well above what we expect future returns to be"), yet only one year before 2004 was between 8% and 14%; "last year's 'normal' return is anything but." American business did terrifically; an untouched index fund would have captured it. Investors failed for three reasons: high costs (excess trading, management fees), decisions "based on tips and fads rather than on thoughtful, quantified evaluation of businesses," and start-and-stop timing. "Investors should remember that excitement and expenses are their enemies. And if they insist on trying to time their participation in equities, they should try to be fearful when others are greedy and greedy only when others are fearful."
- Stay with simple propositions: one key variable at 90% gives 90%; ten independent 90% variables give 35%. "Since a chain is no stronger than its weakest link, it makes sense to look for... mono-linked chains" (the zinc write-off).
- Insurance is a commodity: "Customers by the millions say 'I need some Gillette blades' or 'I'll have a Coke' but we wait in vain for 'I'd like a National Indemnity policy, please.'" Price competition is fierce, "Think airline seats."
- Two ways to beat commodity economics: (a) NICO's managerial mindset, shrinking volume for 14 years rather than cut price ("We never left customers – but they left us"); (b) GEICO's low-cost position pursued with an "unrelenting foot-to-the-floor strategy."
- The institutional imperative "rejects extended decreases in volume"; in insurance the urge to write is worse because mispricing shows up late, and employees rationalize bad pricing to avoid layoffs. NICO's no-layoff promise removes that incentive; the Tom Murphy "$3 million decision" story on hiring.
- "An insurance organization that doesn't care deeply about underwriting at a profit this year is unlikely to care next year either."
- "Extraordinary business ability is largely innate" (only one of NICO's four CEOs graduated college).
- Reinsurance should not be a commodity because "any insurance policy is simply a promise, and... promises vary enormously in their quality." Berkshire plans for a $100 billion industry event (its share 3-5%) and "Berkshire's checks will clear."
- Derivatives: "The real test of the earning power of a derivatives operation is what it achieves after operating for an extended period in a no-growth mode. You only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out." Rapid growth in financial institutions "sometimes masks major underlying problems (and occasionally fraud)."
- Capital intensity: FlightSafety needs $3.50 of capital per $1 of annual revenue, so "utilization rates are all-important"; the manufacturing/service/retail group earns 21.7% on tangible equity with minor leverage but only 9.9% on carrying value because of goodwill paid.
- Stocks as businesses: "Charlie and I ignore such distractions and instead view our holdings as fractional ownerships in businesses... this thinking has been the cornerstone of my investment behavior since I was 19" when he read The Intelligent Investor and "the scales fell from my eyes."
- Big Four (Amex, Coke, Gillette, Wells): $3.83 billion cost, weighted purchase date July 1992, earnings now 31.3% of cost and dividends 11.3% of cost; "satisfactory, though far from spectacular." Over time valuation gains have slightly exceeded earnings growth; year to year, business and market diverge "sometimes to an extraordinary degree."
- Investors look through a fogged windshield, not a clean rear-view mirror; he "talked when I should have walked" during the Bubble.
- Will buy stock or whole businesses "only when purchases can be made at prices that offer us the prospect of a reasonable return on our investment."
- Delegation: "When Charlie and I assign responsibility, we truly hand over the baton"; he learns of Lou Simpson's trades ten days after month-end.
- Trade deficit vs. budget deficit are not twins: a current-account deficit transfers ownership of America abroad (a "Sharecropper's Society"); Adam Smith's dictum concerns product-for-product trade, not wealth-for-product.
- Manage as 100% owner even at reputational risk, citing Keynes: "it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally."
- Three governance questions that count: right CEO? overreaching pay? acquisitions creating or destroying per-share value? Independence is calibrated by Matthew 6:21, "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also"; the least independent directors are those dependent on fees. Two reforms he should have adopted earlier: directors meeting without the CEO, and a whistleblower line.
- Options: non-expensing is "the most important accounting mechanism still available to CEOs who wish to overstate earnings"; properly structured options need an appropriate strike, escalation reflecting retained earnings, and a ban on quick disposal.
- Life-settlement accounting: GAAP charges "losses" on purchase that are not economic losses.

**Businesses discussed and why.**
- *National Indemnity*: no informational advantage, not low cost, outdated distribution, yet a star for 38 years because of underwriting mindset; without it "Berkshire would be lucky to be worth half of what it is today." Don Wurster "will accept a walk rather than swing at a bad pitch."
- *GEICO*: Leo Goodwin's insight to eliminate the agent because auto insurance is mandatory and most consumers know what they need; low-cost producer; near-death from under-reserving in the 1970s, rescue by Jack Byrne, stunted by diversification, revived under Tony Nicely; 6% share expected; New Jersey re-entry.
- *General Re*: Joe Brandon restored underwriting culture; prior-year development still hurting.
- *MidAmerican*: zinc project write-off ($579 million) as a lesson in complexity; HomeServices growing by acquisition.

### 2005

## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 2001-2006

### 1. What investing IS

- A stock is a fractional ownership of a business, nothing else. "Charlie and I ignore such distractions and instead view our holdings as fractional ownerships in businesses... the cornerstone of my investment behavior since I was 19" after reading The Intelligent Investor (2004). The same logic applies to the index: owners in aggregate "can earn between now and Judgment Day" only what their businesses earn; trading just rearranges who owns what (2005).
- Investing and junk-bond buying share one discipline: "make a price value calculation and also... scan hundreds of securities to find the very few that have attractive reward risk ratios" (2002).
- The job is to estimate intrinsic value, which is "all-important" yet "necessarily imprecise and often seriously wrong," and more so the more uncertain the business (2005). Book value is only "a slightly understated gauge" of Berkshire's intrinsic growth (2003, 2004).
- Time horizon: weighted holding period of the Big Four was 12.5 years (2004); positions in Coke, Amex, Gillette, Post, Moody's untouched since 1994/1998/1989/1973/2000, "Brokers don't love us" (2003); "we buy to keep" (2002, 2005). Managers should run businesses "as if it were the only asset your family will own over the next hundred years" (2004).
- The benchmark is relative: beating the S&P modestly over time is the only justification for Berkshire's existence, since anyone can buy the index cheaply (2001, 2003, 2004).
- Avoiding dumb decisions matters more than brilliance (2001); "a long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero" (2005); "A single, big mistake could wipe out a long string of successes" (2006). Risk is defined as "permanent loss of capital" (2006, on Schloss).
- Performance scales badly with size: "We need elephants" (2001, 2006); managers whose assets grow and claim no effect, "His nose is about to grow" (2003).

### 2. What to look for in a business

- The three (later six) criteria: "favorable and enduring economic characteristics," "talented and honest managers," "a sensible price" (2003; restated 2002, 2005).
- Commodity businesses can only win via a disciplined mindset (National Indemnity's shrinking-volume model) or a durable low-cost position pursued relentlessly (GEICO) (2004). GEICO's formula: low costs allow low prices that produce "steady gains in profitable business" (2005); productivity gains fund ever-larger advertising while keeping the low-cost title (2005, 2006).
- Pricing power and its loss: newspapers once had unleashed pricing power "in both advertising and circulation" after competition vanished; when fundamentals erode, "eroding fundamentals will overwhelm managerial brilliance" (2006). Shaw and building products could pass through petrochemical cost spikes only with a lag, but held up because of "strong business franchises" (2004, 2005).
- Return on capital and capital intensity: the manufacturing/service/retail group earned 20.7%, 21.7%, 22.2%, 25% on tangible net worth with minor leverage, but only ~9-11% on carrying value because of goodwill paid (2003-2006). FlightSafety's $3.50 of capital per $1 of revenue means utilization is everything (2004). See's grew profits more than tenfold in a no-growth industry (2005). Regulated utilities offer "fair returns" on large sums, not outsized ones (2005).
- The moat is widened or narrowed daily through customer delight, cost elimination and product improvement; long term beats short term when they conflict (2005). Businesses that make their customers more money (ISCAR) have "no better recipe" (2006).
- Brand and manager as the key assets: Fruit of the Loom's 148-year brand, low-cost manufacturing, and John Holland (2003); Clayton, Forest River, Business Wire, TTI all bought largely on the quality of the owner-operator.
- Simplicity: "stay with simple propositions"; a decision hinging on ten 90% variables succeeds 35% of the time (2004).
- Good business first: "If you want to get a reputation as a good businessman, be sure to get into a good business" (2006). Horace: "Many shall be restored that now are fallen and many shall fall that are now in honor" (2001).
- Growth that destroys value: Gillette's Duracell deal gave up more business value than it received (2005); manufactured-housing volume in the 1990s "was a fool's paradise" of bad loans (2003, 2004); Fred Futile's buybacks and 5%-return projects enrich the option holder, not owners (2005).

### 3. What to look for in management

- Honesty and candor: Buffett reports a pro forma that lowers earnings (2002); writes retroactive accounting out in debits and credits because "we go away suspicious" when footnotes baffle (2006); "unintelligible footnotes usually indicate untrustworthy management" (2002).
- Owner orientation: MiTek managers who bought with borrowed money face downside and cost of capital (2001); Bridges run Ben Bridge "exactly as if it were their own" (2004); Pete Liegl walked away from an LBO owner who told him how to run things (2005).
- Capital allocation as the CEO's defining skill: Buffett's job is to "allocate whatever excess capital their businesses generate" (2002) and "make major capital-allocation decisions" (2006); Jim Kilts reversed Gillette's "capital-allocation blunders" (2005).
- The institutional imperative "rejects extended decreases in volume" (2004); Gen Re's "urge to prevail over competitors" led to mispricing (2001); NICO's no-layoff promise removes the incentive to write bad business (2004).
- Cost consciousness: the widow's "Fred Brown died golf clubs for sale" (2002); Business Wire "No secretaries or management layers" (2005); Jack Ringwalt hunting for a parking meter with time left (2006); headquarters of 19 people (2006).
- Ability is "largely innate" (2004); "It's hard to teach a new dog old tricks" (2002); no subsidiary CEO has ever left voluntarily in 38-42 years.
- Compensation: tie incentives only to results the CEO controls; "no lottery tickets" (2006); options must be expensed and should have strikes rising with retained earnings and holding requirements (2004, 2005); comp committees are "slaves to comparative data" and consultants "Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo" (2005, 2006); "it's difficult to overpay the truly extraordinary CEO... But this species is rare" (2005).
- Directors: business savvy, interest, shareholder orientation, and real ownership bought in the market; fees and D&O insurance corrode independence (2002, 2003, 2004, 2006). The three questions that matter: right CEO, overreaching pay, value-creating acquisitions (2004). Suspect CEOs who promise and always hit targets (2002).
- Delegation is total: "we truly hand over the baton" (2004); "our only function since has been to stay out of the way" (2005).

### 4. Valuation

- Price vs. value is the first calculation in any security (2002). A high probability of at least 10% pre-tax (6-7% after corporate tax) is the floor; otherwise sit on the sidelines even when cash earns under 1% (2002).
- Intrinsic value estimates are imprecise and scale with business uncertainty (2005). Berkshire gives per-share investments and per-share operating earnings as the two inputs (2005, 2006), and warns that Berkshire may be worth more or less than the sum of parts depending on whether holding-company capital allocation adds value.
- Market vs. business performance diverge sharply year to year (Bubble and aftermath) but converge over time; Big Four valuation gains "somewhat exceeded earnings growth because price/earnings ratios have increased" (2004).
- Expected returns: investees at 6-8% earnings growth with prices matching should "double in value in ten years" (2005); the 20th century Dow compounded only 5.3% (2005); 11.2% S&P returns over 35 years are "well above what we expect future returns to be" (2004); "today's equity prices presage only moderate returns" (2001).
- "Their current prices reflect their excellence," so excellent businesses are not automatically buys (2003); "Yesterday's weeds are today being priced as flowers" on junk bonds (2003).
- Acquisition yardstick: compare business value received with business value given up; issuing stock sells part of everything you own (2005).
- Growth-rate claims depend on chosen endpoints (2003, 2005).
- Interest rates set the hurdle for float: a 4% cost was fine with 8% bonds; with sub-2% rates, underwriting profit is required (2001, 2002).
- Berkshire itself should trade "in a zone of reasonableness," at neither a large discount nor premium to intrinsic value (2006).

### 5. Temperament and psychology

- "Be fearful when others are greedy and greedy only when others are fearful" (2004, repeated for reinsurance pricing 2006).
- Inactivity: "occasionally successful investing requires inactivity" (2002); idle capital is painful "but not as painful as doing something stupid" (2003); $43 billion of cash is "not a happy position" but he will only buy at prices offering a reasonable return (2004).
- "Excitement and expenses are their enemies" (2004); "returns decrease as motion increases" (2005).
- Accept lumpiness: "a lumpy 15 over time than a smooth 12" (2002); Ajit will have "an occasional horrible year" (2003).
- Independence over reputation: Keynes on failing conventionally (2004); lemmings are never individually criticized (2004); he would rather be publicly wrong on currencies than privately wrong in dollars.
- Act promptly: dithering/"thumb-sucking" on Gen Re Securities cost hundreds of millions; "the time to act is now" (2005). The Noah rule (2001).
- Circle of competence appears explicitly in the first underwriting rule (2001) and implicitly in "stay with simple propositions" (2004) and in Buffett's admission he would be "a potted plant" on a medical board (2003).
- Concentration: Big Four plus a few others; Buffett keeps 99% of net worth in Berkshire; but Schloss' 1,000-stock record is praised as proof that cheapness, not concentration, was his edge (2006).
- Temperament for a successor: "Independent thinking, emotional stability, and a keen understanding of both human and institutional behavior" (2006).
- Mr. Market is not named in these years, but markets "will do extraordinary, even bizarre, things" (2006) and the Great Bubble "hangover may prove to be proportional to the binge" (2002).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons

- EBITDA: depreciation is a real expense paid up front; "Those who believe that EBITDA is in any way equivalent to true earnings are welcome to pick up the tab" (2001); the ten-years-of-wages-prepaid analogy (2002).
- Pro forma: "except for" is "deceptive nonsense" in insurance (2002).
- Loss reserves are a liability estimate, not a rainy-day fund; "loss development" and "reserve strengthening" are euphemisms; reserving is "a self graded exam"; surprises are asymmetric (10 bad to 1 good); do not discount reserves (2001, 2003, 2005). Under-reserving overstates profits, misprices product, overpays bonuses and prepays taxes.
- Goodwill: amortization ended in 2002 raising reported but not economic earnings (2001, 2002); goodwill paid is why return on carrying value is ~10% while return on tangible equity is 20-25% (2003-2006).
- Stock options are an expense; the 1993-94 Senate/FASB saga; 498 of 500 S&P companies chose non-expensing (2002); non-expensing is the biggest remaining earnings-overstatement tool (2004); fixed-price options are "free capital" (2005).
- Derivatives: mark-to-model/mark-to-myth; earnings "wildly overstated"; marks always lean toward higher bonuses (2002, 2004, 2005); even GAAP-compliant conservative marks produced $409 million of unwinding losses (2006).
- Mark-to-market asymmetry: currency contracts hit earnings daily, long-held stocks do not until sold (2004, 2005); realized gains are "meaningless for analytical purposes" (2003, 2004); the P&G/Gillette $5 billion gain is a non-event (2005).
- Securitization front-ends income; holding receivables with debt shows more debt and lower reported earnings but makes more money, "better profits to better cosmetics" (2003, 2005).
- Retroactive reinsurance: premium less than expected losses, DCRA asset amortized into intentional underwriting losses; the full journal entries (2002, 2006).
- Life-settlement purchases book GAAP "losses" that are not economic (2004).
- Pension assumptions that are "fanciful" are a red flag (2002); pension plans overstating returns is self-serving (2006).
- Consolidation rules can distort: MidAmerican one-line accounting under PUHCA; Value Capital's $20 billion of non-recourse liabilities (2003, 2004).
- Audit committees "can't audit"; four questions for auditors (2002).
- Chosen base years distort growth rates (2003, 2005).

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level

- Buy for cash; issue shares "only grudgingly" (2001); none of the 2005 deals used stock; stock deals have on balance cost shareholders (2005). Acquisitions must deliver business value equal to what is given up (2005).
- Hierarchy: operating businesses when valuations are similar; stocks when much cheaper; bonds when they become attractive (2003); tuck-ins leveraging existing managers (2006).
- Retained earnings are all reinvested; no dividends; MidAmerican likewise retains all earnings, rare for a utility (2005). Policy on dividends, repurchases and issuance will follow "What action will be likely to deliver the best result for shareholders over time?" (2006).
- Buybacks can enrich option holders without improving the business (Fred Futile, 2005), so they are not inherently virtuous.
- Debt: "a four letter word" (2001); only repos, borrowing against understood receivables, and non-recourse utility debt (2005). Every subsidiary pays its own cost of funds plus a markup (Clayton, 2003-2006). Berkshire must be "a fortress of financial strength" because of its promises (2002, 2005).
- Decentralization: 19 people at headquarters; subsidiaries keep their own cultures and philanthropy; "It's not our style to tamper with successful business cultures" (2001).
- Shareholder-designated contributions ended in 2003 because boycotts hurt Pampered Chef consultants (2003).

### 8. Insurance and float

- Float definition and sources (premiums paid up front; long-tail losses) repeated every year; grew from $20 million (1967) to $50.9 billion (2006) plus $7 billion from Equitas.
- Valuation of an insurer: amount of float, its cost, and the long-term outlook for both (2001, 2002). Cost of float: 12.8% (2001), 1% (2002), negative in 2003, 2004, 2005 (despite $3.4 billion of hurricane losses), 2006. Berkshire had an underwriting profit in roughly half its years.
- Discipline over volume: NICO's 14-year shrink (2004); "No must be an important part of any underwriter's vocabulary" (2001); size "simply doesn't count" (2002); Don Wurster takes the walk (2004).
- Three rules of underwriting (2001): evaluable risks with expected profit, limit aggregation and search for hidden correlation, avoid moral hazard. Gen Re broke all three (2001, 2002).
- Price exposure not experience (terrorism, D&O) (2001); use Pascal's wager under climate uncertainty (2005); "it would be a huge mistake to bet that evolving atmospheric changes are benign" (2006).
- Reserving: GEICO nearly died from under-reserving in the 1970s (2001, 2004); Gen Re's $800 million and $1.31 billion charges (2001, 2002); "any insurer that has no idea what its costs are is heading for big trouble" (2002).
- Counterparty strength: cheap reinsurance is "a fool's bargain"; GEICO's $94 million loss on $72,000 of net premium (2002); Berkshire retains its risks and depends on no one (2001, 2003); Gen Re and NICO the only AAA major reinsurers (2003).
- Competitive advantage: tolerance for $2-6 billion single-event losses, liquidity, non-insurance earnings, patient shareholders, speed (Ajit) (2001, 2006); prepared for a $100 billion industry event (2004).
- Retroactive reinsurance as a float engine with intentional underwriting losses (2002, 2006); Equitas as the largest example.
- GEICO as the model low-cost producer: ad spend from $31 million to $631 million; employees down while policies up 42% (2005, 2006).

### 9. Mistakes and changes in thinking

- Dexter (2001): bought a business facing brutal foreign competition, paid with stock, procrastinated on fixes.
- General Re (2001-2003): assumed culture had not changed; under-reserving; terrorism aggregation; "dead wrong."
- Gen Re Securities (2002-2006): knew at purchase it was dangerous, tried to sell instead of shut, "dithered," $409 million cumulative loss; became the standing example of acting late.
- Not selling during the Bubble (2003, 2004): "I talked when I should have walked."
- Oakwood junk debt without understanding industry financing (2003).
- Zinc project: too many variables (2004).
- Stock-financed acquisitions cost shareholders (2005).
- Board silence out of collegiality (2002, 2003); late adoption of executive sessions and whistleblower line (2004).
- NetJets profit forecasts wrong (2005); Europe costlier than expected (2004).
- Blue Chip Stamps (2006) as a lifelong joke on trend-spotting.
- Evolution over the period: (a) decisive shift from equities (114% of net worth in the 1980s) to whole businesses and cash, driven by size and valuations; (b) first-ever currency positions (2002) then migration to foreign equities when carry turned negative (2005, 2006); (c) first non-U.S. acquisition (ISCAR, 2006); (d) selective writing of derivatives despite the WMD warning, because they are "sometimes wildly mispriced" (2006); (e) explicit succession planning for both CEO and CIO (2003, 2005, 2006); (f) growing emphasis on frictional costs and index funds for most investors (2003, 2004, 2005, 2006).

### 11. Macro and market views

- Interest rates: low rates turned tolerable underwriting losses into lemons and forced an underwriting-profit standard (2001, 2002); "pathetically low" rates on cash dragged 2003 earnings; rate differentials later made direct currency holdings a negative carry (2005, 2006).
- Market outlook: The Great Bubble ended March 10, 2000; "The market outperformed business for a very long period and that phenomenon had to end" (2001); three years of declines still left few bargains (2002); 2004's 10.9% "normal" return is historically rare; future S&P returns below the 11.2% 35-year average (2004); investees to grow 6-8% (2005).
- Forecasting: "the cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters" (2003); "macro-economics is a tough game in which few people, Charlie and I included, have demonstrated skill" (2004); unease that many pundits agree on dollar weakness (2004).
- Trade deficit: force-feeding U.S. assets abroad; "Sharecropper's Society"; investment-income account turned negative in 2006 as predicted; "reverse compounding"; a "soft landing" is "wishful thinking"; still, Americans will live better in 10-20 years (2003-2006).
- Derivatives and leverage: systemic "time bombs," concentration among few dealers, collateral spirals, LTCM, total-return swaps defeating margin rules, no central bank for derivatives (2002); outstanding notional "a multiple of what existed in 1998"; levees-before-Katrina warning (2005).
- Housing credit: teaser rates and optional payments; "A rolling loan gathers no loss" (2006); manufactured-housing securitization excess (2003, 2004).
- Climate and catastrophe: Katrina not a worst case; capital inflows depress super-cat prices, so Berkshire cut exposure (2006).
- Taxes: Berkshire paid 2% of all U.S. corporate income tax (2003) and $4.4 billion (2006); corporate share of federal receipts fell from 32% (1952) to 7.4% (2003); "If class warfare is being waged in America my class is clearly winning" (2003).
- Frictional costs: 20% of corporate earnings go to Helpers; 2-and-20 math; investors would be better off sitting still (2005, 2006).
- Market efficiency: Schloss' record refutes EMT; business schools "closed their eyes" (2006).
