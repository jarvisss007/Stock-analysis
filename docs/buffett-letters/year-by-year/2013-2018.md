## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 2013 letter (dated February 28, 2014)

**Context.** Net worth rose $34.2 billion; per-share book value +18.2% vs. the S&P 500's +32.4% (Berkshire has lagged in 10 of 49 years, all but one when the S&P gained more than 15%). Big moves: ~$18 billion spent to buy all of NV Energy (via MidAmerican, $5.6B) and a major stake in H.J. Heinz with 3G Capital ($8B of 9% preferred plus $4.25B of common each); $3.5B to buy out the minority of Marmon and Iscar; 25 bolt-on acquisitions costing $3.1B; a record $11B of capex (twice depreciation). The "Powerhouse Five" (MidAmerican, BNSF, Iscar, Lubrizol, Marmon) earned a record $10.8B pre-tax; insurance had its 11th straight underwriting profit and float reached $77B. Todd Combs and Ted Weschler each beat the S&P and now run $7B+ apiece. The letter contains the famous "Some Thoughts About Investing" section (the Nebraska farm and the NYU retail property) and the 90/10 index-fund instruction in his will.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Book value is "a rough tracking indicator"; what counts is per-share intrinsic value. Repurchases at 120% of book make sense only because intrinsic value "exceeds that percentage of book value by a meaningful amount." He did not buy in 2013 because the price never fell to that level: "If it does, we will be aggressive."
- The Berkshire growth recipe is five-fold: improve subsidiaries' basic earning power; bolt-on acquisitions; growth of investees; repurchase shares at a meaningful discount to intrinsic value; occasional large acquisition; and "rarely, if ever, issuing Berkshire shares."
- He would rather own a non-controlling piece of a wonderful business than 100% of a so-so one (the Hope-diamond-vs-rhinestone line). Flexibility to invest passively "doubles our chances of finding sensible uses for our endless gusher of cash."
- Retained earnings of investees are "every bit as valuable to us as the portion Berkshire records," especially when used for buybacks that raise Berkshire's ownership (each 0.1% of the Big Four is worth $50 million of annual earnings to Berkshire).
- Interest coverage should be measured as pre-tax earnings/interest, "not EBITDA/interest, a commonly-used measure we view as seriously flawed."
- Amortization vs. depreciation: amortization of purchase-accounting intangibles like customer relationships is "clearly not real"; amortization of software is real; "Every dime of depreciation expense we report, however, is a real cost." About 20% of Berkshire's $648M amortization charge was "real."
- Businesses are ranked by "earnings on unleveraged net tangible assets": terrific economics = 25% after-tax to well over 100%; good = 12-20%. The MSR group earned 16.7% after-tax on $25B of net tangible assets with excess cash and little leverage.
- "A business with terrific economics can be a bad investment if the purchase price is excessive."
- His own blunders were not the result of being misled: "I simply was wrong in my evaluation of the economic dynamics of the company or the industry in which it operated."
- Four insurance disciplines: understand all exposures; conservatively assess probability and cost; set a premium that on average delivers a profit; and "be willing to walk away if the appropriate premium can't be obtained." Most insurers "flunk the fourth."
- Float should be viewed as a revolving fund, not a liability payable tomorrow; insurance goodwill's cost "has no bearing on its true value" and should be written to zero if underwriting is chronically bad. GEICO's true economic goodwill ~$20B vs. $1.4B carried.
- The farm/NYU lessons: focus on what the asset will produce, not on daily price; if you can't roughly estimate future earnings, move on; focusing on expected price change is speculation; macro opinions are "a waste of time" and "dangerous"; "A climate of fear is your friend when investing; a euphoric world is your enemy."
- The actual decision process: "We first have to decide whether we can sensibly estimate an earnings range for five years out, or more. If the answer is yes, we will buy the stock (or business) if it sells at a reasonable price in relation to the bottom boundary of our estimate." In 54 years they never passed on an attractive purchase because of the macro or political environment.
- Circle of competence: "It's vital, however, that we recognize the perimeter of our 'circle of competence' and stay well inside of it."
- For non-professionals: own a cross-section of American business via a low-cost S&P 500 index fund, accumulate over a long period, never sell on bad news; the "know-nothing" investor who diversifies and keeps costs minimal will beat the professional "who is blind to even a single weakness." His will: 10% short-term governments, 90% Vanguard S&P 500 fund.
- Ben Graham's The Intelligent Investor (bought 1949), Chapters 8 and 20, "guide my investing decisions today"; "Price is what you pay, value is what you get."
- Public pension promises are a "gigantic financial tapeworm"; his 1975 memo to Katharine Graham is reproduced.

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**
- *Heinz/3G*: a partnership template; Berkshire as financing partner with 9% preferred expected to earn ~12%; "Berkshire never intends to sell a share." Unlike private equity.
- *NV Energy / MidAmerican*: recession-resistant essential service, diversity of earnings streams shields from any single regulator, retains all earnings (more dollars retained than any U.S. utility), lower cost of debt; top customer-satisfaction ranking helps win regulators.
- *BNSF*: carries ~15% of inter-city ton-miles, 500 miles per gallon per ton; interest coverage 9:1; $4B capex, double depreciation.
- *GEICO*: "huge cost advantage"; "Its low costs create a moat – an enduring one – that competitors are unable to cross"; #7 in 1996, now #2.
- *Ajit Jain / BH Reinsurance*: float $37B from a standing start in 1985; a $250B industry mega-cat loss would still leave Berkshire profitable for the year.
- *General Re*: early problems made him briefly think he'd made a huge mistake; "General Re is now a gem."
- *Lloyd's 2007 run-off deal*: "insurance is the sale of promises"; only Berkshire's promise is good enough for the largest long-tail cessions.
- *Big Four (AmEx, Coke, IBM, Wells Fargo)*: "excellent businesses... run by managers who are both talented and shareholder-oriented"; $4.4B look-through earnings vs. $1.4B dividends.
- *Bank of America warrant*: effectively the fifth-largest equity position.
- *Nebraska Furniture Mart / Mrs. B*: bought on a 1.25-page proposal with unaudited financials because "her word was good enough for me."
- *Clayton, CORT, XTRA, Berkadia*: Clayton 4.7% of U.S. single-family homes built.
- *Energy Future Holdings bonds*: see mistakes.

**Mistakes admitted and the lesson.** Energy Future Holdings: bought ~$2B of LBO debt "pursuant to a decision I made without consulting with Charlie. That was a big mistake," a pre-tax loss of $873M; "Next time I'll call Charlie." The MSR group has "a few" businesses with very poor returns because he misjudged economic dynamics; "I have not, however, made my last mistake in purchasing either businesses or stocks."

**Verbatim quotes (2013).**
1. "At Berkshire, we much prefer owning a non-controlling but substantial portion of a wonderful company to owning 100% of a so-so business; it's better to have a partial interest in the Hope diamond than to own all of a rhinestone." (2013)
2. "When Wall Streeters tout EBITDA as a valuation guide, button your wallet." (2013)
3. "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike." — Ben Graham, quoted as the section epigraph (2013)
4. "You don't need to be an expert in order to achieve satisfactory investment returns. But if you aren't, you must recognize your limitations and follow a course certain to work reasonably well. Keep things simple and don't swing for the fences. When promised quick profits, respond with a quick 'no.'" (2013)
5. "Focus on the future productivity of the asset you are considering. If you don't feel comfortable making a rough estimate of the asset's future earnings, just forget it and move on. No one has the ability to evaluate every investment possibility. But omniscience isn't necessary; you only need to understand the actions you undertake." (2013)
6. "If you instead focus on the prospective price change of a contemplated purchase, you are speculating... And the fact that a given asset has appreciated in the recent past is never a reason to buy it." (2013)
7. "Games are won by players who focus on the playing field – not by those whose eyes are glued to the scoreboard. If you can enjoy Saturdays and Sundays without looking at stock prices, give it a try on weekdays." (2013)
8. "Forming macro opinions or listening to the macro or market predictions of others is a waste of time. Indeed, it is dangerous because it may blur your vision of the facts that are truly important." (2013)
9. "A climate of fear is your friend when investing; a euphoric world is your enemy." (2013)
10. "Those people who can sit quietly for decades when they own a farm or an apartment house too often become frenetic when they are exposed to a stream of stock quotations and accompanying commentators delivering an implied message of 'Don't just sit there, do something.' For these investors, liquidity is transformed from the unqualified benefit it should be to a curse." (2013)
11. "In the 54 years we have worked together, we have never foregone an attractive purchase because of the macro or political environment, or the views of other people. In fact, these subjects never come up when we make decisions." (2013)
12. "So ignore the chatter, keep your costs minimal, and invest in stocks as you would in a farm." (2013)

### 2014 letter (dated February 27, 2015) — the Golden Anniversary letter

**Context.** Net worth +$18.3B; book value per share +8.3% (market value +27.0%, S&P +13.7%). A column for market price was added to the performance table because the book-value/intrinsic-value gap has widened as Berkshire shifted from marked-to-market securities to operating businesses. Powerhouse Five earned $12.4B; BNSF had a bad service year and will spend $6B in 2015; 31 bolt-ons costing $7.8B (incl. Duracell via a stock exchange with P&G); Van Tuyl auto dealerships bought; Heinz with 3G praised; Tesco sold at a $444M loss; float $84B and 12th straight underwriting profit. Separately, Buffett wrote "Berkshire – Past, Present and Future" and Munger wrote "Vice Chairman's Thoughts – Past and Future"; neither edited the other.

**Core teachings in the main letter.**
- Stock prices are erratic short-term but "Over time, however, stock prices and intrinsic value almost invariably converge."
- Bolt-ons are encouraged "if they are sensibly-priced. (Most deals offered us aren't.)"
- Investing and managing businesses are two separate skills that each inform the other, so Todd and Ted were given subsidiaries to chair.
- Investing was defined (citing 2011) as "the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power – after taxes have been paid on nominal gains – in the future." The dollar lost 87% of its purchasing power 1965-2014 while the S&P returned 11,196%; hence "it has been far safer to invest in a diversified collection of American businesses than to invest in securities – Treasuries, for example – whose values have been tied to American currency."
- Volatility is not risk: "Volatility is far from synonymous with risk. Popular formulas that equate the two terms lead students, investors and CEOs astray." Stocks are riskier than cash for a day/week/year, but for multi-decade investors "quotational declines are unimportant."
- Investors make stocks risky through their own behavior: "Active trading, attempts to 'time' market movements, inadequate diversification, the payment of high and unnecessary fees to managers and advisors, and the use of borrowed money."
- "borrowed money has no place in the investor's tool kit: Anything can happen anytime in markets."
- Institutions underperform the index investor largely because of fees and consultants; "Most advisors, however, are far better at generating high fees than they are at generating high returns. In truth, their core competence is salesmanship." Read Bogle.
- Amortization/EBITDA lesson repeated: $1.15B amortization, ~20% real; "When CEOs tout EBITDA as a valuation guide, wire them up for a polygraph test."
- MSR group earned 18.7% after-tax on $24B net tangible assets.
- Low interest rates reduce earnings on float and worsen the industry's already subnormal returns on tangible net worth.
- Underwriting discipline: "That message is given at least lip service by all insurers; at Berkshire it is a religion."
- Float as "owing $1 that in effect will never leave the premises"; GAAP treats it the same as debt due tomorrow.

**Core teachings in "Berkshire – Past, Present and Future".**
- Buying Berkshire (textiles) was "a monumentally stupid decision" driven by irritation at Seabury Stanton's 1/8-point chisel; buying Berkshire at $7.50 vs. $10.25 working capital was a cigar butt: "Though the stub might be ugly and soggy, the puff would be free."
- Buying National Indemnity inside Berkshire (61%-owned) rather than through BPL was "a colossal mistake" that "diverted $100 billion or so from BPL partners to a collection of strangers."
- Cigar-butt investing "was scalable only to a point. With large sums, it would never work well," and "marginal businesses purchased at cheap prices... are the wrong foundation on which to build a large and enduring enterprise. Selecting a marriage partner clearly requires more demanding criteria than does dating."
- Charlie's blueprint: "Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices."
- See's Candy (1972) as the model: ~$4M pre-tax on $8M net tangible assets, plus an off-balance-sheet "broad and durable competitive advantage that gave it significant pricing power," with growth needing "only minor amounts of incremental investment." He balked at paying 3x NTA ($25M); See's has since earned $1.9B pre-tax on $40M of added investment. "I gained a business education about the value of powerful brands."
- Dexter Shoe (1993): $433M paid in stock now worth $5.7B; "Its competitive strengths, however, were soon to evaporate because of foreign competition. And I simply didn't see that coming."
- Rule for stock deals: "The intrinsic value of the shares you give in an acquisition must not be greater than the intrinsic value of the business you receive." Bankers never quantify this; "customary premiums" and EPS accretion are "an absolutely asinine way" to evaluate deals; synergies are "conjured up"; dis-synergies never mentioned; post-mortems "should instead be standard practice."
- Conglomerate history: 1960s conglomerateurs used high P/E stock, pooling accounting and deal velocity; "business models based on the serial issuances of overpriced shares – just like chain-letter models – most assuredly redistribute wealth, but in no way create it." He and BPL "have never invested in companies that are hell-bent on issuing shares."
- Why the conglomerate form works at Berkshire: tax-free, low-friction redeployment of capital from businesses with limited reinvestment opportunities (See's) to better ones; freedom from "historical biases created by lifelong association with a given industry" ("If horses had controlled investment decisions, there would have been no auto industry"); ability to buy pieces of wonderful businesses via common stocks, which "are often far more attractive than the businesses we are concurrently being offered in their entirety"; being the home of choice for sellers vs. synergy-hungry competitors and leveraged PE buyers for whom "'equity' is a dirty word."
- Spin-offs make no sense for Berkshire; bankers urge "control premiums" to buy and then "unlock value" to spin off; "fees too often lead to transactions rather than transactions leading to fees." LTV/Jimmy Ling: "never forget that 2+2 will always equal 4."
- Next 50 years: chance of permanent capital loss for patient holders is very low, BUT "a sound investment can morph into a rash speculation if it is bought at an elevated price. Berkshire is not exempt from this truth." Hold at least five years; don't use borrowed money (three ~50% drops since 1965).
- Financial staying power = (1) large reliable earnings stream, (2) massive liquid assets ($20B+ in T-bills, "not other substitutes for cash"), (3) no significant near-term cash requirements; no derivative contracts requiring large collateral; "Cash, though, is to a business as oxygen is to an individual."
- Risk philosophy: "We will never play financial Russian roulette with the funds you've entrusted to us, even if the metaphorical gun has 100 chambers and only one bullet. In our view, it is madness to risk losing what you need in pursuing what you simply desire."
- Size limits returns; eventually (10-20 years) Berkshire will have to pay out via dividends or buybacks; if below intrinsic value, "massive repurchases will almost certainly be the best choice."
- Governance: directors paid token fees, own shares bought in the market, no D&O insurance; Howard as non-executive chairman as a "safety valve" to remove a wrong CEO; CEO must be "rational, calm and decisive," know his limits, be "all in" for the company, resist peer-matched pay; must fight the "ABCs of business decay, which are arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency" (GM, IBM, Sears, U.S. Steel). "Tone at the top." Extreme delegation, no committees, no budgets required, no HR/PR/IR/strategy/acquisitions departments.
- Owner base: the dividend proxy resolution was rejected 89:1 (A) and 47:1 (B).

**Core teachings in Munger's "Vice Chairman's Thoughts".** Munger lays out "the Berkshire system" in 15 numbered elements, including: a diffuse conglomerate "averse only to activities about which it could not make useful predictions"; subsidiaries' CEOs with "very extreme autonomy"; almost nothing at headquarters; casualty insurers always prominent, producing underwriting gains and float; no system-wide personnel, option or incentive system; the Chairman reserves only a few activities (manage securities, choose and pay subsidiary CEOs and get succession recommendations, deploy excess cash ideally into new subsidiaries, be available but require no contact, write a long logical letter "designed as he would wish it to be if he were only a passive shareholder," be a cultural exemplar, reserve "much time for quiet reading and thinking," and admire what others accomplish); buy subsidiaries with cash, not stock; "Berkshire would not pay dividends so long as more than one dollar of market value for shareholders was being created by each dollar of retained earnings"; pay "a fair price for a good business that the Chairman could pretty well understand" with a good CEO in place; seek "trustworthiness, skill, energy, and love for the business"; almost never sell a subsidiary; never force a CEO to retire for age; little debt, "virtually perfect creditworthiness under all conditions"; user-friendly and confidential with sellers. Themes he diagnoses: maximize rationality/skills/devotion starting with Buffett himself; "win/win results everywhere"; long-term decisions by people who stay to bear consequences; minimize headquarters bureaucracy; spread wisdom like Ben Graham. Four reasons for success: Buffett's constructive peculiarities, the system's constructive peculiarities, good luck, and "the weirdly intense, contagious devotion" of shareholders and admirers. The John Wooden analogy: concentrating playing time on the best players, and Buffett "much out-Woodened Wooden" by concentrating skill in one person who improved for 50 years. Casualty insurance normally "a producer of mediocre results, even when very well managed"; Berkshire's outcome was "not a natural result." Why cash not stock: "it was hard to get anything in exchange for Berkshire stock that was as valuable as what was given up." Why acquisitions worked: no "department of acquisitions" under pressure to buy, no reliance on "helpers," Buffett "held self-delusion at bay as he underclaimed expertise," and "almost inhuman patience" (one net new business in his first ten years). Big mistakes were mostly omissions, e.g. not buying Walmart; net worth would be "at least $50 billion higher." He predicts Berkshire stays "better-than-normal" even if Buffett left tomorrow and successors were only moderate, names Ajit Jain and Greg Abel as "world-leading," and recommends versions of the Berkshire system (treating bureaucracy "like the cancers they so much resemble") be tried elsewhere, citing George Marshall ignoring seniority in picking generals.

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**
- *BNSF*: lost share to Union Pacific on service failures; capex to run 26% of revenue vs. industry ~16-18%.
- *Van Tuyl*: 78 dealerships; strategy of making local managers owner-partners.
- *Heinz*: "run far better under Alex Behring... and Bernardo Hees... than would be the case if I were in charge."
- *Marmon railcars*: fleet carried at a "bargain" because no profit is booked on internal transfers; worth considerably more than $5B carrying value.
- *Clayton*: "Our blue-collar borrowers, in many cases, proved much better credit risks than their higher-income brethren"; kept lending through 2008-09 because of Berkshire backing.
- *National Indemnity*: bought in 15 minutes on a 1.5-page homemade contract with no audit because "Jack was honest" and quirky.
- *GEICO*: 10.8% share vs 2.5% in 1995; the gecko "works without pay."
- *Tesco*: see mistakes.
- *Big Four*: unrealized gains $42B; look-through earnings $4.7B vs. $1.6B dividends.
- *See's, Dexter, NICO, Waumbec, Gen Re*: discussed above.

**Mistakes admitted and the lesson.** Tesco: he soured on management in 2013 and sold slowly ("thumb-sucking," per Charlie); "In the world of business, bad news often surfaces serially: You see a cockroach in your kitchen; as the days go by, you meet his relatives." After-tax loss $444M (0.2% of net worth). Also, in the anniversary piece: not tendering to Stanton; putting NICO in Berkshire; Waumbec Mills (1975); Dexter; several other stock-financed purchases of "so-so" businesses; delaying the textile shutdown for far too long. Munger adds the errors of omission (Walmart).

### 2015 letter (dated February 27, 2016)

**Context.** Net worth +$15.4B; book value +6.4% but market value fell 12.5% (S&P +1.4%). Precision Castparts bought for $32B+ cash (brought to him by Todd Combs); Heinz merged with Kraft (Berkshire 27%, cost $9.8B, GAAP forced a $6.8B write-up); BNSF fixed its service with a record $5.8B capex and record $6.8B pre-tax; Powerhouse Five earned $13.1B; 29 bolt-ons; 13th straight underwriting profit, float $88B; $16B capex overall. Long sections on productivity and prosperity (farming, railroads, auto insurance, utilities), "Important Risks" (why 10-K risk factors are useless, cyber/nuclear risk, climate change and insurance), Clayton's lending practices, and the first inclusion of underwriting income in the "two pillars" of intrinsic value.

**Core teachings in this letter.**
- Book value and intrinsic value diverged after the early 1990s because "The carrying value of the 'losers' we own is written down, but 'winners' are never revalued upwards" — an asymmetry "with which we agree."
- Two quantitative pillars of Berkshire's intrinsic value: per-share investments ($159,794) and per-share pre-tax earnings from businesses ($12,304), now including underwriting income (averaged $1,434/share over the decade). Dividends/interest from investments are excluded from the second pillar to avoid double counting.
- 3G's method (buy, then promptly eliminate unnecessary costs) vs. Berkshire's: "avoidance of bloat, buying businesses such as PCC that have long been run by cost-conscious and efficient managers," then leaving them alone. Mungerism: "If you want to guarantee yourself a lifetime of misery, be sure to marry someone with the intent of changing their behavior."
- Only friendly deals: "At Berkshire, we go only where we are welcome."
- Big Four returns on tangible equity "range from excellent to staggering"; each 1 percentage point of ownership = ~$500M of annual earnings.
- Stock-based compensation IS an expense: "The very name says it all: 'compensation.' If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if real and recurring expenses don't belong in the calculation of earnings, where in the world do they belong?" Analysts who parrot adjusted figures are "guilty of propagating misleading numbers."
- Railroad depreciation "falls far short of the capital outlays needed to merely keep the railroad running properly, a mismatch that leads to GAAP earnings that are higher than true economic earnings." So: add back most amortization, but subtract something for BNSF's inadequate depreciation. "When CEOs or investment bankers tout pre-depreciation figures such as EBITDA as a valuation guide, watch their noses lengthen while they speak."
- Capital allocation mistakes come from two sources: misjudging economic dynamics, and "evaluating either the fidelity or the ability of incumbent managers or ones I later appointed."
- MSR group earned 18.4% after-tax on $25.6B of net tangible assets with "only token amounts of leverage."
- Productivity is "the all-important factor in America's economic growth"; 2% real growth compounds to +34.4% per capita in 25 years; today's upper-middle-class live better than Rockefeller did in 1930; "For 240 years it's been a terrible mistake to bet against America, and now is no time to start."
- The flip side of productivity: workers at Dexter and the New Bedford textile mill "had no Plan B"; the answer is safety nets (he favors an expanded EITC), not restraining productivity. "We need shed no tears for the capitalists... It's their job to take care of themselves."
- Diversified investors who sit tight "are certain to prosper: In America, gains from winning investments have always far more than offset the losses from clunkers."
- 10-K risk sections are useless because they don't assess probability, cost range, or timing: "A threat that will only surface 50 years from now may be a problem for society, but it is not a financial problem for today's investor." Truly important risks "are usually well known" (coal volume at BNSF, driverless cars at GEICO, newspaper circulation, storage for renewables, online retail).
- Small annual probabilities become near-certainties over long horizons (1-in-30 per year = 96.6% in a century); the one risk Berkshire can't shed is a mass-destruction attack on the U.S.
- Climate change and Pascal's Wager / "Noah's Law: If an ark may be essential for survival, begin building it today, no matter how cloudless the skies appear." But as a shareholder of an insurer it's not a worry: policies are repriced annually, and rising loss costs make insurers more valuable (GEICO's $30 loss-per-policy in 1951 became $1,000; a $600M business became a $23B one).
- Clayton: 100% risk retention on mortgages it originates; losses on foreclosures dwarf sale profits; lending long/borrowing short is "risky business" but Berkshire's $40-60B of short-term cash makes the whole enterprise "heavily asset-sensitive" and a beneficiary of rising rates.
- The 2008 mortgage machine failed because originator and packager "had no skin in the game"; "When Wall Street gets 'innovative,' watch out!"
- Insurance industry outlook: low rates "virtually guarantee" falling float earnings; results over the next ten years will fall short of the past decade, "particularly for those companies that specialize in reinsurance."
- Role models: Tom Murphy and Dan Burke of Capital Cities were "the best managerial duo... that Charlie and I ever witnessed." "Much of what you become in life depends on whom you choose to admire and copy."
- No one is hired at headquarters unless truly needed; "That's why you've never read about 'restructuring' charges at Berkshire."

**Businesses/investments discussed and why.**
- *Precision Castparts*: world's premier aerospace component supplier under Mark Donegan, who "transform[s] very ordinary raw materials into extraordinary products"; multi-year contracts; acquisitive.
- *Kraft Heinz*: passion shared with 3G "to buy, build and hold large businesses that satisfy basic needs and desires."
- *BNSF*: 17% of intercity ton-miles, 45% more than nearest rival; revenue/ton-mile under 3¢ vs. 4.2-5.3¢ for other majors; 702M ton-miles with 47,000 employees vs. 411M with 45,000 in 1996.
- *BHE*: Iowa retail rate 6.8¢ vs. 9.5¢ for Alliant and 10.4¢ national average; wind = 47% of Iowa retail MWh; no Iowa rate increase in 16 years; never paid a dividend to Berkshire; efficiency matters now because subsidized renewables can erode high-cost incumbents.
- *GEICO*: direct-distribution history (State Farm, USAA, Goodwins 1936); underwriting expense 14.7% of premiums vs ~25% at State Farm/Allstate; 34,000 employees vs. ~60,000 under an agency model; "the second mouse gets the cheese."

Prosperity has been bipartisan (7 Republican, 7 Democratic presidents) through inflation, 21% prime rate, wars, panics. National "savings" are to America what retained earnings are to Berkshire. "It is beyond arrogance for American businesses or individuals to boast that they have 'done it alone.'"
- GEICO history as a management lesson: near-bankruptcy in 1976 from under-reserving; Jack Byrne as "the perfect man for the job"; Berkshire bought a third, which grew to half through GEICO's buybacks "without our spending a dime," for $47M; paid $2.3B for the other half in 1995 ("and people say I never pay up!"); Tony Nicely added more than $50B of intrinsic value; underwriting profits $15.5B since purchase; float $2.5B → $22.1B.

Implicitly, the Kraft Heinz write-down. On GEICO 1976: a lesson in what underpricing from bad reserving can do even to a great franchise.

## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 2013-2018

### 1. What investing IS, according to these letters

- Investing is foregone consumption now for more purchasing power later. The 2014 letter re-quotes the 2011 definition — "the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power – after taxes have been paid on nominal gains – in the future" (2014) — and 2017 restates it as "an activity in which consumption today is foregone in an attempt to allow greater consumption at a later date. 'Risk' is the possibility that this objective won't be attained" (2017). This definition drives his conclusion that currency-denominated assets (Treasuries, bonds, gold) have been and will be the risky assets for long-horizon investors, since the dollar lost 87% of its purchasing power 1965-2014 (2014, 2017, 2018).
- A stock is a piece of a business. "When Charlie and I buy stocks – which we think of as small portions of businesses – our analysis is very similar to that which we use in buying entire businesses" (2013); stocks are "interests in businesses, not... ticker symbols" (2017); the portfolio is "an assembly of companies that we partly own" earning ~20% on net tangible equity (2018).
- The farm and NYU parables (2013) are the clearest statement of method: estimate the asset's normalized productivity (both yielded ~10% unleveraged), expect productivity and prices to rise, ignore daily quotations, never be under pressure to sell, and never consult macro forecasts. The explicit decision rule: can we "sensibly estimate an earnings range for five years out, or more"? If yes, buy "at a reasonable price in relation to the bottom boundary of our estimate"; if not, "we simply move on to other prospects" (2013).
- Speculation is defined as focusing on the prospective price change rather than the asset's output; past appreciation "is never a reason to buy" (2013).
- Time horizon: hold Berkshire at least five years; for shorter periods market movements dominate intrinsic-value changes (2014). For most investors the horizon should be multi-decade, in which case "quotational declines are unimportant" (2014). "Over time, stock prices gravitate toward intrinsic value" (2016); "investment performance converges with business performance" (2018); Graham's voting machine / weighing machine quoted in 2014 and 2017.
- For non-professionals the "what" is a low-cost S&P 500 index fund and the "when" is steady accumulation with no selling on bad news (2013, 2014, 2016, 2017). He commits his own estate to 90% Vanguard S&P / 10% short-term governments (2013).
- Inactivity is a virtue: "Stick with big, 'easy' decisions and eschew activity" (2017); the Bet partners made one decision in ten years while the hedge-fund managers made tens of thousands (2017).

### 2. What to look for in a business

- The 2017 acquisition criteria are the canonical list for this period: "durable competitive strengths; able and high-grade management; good returns on the net tangible assets required to operate the business; opportunities for internal growth at attractive returns; and, finally, a sensible purchase price" (2017). The 2014 published Acquisition Criteria add: consistent demonstrated earning power (no projections, no turnarounds), good ROE with little or no debt, management in place, simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it"), and an offering price (2014). 2018 restates the goal as "ably-managed businesses... that possess favorable and durable economic characteristics... at sensible prices."
- The yardstick is return on unleveraged net tangible assets: 25%-100%+ after-tax = terrific; 12-20% = good; the MSR group as a whole earned 16.7% (2013), 18.7% (2014), 18.4% (2015), 24% (2016) with excess cash and little leverage. The equity portfolio earns ~20% on net tangible equity without excessive debt (2018).
- See's is the archetype of the ideal business: $4M pre-tax on $8M of NTA, a "broad and durable competitive advantage that gave it significant pricing power," growth requiring "only minor amounts of incremental investment," so it "could be expected to gush cash for decades" — $1.9B earned on $40M added (2014). Cash it couldn't reinvest was redeployed elsewhere, which is the whole case for the conglomerate form (2014).
- Moats named explicitly: GEICO's low-cost position — "Its low costs create a moat – an enduring one – that competitors are unable to cross" (2013, 2014, 2015, 2016); BHE's regulated essential service with diverse earnings streams and the lowest rates in its region (2013-2016); BNSF as "the most important artery in our economy's circulatory system" with 4x truck fuel efficiency and 3¢ vs 4-5¢ per ton-mile (2013-2016); Berkshire Re's unmatched capital and "brains" (2013-2017); strong brands learned from See's (2014).
- Predictability: Munger's system is "averse only to activities about which it could not make useful predictions" (2014); "Charlie and I have no idea what a great many companies will look like ten years from now" (2014); he will not analyze PCC's German acquisition but will bet on Donegan (2017).
- Capital intensity is acceptable when returns are regulated and reasonable (BHE and BNSF are given their own section; "We relish making such commitments as long as they promise reasonable returns"), but he flags that railroad depreciation understates true maintenance capex (2015, 2016, 2017, 2018).
- Growth that creates value: investees retaining earnings for buybacks and good projects; Berkshire's own retained earnings "must earn their keep" (2016); growth is destroyed by stock-funded acquisitions of so-so businesses (2014) and by cigar-butt economics at scale (2014).
- Industry structure matters: the P/C industry "will continue its dismal record of earning subnormal returns on tangible net worth" because of competition (every year), yet Berkshire's disciplined units earn well because of hard-to-replicate models (every year). Efficiency that the business "didn't need" (BHE's utilities) becomes decisive when the economic model changes (2015).

### 3. What to look for in management

- Honesty and candour first: Jack Ringwalt and Mrs. B were bought on a handshake without audits because their word was good (2013, 2014). Munger's criteria for subsidiary CEOs: "trustworthiness, skill, energy, and love for the business" (2014). Mistakes have come from misjudging "the fidelity or the ability of incumbent managers" (2015, 2016).
- Owner-orientation: managers "run their businesses as if they were the only asset owned by their families" (2013-2017); directors and managers hold shares bought in the market, "None, however, gets the upside of ownership without risking the downside" (2017); no D&O insurance, token director fees (2014).
- Capital allocation is the CEO's main job: "Managing Berkshire is primarily a job of capital allocation, coupled with the selection and retention of outstanding managers" (2014). A CEO must be "rational, calm and decisive," "know his limits" (Tom Watson's "I'm smart in spots and I stay around those spots"), be "all in" for the company, and not reach for peer-matched pay (2014). "it's great to have a manager with a 160 IQ – unless he thinks it's 180" (2016). Soundness beats brilliance (2016).
- Institutional imperative, as described without the label: CEOs "self-select for 'can-do' types"; a deal-hungry CEO "will never lack for forecasts that justify the purchase"; subordinates and bankers cheer; "Spreadsheets never disappoint" (2017). "The other guy is doing it, so we must as well" is the fourth insurance commandment broken (every year). A CEO with capital in a declining business "seldom elects to massively redeploy that capital" (2014). The "ABCs of business decay... arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency" felled GM, IBM, Sears and U.S. Steel (2014).
- Red flags: managements "hell-bent on issuing shares" signal "a promotion-minded management, weak accounting, a stock that is overpriced and – all too often – outright dishonesty" (2014); managements that always "make the numbers" (2016); routine "adjusted earnings" that exclude stock comp and restructuring (2015, 2016, 2018); "bold, imaginative accounting" (2014); 1960s auditors answering "What number do you have in mind?" (2016).
- Compensation: Berkshire pays several hundred executives large sums in cash and expenses all of it; stock-based pay is compensation (2015, 2016, 2018). No system-wide option or incentive system (Munger 2014). Todd and Ted are paid for over-performance (2016).
- Decentralization as management policy: no committees, no required budgets, no HR/PR/IR/strategy/legal departments at HQ, 24-25 people at headquarters for 300,000+ employees (2013-2016); "our trust produces better results than would be achieved by streams of directives, endless reviews and layers of bureaucracy" (2014); Mungerism about not marrying to change someone (2015). Berkshire does not prepare monthly earnings reports or a company-wide budget, so there is never a "number" to hit (2018).
- Role models: Tom Murphy and Dan Burke (2015); Tony Nicely (2018); 3G's cost discipline admired but not copied (2015).

### 4. Valuation: price, intrinsic value, margin of safety, discount rates, growth

- Intrinsic value cannot be stated precisely "for Berkshire shares (nor, in fact, for any other stock)" but "It is possible, however, to make a sensible estimate" (2013, 2014, 2015). For Berkshire the estimate rests on two quantitative pillars — per-share investments and per-share pre-tax operating earnings (from 2015 including underwriting income) — plus a qualitative one; the stock has compounded at roughly the rate of both pillars (2013-2015). By 2018 the estimate is the sum of four groves minus deferred taxes on securities, with no tax deduction for wholly-owned businesses because they will never be sold.
- Book value is only a crude tracker whose relevance has collapsed because winners are never written up and losers are written down (2014, 2015, 2016); it is abandoned as the headline metric in 2018, with market price adopted as the long-run scorecard.
- Margin of safety in practice: the 120%-of-book repurchase threshold is "a significant discount to Berkshire's intrinsic value, a spread that is appropriate because calculations of intrinsic value can't be precise" (2016); buy only "in relation to the bottom boundary of our estimate" of earnings (2013); directors will repurchase only "well below intrinsic value" (2014).
- Price discipline: "a business with terrific economics can be a bad investment if the purchase price is excessive" (2013, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2018); "a sound investment can morph into a rash speculation if it is bought at an elevated price. Berkshire is not exempt from this truth" (2014); near double book value it "may well be many years before the investor can realize a profit" (2014); 2017 and 2018 deals were blocked by all-time-high prices, so capital went to stocks that "offered far more for our money."
- Interest rates as the benchmark: the 2012 bond at 95.7 with a 0.88% yield was "more than 100 times earnings" that could not grow, versus S&P dividends at 2.5% and growing plus retained earnings (2017); equities become safer than bonds over long horizons "assuming that the stocks are purchased at a sensible multiple of earnings relative to then-prevailing interest rates" (2017); 20% returns on tangible equity are "mind-blowing" against 3% long Treasuries (2018). He gives no discount-rate formula in these letters.
- Growth: the five-part plan to build per-share intrinsic value (2013, 2014, 2015); growth via issuing stock is only worth it when "we receive as much in intrinsic business value as we give" (2014 criteria); Berkshire's size now "precludes a brilliant result: Prospective returns fall as assets increase" (2016) and future percentage gains "cannot be dramatic" (2014).
- Look-through valuation: the Big Four's retained earnings, buybacks and unrealized gains are counted as value even though GAAP ignores them (2013-2015); Principle 6 expects each retained dollar to produce at least a dollar of market value, and 2018 says it has delivered "more than one dollar for each dollar."

### 5. Temperament and psychology

- Mr. Market reappears as "a moody fellow with a farm bordering my property [who] yelled out a price every day" — the investor can only be benefited by his erratic behavior (2013). Liquidity becomes "a curse" for those who react to quotes and commentators (2013).
- Fear and greed: "A climate of fear is your friend when investing; a euphoric world is your enemy" (2013); "widespread fear is your friend... personal fear is your enemy" (2016); dark clouds "briefly rain gold" and one must carry "washtubs, not teaspoons" (2016); crashes offer "extraordinary opportunities to those who are not handicapped by debt" (2017); "A bull market is like sex. It feels best just before it ends" (Barton Biggs, 2013).
- Patience and inactivity: Munger praises Buffett's "almost inhuman patience" (2014); only one investment decision in ten years during the Bet (2017); the "know-nothing" investor who sits tight beats institutions (2013, 2014); "a willingness to look unimaginative for a sustained period – or even to look foolish – is also essential" (2017).
- Concentration: Munger's John Wooden analogy — give all the playing time to the best players; Buffett "much out-Woodened Wooden" (2014). Buffett put 65% of his $9,800 net worth into GEICO in 1951 (2014). Berkshire's own holdings are 5-10% stakes in a limited number of large companies (2018). For the non-professional, however, diversification plus low cost is the prescription (2013, 2014, 2015).
- Circle of competence: "recognize the perimeter of our 'circle of competence' and stay well inside of it" (2013); "We are limited, of course, to businesses whose economic prospects we can evaluate. And that's a serious limitation" (2014); "I don't understand manufacturing operations as well as I do the activities of real estate brokers, home builders or truck stops" (2017); Munger: Buffett "underclaimed expertise" and "held self-delusion at bay" (2014).
- Leverage and the mind: "an unsettled mind will not make good decisions" (2017); Berkshire shares fell ~50% four times and will again (2014, 2017); "Berkshire shares should not be purchased with borrowed money" (2014).
- Ignore the noise: "Market forecasters will fill your ear but will never fill your wallet" (2014); stocks should not be sold because of "downgrades by 'the Street,' expected Federal Reserve actions, possible political developments, forecasts by economists or whatever else might be the subject du jour" (2018).
- Humility about luck: 1,000 monkeys produce a nine-year prophet (2016); "in the short run, it's difficult to determine whether a great record is due to luck or talent" (2014); Munger lists "good luck" among four causes of Berkshire's success (2014); "Through dumb luck, Charlie and I were born in the United States" (2014).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons

- Amortization vs depreciation (every year 2013-2018): purchase-accounting amortization of customer relationships and similar intangibles is "clearly not real" (only ~20% of the charge is real: $648M in 2013, $1.15B 2014, $1.1B 2015, $1.5B 2016, $1.3B 2017, $1.4B 2018), and should be added back; depreciation is "a real cost" and at railroads it understates true maintenance capex (2015-2018). EBITDA as a valuation guide: "button your wallet" (2013), "wire them up for a polygraph test" (2014), "watch their noses lengthen" (2015), "adjusted EBITDA... excludes a variety of all-too-real costs" (2018). Interest coverage = EBIT/interest, "not EBITDA/interest" (2013-2016).
- Stock-based compensation is an expense (2015, 2016, 2018); the thought-experiment lab that would restate Berkshire's cash pay as "paid in lieu of stock" (2016); "What else could it be – a gift from shareholders?" (2018).
- Restructuring charges are recurring business costs; Berkshire has restructured "dozens of times" and never asked you to ignore them; only a true rebasing like Kraft Heinz justifies multi-year explanation (2016, 2018).
- Float accounting: deducted in full as a liability but really a revolving fund; "Owing $1 that in effect will never leave the premises... is worlds different from owing $1 that will go out the door tomorrow"; GAAP treats them identically (2014-2018). Insurance goodwill of $15.5B is carried at cost though float has more than tripled since 2000 (2015, 2016).
- Book value vs intrinsic value: losers written down, winners never written up (2014-2016); Marmon railcars carried at a "bargain" because no profit is booked on internal transfers (2014); Kraft Heinz written up $6.8B by GAAP on the merger "an outcome only an accountant could love" (2015); the $1.8B charge to "capital in excess of par value" for buying out Marmon/Iscar minorities "instantly increased Berkshire's excess of intrinsic value over book value by the same $1.8 billion" (2013).
- Mark-to-market of equities in net income (new rule, 2017-2018) makes the bottom line "useless"; quarterly GAAP swung from -$25.4B to +$18.5B in 2018; focus on operating earnings.
- Look-through earnings: dividends received understate "true" earnings; Principle 6 (2013-2015, 2017, 2018 with the dividends vs retained-earnings table for the top five).
- Loss reserves are "a self-graded exam" (2017); initial catastrophe estimates run low (2017); under-reserving nearly bankrupted GEICO in 1976 (2018); CEOs who fudge "just this once" foster "cookie-jar" reserves and worse (2016, 2018).
- Tax lessons: corporate dividend income is taxed at 10.5¢/7¢ per dollar for the parent vs 35¢ on capital gains (2016); offshore cash is worth less than domestic (2016); the Treasury's "AA shares" and the 14/35ths returned by the 2017 tax cut (2018); deferred taxes are an interest-free loan (2018).
- Derivatives: legacy equity-put contracts were profitable and provided float, but newly written contracts require full collateral, which "ended our interest in derivatives" (2013, 2014).
- Pension accounting: public pension promises are a "gigantic financial tapeworm"; his 1975 memo on pension math is reproduced (2013); public pensions suffered "poor investment performance accompanied by huge fees" (2016).
- 10-K risk factors never help evaluate a business because they omit probability, cost and timing (2015).
- Formulae given: buyback value math via the three-partner example (2016); intrinsic value ≈ four groves minus securities taxes (2018); productivity compounding (2% growth → +34.4% per capita over 25 years, 2015); 1-in-30 per year → 96.6% per century (2015); a 1% fee cuts a 77-year result in half (2018).

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level

- Retained-earnings test, stated by Munger: no dividends "so long as more than one dollar of market value for shareholders was being created by each dollar of retained earnings" (2014). Buffett: reinvested dollars "must earn their keep" (2016); Berkshire retained more earnings than any U.S. company in 2015-16; a 100% payout would have left Berkshire with its original $22M (2018). Shareholders voted 47:1 (B) and 89:1 (A) against a dividend (2014).
- Buybacks: value-creating only below intrinsic value; two exceptions; "What is smart at one price is stupid at another" (2016); 120%-of-book authorization (2013-2016) which proved "hard to do" because the signaling effect kept the price above it (2016); the threshold is later dropped in favor of discretion "at a discount to Berkshire's intrinsic value" with full information to selling partners (2018). Investee buybacks are cheered when the stock is cheap (2013-2018). Eventually "massive repurchases will almost certainly be the best choice" when reinvestment opportunities run out (2014).
- Issuing stock: the cardinal rule — "The intrinsic value of the shares you give in an acquisition must not be greater than the intrinsic value of the business you receive" (2014); Dexter and Gen Re as the two great sins (2014, 2016); "rarely, if ever, issuing Berkshire shares" (2013-2015); the colonoscopy line (2016); shares up only 8.3% in 18 years (2016); Munger: "it was hard to get anything in exchange for Berkshire stock that was as valuable as what was given up" (2014).
- Acquisitions: evaluate on an all-equity basis, never count synergies (2017); never bid in auctions, only friendly deals, five-minute answers (2014 criteria, 2015); bolt-ons encouraged only "if they are sensibly-priced" (2014, 2015); post-mortems should be standard practice (2014); "customary premiums" and EPS accretion are "asinine" tests (2014); spin-offs "make no sense for us" (2014); bankers' incentives (2014, 2017).
- The two-pronged approach (whole businesses or 5-10% stakes) doubles the odds of finding sensible uses for cash and "is rare in corporate America" (2013-2015, 2018); when private prices are "sky-high" the money goes to stocks (2018).
- Partnerships: 3G/Heinz as a template (2013-2015); permanent equity partner preferred, financing partner acceptable (2014, 2015); Berkadia, Mars, Leucadia, Pilot Flying J (2014, 2017).
- Leverage/debt: at least $20B of T-bills always; never material short-term obligations; no collateral-call exposure (2013, 2014, 2018); subsidiary debt at BNSF/BHE is non-recourse and covered 6-9x by EBIT (2013-2016, 2018); "Our aversion to leverage has dampened our returns over the years. But Charlie and I sleep well" (2017); Russian roulette with 100 chambers (2014) and "usually win, occasionally die" (2018); Clayton's lend-long/borrow-short is tolerated only because Berkshire overall is asset-sensitive (2015, 2016); private-equity buyers "love... debt" and equity "is a dirty word" (2014); cheap debt in 2017 made every deal accretive for others.
- Cash: "oxygen" (2014); cash held by insurers slightly less valuable than parent cash; offshore cash less valuable still (2016); $116B of cash "far beyond the level Charlie and I wish Berkshire to have" (2017).
- Why the conglomerate works: tax-free, frictionless capital movement, no industry bias, option to buy stocks, home of choice for sellers, tax credits usable because of other taxable income, single board (2014, 2018: "the whole is greater – considerably greater – than the sum of the parts").

### 8. Insurance and float

- Float grew from $39M (1970) to $77.2B (2013), $83.9B (2014), $87.7B (2015), $91.6B (2016), $114.5B (2017, with the $10.2B-premium AIG retroactive deal), $122.7B (2018). Underwriting profit streaks: 11 years/$22B (2013), 12/$24B (2014), 13/$26.2B (2015), 14/$28B (2016); then a $3.2B loss in 2017 from three hurricanes; 15 of 16 years/$27B through 2018.
- Economics: collect-now-pay-later; float is "money that doesn't belong to us but that we can invest for Berkshire's benefit"; when underwriting is profitable "we enjoy the use of free money – and, better yet, get paid for holding it" (every year). Long-tail lines and retroactive reinsurance generate the most float (2017). Float can't be withdrawn, so no runs; decline would be at most ~3%/yr (every year). Float has been "of more utility to us than an equivalent amount of equity" (2018).
- Industry: competition guarantees "subnormal returns on tangible net worth" (every year); low interest rates make it worse, especially for bond-heavy reinsurers (2014-2016); Berkshire's flexibility in investing float is its edge: "When others are constrained, our choices expand" (2016, 2018).
- Four underwriting disciplines, with walking away as the hardest (2013-2016); underwriting discipline is "a religion, Old Testament style" (2015, 2016, 2018).
- Risk and capital: a $250B industry mega-cat would still leave Berkshire profitable (2013-2016); a $400B event (~2% annual probability) would cost ~$12B while most of the industry would be insolvent (2017); Berkshire's share of U.S. mega-cats ~3% (2017); the Big One may be cyber (2018); after it, "we will be looking to add business the next day" (2018).
- Insurance is "the sale of promises"; only Berkshire's promise satisfies the largest cedents (Lloyd's 2007, AIG 2017); all eight P/C policies in history with premiums over $1B were written by Berkshire (2014).
- Units: Ajit Jain (reinsurance; BHSI formed 2013 and profitable from the start; Ajit "If there were ever to be another Ajit and you could swap me for him, don't hesitate"), Gen Re (Tad Montross then Kara Raiguel), GEICO (cost moat, market share 2.5% in 1995 → 10.8% in 2014 → ~12% in 2016; expense ratio 14.7%; Tony Nicely added >$50B of intrinsic value), the "Other Primary" group (13% underwriting margin on premiums, float $943M → $11.6B).
- Climate change is not an insurer's worry because policies reprice annually and rising loss costs enlarge the business; super-cat rates had fallen, so Berkshire backed away (2015).
- Munger's view: casualty insurance ordinarily yields "mediocre results, even when very well managed"; Berkshire's result "was not a natural result" and Buffett could not recreate it from a small base (2014).

### 9. Mistakes and what changed in his thinking over these years

- The cigar-butt → quality arc is told in full in 2014: Graham's cheap-stock technique made the 1950s his best decade, but it "was scalable only to a point" and such businesses "are the wrong foundation on which to build a large and enduring enterprise"; Charlie's blueprint ("buy wonderful businesses at fair prices") and See's (1972) changed everything; "Listening to Charlie has paid off."
- Buying Berkshire out of pique (1964-65), putting NICO inside Berkshire (the "most costly" mistake, ~$100B diverted), Waumbec (1975), 18 years of textile losses — all 2014. In 2015 the human cost of textiles and Dexter is added (workers with "no Plan B").
- Dexter Shoe: $433-434M in stock, now worth $5.7B (2014) / >$6B (2016); failed to foresee foreign competition.
- General Re stock issuance (+21.8% shares) was "a terrible mistake" (2016) even though Gen Re became "a gem"; MidAmerican in cash "atoned."
- Energy Future Holdings bonds, $873M pre-tax loss, bought without consulting Charlie (2013).
- Tesco: "thumb-sucking" after souring on management; $444M loss; cockroach lesson (2014).
- Smaller operating businesses with poor returns from misjudged economics and misjudged managers' "fidelity or ability" (2013-2016); dumb purchases with goodwill later written off (2015, 2016).
- Errors of omission, per Munger: Walmart and others worth "at least $50 billion" (2014). Buffett (2018): "I will... miss many opportunities, some of which should have been obvious to me."
- Changes in stance: adds market price to the performance table (2014) and then retires book value as the headline metric (2018); starts counting underwriting profit in intrinsic-value pillars (2015); drops the 120%-of-book buyback formula for a discretionary "below intrinsic value" standard (2018); clarifies that no marketable security is held "forever" (2016); warns that his earlier deficit-doom preaching was wrong given what stocks did (2018); acknowledges the size handicap more bluntly each year; hands operations to Jain and Abel (2017-2018) and says Berkshire is "far better managed than when I alone was supervising operations" (2018). Airlines, once avoided, were bought in 2016 — not discussed in principle, only joked about.

### 10. Mentions of Graham, Munger, Fisher, Keynes and other investors

- **Ben Graham**: The Intelligent Investor (1949) "changed" his financial life; Chapters 8 and 20 still guide his decisions; "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike"; "Price is what you pay, value is what you get"; Graham's GEICO bonanza and his Northern Pacific recommendation (2013). "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves" via Graham (2014). The voting/weighing machine (2014, 2017). Graham taught the cheap-stock technique "and it worked" (2014). ### 11. Macro and market views

- America: "who has ever benefited during the past 237 years by betting against America?" (2013); real per-capita output sextupled in his lifetime (2014, 2015); 2% growth compounds to a $19,000 per-capita gain in a generation (2015); $90 trillion of wealth (2016), $108 trillion household wealth (2018); "Babies born in America today are the luckiest crop in history" (2015, 2016); the American Tailwind, bipartisan prosperity, $114.75 → $606,811 (2018). Yet he warns of the one unhedgeable risk — a cyber/biological/nuclear/chemical attack (2015).
- Forecasting: macro opinions are "a waste of time" and "dangerous" (2013); no one can predict panics (2014, 2016, 2017, 2018); "My expectation of more stock purchases is not a market call" (2018); Buffett himself was wrong preaching deficit doom (2018).
- Interest rates: low rates compress float earnings industry-wide (2014-2016); Berkshire is "heavily asset-sensitive" and benefits from rising short rates (2015, 2016); bonds at 0.88% were "a dumb – a really dumb – investment compared to American equities" (2017); 30-year Treasuries at 3% vs businesses earning 20% on tangible equity (2018); stocks should be bought "at a sensible multiple of earnings relative to then-prevailing interest rates" (2017). He does not use the phrase "gravity" in these letters.
- Inflation and currency: the dollar lost 87% of purchasing power 1965-2014; currency-denominated instruments are the risky assets over long horizons (2014, 2017); gold bought in 1942 would be worth under 1% of an index fund (2018); rising loss costs make insurers more valuable (2015); inflation-era depreciation inadequacy survives in railroads (2016).
- Leverage and credit cycles: cheap debt drives overpriced deals (2017); "At rare and unpredictable intervals, however, credit vanishes and debt becomes financially fatal" (2018); the 2008 mortgage machine with no skin in the game (2015); Treasury bills, not substitutes, are the only real liquidity (2014, 2017).
- Derivatives: exited except for utility hedges because of full-collateral requirements (2013, 2014).
- Market timing: enter steadily over time; the danger is buying at "extreme exuberance" and then being disillusioned (2013); when "2+2 will always equal 4" is called old-fashioned, "zip up your wallet, take a vacation and come back in a few years to buy stocks at cheap prices" (2014).
- Fees and the asset-management industry: the single most sustained macro-level critique of the period — the 2005 argument, the Bet, "2 and 20," consultants as "hyper-helpers," $100B+ wasted by the elite, pensions underfunded by fees plus poor performance (2014, 2016, 2017, 2018).
- Productivity and distribution: productivity is "the all-important factor" in growth; the losers deserve safety nets (EITC), not restraints on efficiency (2015); the market system is "an economic traffic cop" and government redirects a significant share of the bounty (2016).
- Climate change: "highly likely" a major problem; Noah's Law; but irrelevant to an annually-repricing insurer (2015).
