## Part 1: Year-by-year

### 2007 letter (written February 2008)

**Context.** Net worth up $12.3 billion, book value per share +11% (vs S&P +5.5%); 43-year compounded book-value growth 21.1%. Housing-linked units (brick, carpet, brokerage) were hurt as the housing bubble ("Please, God, Just One More Bubble") burst and lenders' "weakened lending practices" were exposed. Insurance had a second straight catastrophe-free year, and Buffett warns margins will "fall significantly in 2008." The big event: the Christmas-Day agreement to buy 60% of Marmon (Pritzker family; 125 businesses, Union Tank Car) for $4.5 billion, the largest cash purchase in Berkshire's history. Also sold PetroChina for $4 billion (bought for $488 million). Richline (jewelry) and BoatU.S. added. TTI and Iscar "performed magnificently."

**Core teachings.**
- The four acquisition criteria restated: "a) a business we understand; b) favorable long-term economics; c) able and trustworthy management; and d) a sensible price tag." Prefer to buy the whole business or at least 80%, but happy to own small pieces of great businesses via the stock market: "It's better to have a part interest in the Hope Diamond than to own all of a rhinestone."
- A truly great business must have an *enduring* moat protecting high returns on invested capital, because "the dynamics of capitalism guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning high returns." Moat examples: low-cost producer (GEICO, Costco) or a powerful worldwide brand (Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express). "Roman Candles" are companies whose moats proved illusory.
- The "enduring" criterion rules out industries prone to rapid and continuous change: "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all." Creative destruction is good for society but "precludes investment certainty."
- It also rules out businesses whose success depends on a superstar manager (the brain-surgeon partnership vs. the Mayo Clinic). "If a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot be deemed great."
- What he seeks: "Long-term competitive advantage in a stable industry." Organic growth is a bonus, not a requirement, because "there's no rule that you have to invest money where you've earned it"; truly great businesses can't reinvest large portions of earnings internally at high rates for long.
- The great / good / gruesome taxonomy expressed as three "savings accounts": the great one pays an extraordinarily high rate that rises over time (See's); the good one pays an attractive rate that is also earned on added deposits (FlightSafety, utilities); the gruesome one pays an inadequate rate *and* requires you to keep adding money (airlines).
- See's as the "prototype of a dream business": bought for $25m in 1972 when sales were $30m, pre-tax earnings under $5m and capital employed $8m (60% pre-tax on invested capital). Two reasons for low capital needs: sold for cash (no receivables) and short production/distribution cycle (low inventories). By 2007: sales $383m, pre-tax $82m, capital $40m; only $32m reinvested since 1972 while $1.35 billion of pre-tax earnings were sent to Berkshire to buy other businesses. A typical company growing earnings from $5m to $82m "require[s], say, $400 million or so of capital investment."
- Growth that requires capital is still fine ("nothing shabby about earning $82 million pre-tax on $400 million of net tangible assets") but "it's far better to have an ever-increasing stream of earnings with virtually no major capital requirements. Ask Microsoft or Google."
- The worst business "grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money. Think airlines." Investors are "attracted by growth when they should have been repelled by it."
- How he judges investee stocks: not by market price in a given year but by the same two tests applied to owned businesses — improvement in earnings (allowing for industry conditions) and whether moats widened.
- Don't issue stock for acquisitions: Dexter cost not $400m but $3.5 billion because it was paid in Berkshire shares.
- "Confession time": all mistakes were "unforced errors" — no consultant or banker pushed him.
- Managers who have no financial need to work and have "exactly the job they want" bring a "here-today, here-forever mindset." Berkshire is "not big fans of resumes. Instead, we focus on brains, passion and integrity."
- Accounting: the options-expensing fight (1994 Senate vote 88-9; only 2 of 500 S&P companies chose to expense), options backdating, and pension return assumptions (8% average is unrealistic: 28% in bonds at ≤5% means equities must earn 9.2% after fees).
- The Dow math: 66 to 11,497 in the 20th century = 5.3%/yr; matching that requires Dow ~2,000,000 by 2099; 10% expectations imply 24,000,000. "Beware the glib helper who fills your head with fantasies while he fills his pockets with fees."
- Why passive investors must win as a class: everyone collectively earns the average minus costs; passive investors have very low costs; therefore active investors as a group must underperform by their higher costs. "The passive group – the 'know-nothings' – must win."
- Derivatives: Berkshire holds the money in all cases (no counterparty risk); mark-to-market accounting will cause big swings in reported earnings that "neither cheer nor bother" him; he trades short-run earnings volatility for long-run gains in net worth, as in catastrophe insurance.
- Insurance loss reserves are an "honor system" guesstimate; "in every report our estimate is sure to be wrong" (the "rented suit" story).
- Trade deficit / dollar: $2 billion/day of IOUs shipped abroad; a plunging dollar hadn't cured the trade deficit; sovereign wealth funds buying U.S. assets is "our doing." Currencies matter (Brazilian real position; Amazon euro bonds bought at 57% of par — "you can occasionally find markets that are ridiculously inefficient – or at least you can find them anywhere except at the finance departments of some leading business schools").
- "Start-ups are not our game" (Amex 1850, Wells 1852, P&G 1837, Coke 1886).

**Businesses/investments and why.**
- *See's Candy*: great — high and rising return on tiny capital; durable brand built over 50 years; boxed chocolate industry is unexciting (2% volume growth) yet See's earns ~half the industry's profits.
- *FlightSafety*: good — durable advantage ("Going to any other flight-training provider than the best is like taking the low bid on a surgical procedure") but $1.635 billion capex since 1996 vs $923m depreciation; $159m earnings gain on $509m incremental investment; "put-up-more-to-earn-more."
- *MidAmerican / regulated utilities*: in the "good" category — will earn considerably more in ten years but "will invest many billions to make it." Walter Scott, Dave Sokol, Greg Abel as partners; unanimous decisions.
- *Airlines / US Air preferred (1989)*: gruesome — insatiable capital demand, no durable advantage; "if a farsighted capitalist had been present at Kitty Hawk, he would have done his successors a huge favor by shooting Orville down." Got lucky selling in 1998; company went bankrupt twice thereafter.
- *TV stations* (the Dallas-Fort Worth NBC station Tom Murphy offered for $35m in 1972): "See's-like businesses that required virtually no capital investment and had excellent prospects for growth."
- *GEICO*: "widest moat of any of our insurers"; market share 2.5% (1995) to 7.2%; ad spend $31m to $751m; low-cost producer.
- *General Re*: the Tiffany reputation "outlived its factual underpinnings," which he "completely missed" in 1998; restored by Brandon and Montross.
- *Ajit Jain*: built a great specialty reinsurer from scratch; Equitas $7.1 billion single premium.
- *Marmon*: priced "using only Marmon's financial statements, employing no advisors and engaging in no nit-picking"; "a deal is a deal."
- *Clayton Homes*: most earnings come from an $11 billion loan portfolio; delinquencies stable despite 2007 mortgage turmoil.
- *PetroChina*: bought at an implied $37 billion valuation when he thought it worth ~$100 billion; sold at $275 billion market value when that matched his estimate relative to other oil majors.
- *Big four stock holdings* (Amex, Coke, P&G, Wells): judged by earnings growth (12%, 14%, 14%) and widened moats; Wells' earnings dipped but "I believe its intrinsic value increased."
- *Amazon euro bonds*: mispriced as junk; currency gain added $118m.

**Mistakes admitted.**
- Nearly "blew" See's by refusing to go above $25 million when the seller asked $30 million ("Otherwise I would have balked, and that $1.35 billion would have gone to somebody else"). Lesson: don't let a small price difference kill a great business purchase (also "I'm a 'one-price' guy" — contrasted with the MidAmerican $35.05 where he "wilted" and is glad he did).
- Turned down the Dallas-Fort Worth TV station at $35m that has since earned at least $1 billion and is worth ~$800m: "my brain had gone on vacation."
- Dexter (1993, $433m in Berkshire stock): "What I had assessed as durable competitive advantage vanished within a few years," and paying in stock compounded the error to $3.5 billion — "the worst deal that I've made." Lesson: misjudged moat durability; never use undervalued stock as currency.
- US Air preferred 1989.
- General Re's deteriorated culture in 1998: "a flaw that I completely missed."

### 2008 letter (written February 27, 2009)

**Context.** The financial-crisis letter. Net worth fell $11.5 billion; book value per share -9.6% — the worst year in Berkshire's 44-year table, though the S&P fell 37%. Credit markets went "non-functional"; by Q4 "paralyzing fear" produced a "freefall in business activity ... accelerating at a pace that I have never before witnessed." Treasury and Fed went "all in" (he predicts "an onslaught of inflation" as an aftereffect). Berkshire invested $14.5 billion in fixed-income securities of Wrigley, Goldman Sachs and GE with equity kickers; closed Marmon (64%); launched Berkshire Hathaway Assurance (muni bond insurer); bought 10% of BYD. Big mistake: ConocoPhillips bought near the oil peak; two Irish banks (-89%).

**Core teachings.**
- The four goals in good years and bad: (1) Gibraltar-like financial position with huge excess liquidity, modest near-term obligations and many earnings sources; (2) widen moats; (3) acquire new earnings streams; (4) nurture outstanding managers.
- He can't predict markets even when confident about the economy: "We're certain, for example, that the economy will be in shambles throughout 2009 – and, for that matter, probably well beyond – but that conclusion does not tell us whether the stock market will rise or fall." In 75% of past 44 years the S&P rose; a similar fraction will be positive in the next 44, but no one knows which.
- "When investing, pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy." Price declines in holdings "do not bother Charlie and me. Indeed, we enjoy such price declines if we have funds available to increase our positions."
- Graham's "Price is what you pay; value is what you get" — "Whether we're talking about socks or stocks, I like buying quality merchandise when it is marked down."
- Two-bucket valuation framework ("Yardsticks"): per-share investments ($77,793, down from $90,343) and per-share pre-tax earnings from non-insurance businesses ($3,921 vs $4,093). Insurance's value is counted via the float it generates. "We like buying underpriced securities, but we like buying fairly-priced operating businesses even more."
- Float "cost us less than zero" — Berkshire was paid $2.8 billion to hold $58.5 billion of float. Most insurers run underwriting losses; Berkshire's advantage is managers and "entrenched and valuable franchises."
- "Buyer of choice" for family businesses by deserving it: keep promises, avoid leveraging acquired businesses, grant autonomy, hold through thick and thin. Contrast with private equity ("LBO became a bad name. So in Orwellian fashion, the buyout firms decided to change their moniker"), which "almost invariably results in dramatic reductions in the equity portion."
- In regulated utilities, be the buyer of choice of *regulators*; reputation in existing jurisdictions determines welcome in new ones. MidAmerican has never paid a dividend; "in our utility business, we spend all we earn, and then some."
- Housing/lending lessons from Clayton: borrowers compared payments with "actual – not hoped-for – income" and intended to pay off the mortgage "whatever the course of home prices"; foreclosures happen because people can't make payments, not because of negative equity. Prescription: ≥10% honest down payment, payments comfortably handled by verified income. "Putting people into homes, though a desirable goal, shouldn't be our country's primary objective. Keeping them in their homes should be the ambition."
- Government guarantees distort credit: "it is much better to be a financial cripple with a government guarantee than a Gibraltar without one."
- Muni bond insurance: the historical low-default record reflects *uninsured* bonds; a universe of insured bonds would behave differently (NYC 1975 example — concessions came because local holders were hurt; if Berkshire had insured the bonds, it would be asked to "share"). Losses would be correlated across issuers. Tax-exempt insurance "has the look today of a dangerous business" like catastrophe insurance: a string of loss-free years followed by a wipeout.
- Beware history-based models: "If merely looking up past financial data would tell you what the future holds, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." Mortgage losses came from models calibrated on moderate-price-rise periods. "Beware of geeks bearing formulas."
- Liquidity pledge: "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations. When forced to choose, I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits." Sold J&J, P&G, Conoco to fund the preferred purchases.
- The pendulum "has gone from underpricing risk to overpricing it." The late-2008 Treasury bubble may rank with the Internet and housing bubbles. "Clinging to cash equivalents or long-term government bonds at present yields is almost certainly a terrible policy if continued for long." "Approval, though, is not the goal of investing ... Beware the investment activity that produces applause; the great moves are usually greeted by yawns."
- Derivatives are dangerous: they increased leverage, made banks un-analyzable, enabled Fannie/Freddie misstatements (OFHEO's 100+ staff missed it). "Auditors can't audit these contracts, and regulators can't regulate them." Transparency won't cure it. Counterparty webs: "It's not just whom you sleep with, but also whom they are sleeping with." First Law of Corporate Survival: "Modest incompetence simply won't do; it's mindboggling screw-ups that are required."
- Why Berkshire still holds 251 derivatives: each was "mispriced at inception"; Berkshire holds the money (no counterparty risk); derivatives "float" of $8.1 billion; minimal collateral posting (<1% of portfolio even in Q4 2008). "The CEO of any large financial organization must be the Chief Risk Officer as well. If we lose money on our derivatives, it will be my fault."
- Equity puts explained: $37.1 billion notional on four indices, 15–20 year terms, exercisable only at expiry, $4.9 billion premiums received; Black-Scholes liability $10 billion. To lose all $37.1 billion all four indices would have to go to zero; a 25% decline would cost ~$9 billion payable 2019–2028.
- Black-Scholes critique with the 100-year put example: formula says $2.5m premium for a $1 billion 100-year S&P put; realistic expectation of loss ~$5m; implies borrowing for 100 years at 0.7% (or 6.2% in the worst case) — "either my assumptions are crazy or the formula is inappropriate." Volatility from past price moves is "simply irrelevant" to the probability-weighted value of American business a century out (the manic-depressive neighbour quoting a farm daily). He still uses Black-Scholes because "CEOs who have concocted their own valuations for esoteric financial instruments have seldom erred on the side of conservatism."
- Mark-to-market vs accrual: the same muni risk insured as a derivative showed a $631m loss, while in BHAC under accrual accounting it would have shown a small profit; eventual results identical.
- American resilience: 20th century saw two wars, a dozen panics, 21.5% prime rate, the Depression; standard of living rose seven-fold; "America's best days lie ahead."

**Businesses/investments and why.**
- *GEICO*: low-cost producer; "GEICO grows because it saves money for motorists"; productivity 299 to 439 policies per employee in five years; share 2.0% (1993) to 7.7%; number three insurer; "two hungry mosquitoes in a nudist camp."
- *General Re*: a "business of long-term promises"; the only reinsurer backed by a AAA corporation ("It's difficult for an empty sack to stand upright").
- *Ajit's reinsurance*: 31 employees, "one of the most remarkable businesses in the world."
- *MidAmerican*: pipelines rose from 9th/39th to 1st/3rd in customer satisfaction; Iowa rates unchanged, wind leadership; "we spend all we earn, and then some."
- *Clayton*: sensible lending; FICO median 644 yet delinquencies 3.6%; "Jimmy Stewart would have loved these folks."
- *CTB*: bought for $140m in 2002, earned $89m pre-tax in 2008; Vic Mancinelli "focuses on blocking and tackling."
- *Iscar*: bought Tungaloy in November 2008; three managers "like winning the Triple Crown."
- *Moody's and BNSF*: moved to equity accounting after passing 20% (Moody's via its own buybacks).
- *Wrigley, Goldman, GE preferreds*: high current yields plus equity participation "as a bonus."
- *BHAC*: second-to-pay muni insurance at 3.3% vs the 1.5% he'd offered to be first-to-pay; "our guarantee is far more valuable than theirs."

**Mistakes admitted.**
- ConocoPhillips: "I bought a large amount of ConocoPhillips stock when oil and gas prices were near their peak ... the terrible timing of my purchase has cost Berkshire several billion dollars." A "major mistake of commission."
- Two Irish banks: $244m written down to $27m, an 89% loss.
- Errors of omission: "sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking."
- Underpriced his initial monoline offer (1.5%) — the rejection "proved to be very good news."
- Expected the high-yield credit contracts to be profitable; "the possibility of an eventual loss has increased."
- Gen Re's derivatives book: couldn't "get our minds around" 23,218 contracts; took five years and $400m+ to unwind. "I liked you better before I got to know you so well."

- "When investing, pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy." (2008)
- "Long ago, Ben Graham taught me that 'Price is what you pay; value is what you get.' Whether we're talking about socks or stocks, I like buying quality merchandise when it is marked down." (2008)
- "We're certain, for example, that the economy will be in shambles throughout 2009 – and, for that matter, probably well beyond – but that conclusion does not tell us whether the stock market will rise or fall." (2008)
- "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations. When forced to choose, I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits." (2008)
- "The investment world has gone from underpricing risk to overpricing it. This change has not been minor; the pendulum has covered an extraordinary arc." (2008)

### 2009 letter (written February 26, 2010)

**Context.** Net worth up $21.8 billion; book value per share +19.8% (S&P +26.5%). The headline event is the acquisition of the rest of Burlington Northern Santa Fe (BNSF) for ~$100/share, 40% in Berkshire stock (overall ~30% of total cost in stock, since Berkshire already owned 22.5%); the B shares were split 50-for-1. The letter is framed as a "freshman orientation" for the 65,000 new BNSF shareholders. Berkshire supplied $15.5 billion of capital at the peak of the crisis ($9 billion to Goldman, GE, etc.; $6.5 billion for Wrigley). NetJets lost $711 million and was rescued by Dave Sokol; the GEICO credit card was a ~$50 million fiasco; Berkadia (50% with Leucadia) was formed. Cash fell from $44.3 billion (start 2008) to $30.6 billion.

**Core teachings.**
- How to measure yourself: pick "a rational and unbending standard" at the outset so you don't "paint the bull's eye" around where the arrow lands. The S&P is the bogey because shareholders can match it at no cost via an index fund. Ideal measure is change in per-share intrinsic value; since that can't be calculated precisely, book value is the "crude proxy."
- Book value understates intrinsic value, hugely so in insurance; in 1965 Berkshire sold at a discount to the book value of "underearning textile assets," now at a premium to the book of first-class businesses (hence market gain 22% vs book 20.3%).
- Berkshire's "defense has been better than our offense": it beat the S&P in all 11 negative years and in every one of 41 rolling five-year periods, but "huge sums forge their own anchor" and future advantage "will be a small fraction of our historical edge."
- "What We Don't Do" — Munger's inversion ("All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there"; Jacobi's "Invert, always invert"):
  - Avoid businesses whose futures can't be evaluated no matter how exciting the product — autos (1910), aircraft (1930), TV (1950) all had obvious growth and "competitive dynamics that would decimate almost all of the companies entering those industries." Seeing industry growth is not the same as judging margins and returns on capital. "At Berkshire we will stick with businesses whose profit picture for decades to come seems reasonably predictable."
  - Never depend on the kindness of strangers; "Too-big-to-fail is not a fallback position at Berkshire." Hold $20 billion+ of cash and pay the price in low yields ("But we sleep well").
  - Let subsidiaries run themselves, accepting "the visible costs of a few bad decisions" over "the many invisible costs that come from decisions made too slowly – or not at all – because of a stifling bureaucracy." Headquarters limits itself to "allocating capital, controlling enterprise risk, choosing managers and setting their compensation."
  - Don't woo Wall Street; seek partners who understand the business and concur with its policies; communicate as "we would like to know if our positions were reversed"; release results on weekends.
- Insurance economics tutorial: collect-now, pay-later yields float that stays stable relative to premiums; underwriting profit means "get paid for holding" free money; the industry as a whole runs underwriting losses because competition for float is intense; Berkshire's float $16m (1967) to $62 billion, seven straight underwriting-profit years.
- The GEICO purchase joke ("Damn, I knew I should have waited") — paying 50x the original price for the second half was still right; the low-cost producer wins.
- Capital-intensive businesses now acceptable: "In earlier days, Charlie and I shunned capital-intensive businesses such as public utilities. Indeed, the best businesses by far for owners continue to be those that have high returns on capital and that require little incremental investment to grow." But given ever-increasing cash, Berkshire will enter businesses requiring large capex if they can earn "decent returns on the incremental sums they invest." Result: "above-average, though certainly not spectacular, returns."
- The "social compact" with regulators for utilities and BNSF: provide essential service and invest ahead of demand; expect fair allowed returns. "If either side shirks its obligations, both sides will inevitably suffer."
- Housing supply/demand: starts of ~2m/yr vs household formation ~1.2m created the overhang; cure is starts far below formations (2009 starts 554,000 — "paradoxically, this is good news").
- Big opportunities: "I should have done far more. Big opportunities come infrequently. When it's raining gold, reach for a bucket, not a thimble."
- The definition of what matters: "In the end, what counts in investing is what you pay for a business – through the purchase of a small piece of it in the stock market – and what that business earns in the succeeding decade or two."
- "A climate of fear is their best friend. Those who invest only when commentators are upbeat end up paying a heavy price for meaningless reassurance."
- Risk control cannot be delegated: "If Berkshire ever gets in trouble, it will be my fault. It will not be because of misjudgments made by a Risk Committee or Chief Risk Officer." Boards of big financial institutions are "derelict" if the CEO doesn't bear full responsibility; failed CEOs and directors should "pay a heavy price – one not reimbursable by the companies they've damaged nor by insurance."
- Issuing stock in acquisitions ("An Inconvenient Truth (Boardroom Overheating)"): "If we wouldn't dream of selling Berkshire in its entirety at the current market price, why in the world should we 'sell' a significant part of the company at that same inadequate price by issuing our stock in a merger?" You "can't exchange an undervalued stock for a fully-valued one without hurting your shareholders." Worked example of A and B both worth $100 trading at $80: 1.25 shares of A for B costs A's holders $125 of intrinsic value; the CEO "now runs a company twice as large." Overvalued stock as currency is "counterfeit money" ("air-for-assets" deals of the late 1960s).
- Bankers never discuss the true value of what is being *given*; directors should hire a second advisor to argue against the deal with a fee contingent on it not happening. "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut."
- The bank acquisition story: "We need to show that we are in the hunt. Besides, it's only a small deal." Munger: "Are we supposed to applaud because the dog that fouls our lawn is a Chihuahua rather than a Saint Bernard?"
- Derivatives float of $6.3 billion is separate from insurance float; "wild swings neither cheer nor bother Charlie and me."
- "We eat our own cooking" at NetJets — same planes, same prices.

**Businesses/investments and why.**
- *BNSF*: economics resemble electric utilities — essential service, heavy investment far exceeding depreciation for decades, wise regulators needed, logical user of non-guaranteed debt; run by Matt Rose. Paid partly in stock, "a close one"; more stock would have made "no sense."
- *GEICO*: share 2.5% to 8.1%; float $2.7b to $9.6b; underwriting profit in 13 of 14 years; low-cost producer status.
- *Ajit Jain*: "If Charlie, I and Ajit are ever in a sinking boat – and you can only save one of us – swim to Ajit."
- *General Re*: "a gleaming jewel"; 100% of Cologne Re.
- *MidAmerican*: Iowa and Western utilities earned $2.5b in three years while spending $3b on wind; never paid a dividend.
- *McLane*: record $344m pre-tax on $31.2b sales — ~1¢ per dollar; "McLane's prime asset, however, is Grady."
- *Marmon, Iscar*: strong in a depressed industrial sector; "Nothing stops Israel-based Iscar – not wars, recessions or competitors."
- *NetJets*: operational success, financial failure — $157m aggregate pre-tax loss over 11 years, debt from $102m to $1.9b; "It's clear that I failed you."

### 2011 letter (written February 25, 2012)

**Context.** Book value per share +4.6% (S&P +2.1%). The letter contains the essay "The Basic Choices for Investors and the One We Strongly Prefer."

**Core teachings.**
- Definition of investing: "the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power – after taxes have been paid on nominal gains – in the future. More succinctly, investing is forgoing consumption now in order to have the ability to consume more at a later date."
- Risk redefined: "The riskiness of an investment is not measured by beta ... but rather by the probability – the reasoned probability – of that investment causing its owner a loss of purchasing-power over his contemplated holding period." Fluctuating assets need not be risky; "a non-fluctuating asset can be laden with risk."
- Three investment categories:
  1. *Currency-based* (money-market funds, bonds, mortgages, deposits): "thought of as 'safe.' In truth they are among the most dangerous of assets. Their beta may be zero, but their risk is huge." The dollar lost 86% since 1965 ($7 today buys what $1 did); a tax-free institution needed 4.3% just to stay even; T-bills returned 5.7% but after a 25% income tax (1.4 points) and the inflation "tax" (4.3 points) a taxpayer earned nothing real — the inflation tax was triple the income tax. "Right now bonds should come with a warning label." Berkshire still holds T-bills for liquidity ($20b working level, $10b minimum) and buys other currency securities only when mispriced (junk-bond debacles) or when rates are high enough to offer capital gains. Shelby Cullom Davis: "Bonds promoted as offering risk-free returns are now priced to deliver return-free risk."
  2. *Non-productive assets* bought in the hope someone will pay more (tulips; gold): requires "an expanding pool of buyers"; gold is "neither of much use nor procreative" — "if you own one ounce of gold for an eternity, you will still own one ounce at its end." Rising prices create bandwagon buyers who "create their own truth – for a while." Pile A (all the world's gold, a 68-foot cube, $9.6 trillion) vs Pile B (all U.S. cropland producing $200b/yr plus 16 Exxon Mobils plus $1 trillion walking-around money). "You can fondle the cube, but it will not respond." "What the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end."
  3. *Productive assets* — businesses, farms, real estate — ideally ones that "in inflationary times ... deliver output that will retain its purchasing-power value while requiring a minimum of new capital investment." Coca-Cola, IBM, See's pass this "double-barreled test"; regulated utilities fail it ("To earn more, their owners must invest more") yet are still superior to the other two categories. The "commercial 'cows'" metaphor: value "determined not by the medium of exchange but rather by their capacity to deliver milk." This category "will prove to be the runaway winner" and "by far the safest."
- The first two categories "enjoy maximum popularity at peaks of fear": "cash is king" in late 2008 when cash should have been deployed; "cash is trash" in the early 1980s when bonds were most attractive. "Investors who required a supportive crowd paid dearly for that comfort."
- Share repurchases: favoured when (1) the company has ample funds for operations and liquidity and (2) "its stock is selling at a material discount to the company's intrinsic business value, conservatively calculated." Buying back to offset dilution or because of excess cash doesn't suffice: "Continuing shareholders are hurt unless shares are purchased below intrinsic value. The first law of capital allocation – whether the money is slated for acquisitions or share repurchases – is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another." (Praises Jamie Dimon's letters on this.) Mixed emotions about "cashing out partners at a discount"; wants exiting partners fully informed. No interest in "supporting the stock"; bids fade in weak markets; no buybacks if cash < $20b.
- The IBM buyback arithmetic: as a long-term owner of a repurchasing company, you should "wish for IBM's stock price to languish throughout the five years." At $200 average IBM buys 250m shares (Berkshire's stake to ~7%); at $300 it buys 167m (stake 6.5%); on $20b of fifth-year earnings Berkshire's share is $100m greater under the low-price scenario. "If you are going to be a net buyer of stocks in the future, either directly with your own money or indirectly ... you are hurt when stocks rise. You benefit when stocks swoon." The commuter rejoicing at higher gas prices. "'Talking our book' about a stock we own – were that to be effective – would actually be harmful to Berkshire."
- Graham's Chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor* (on price fluctuations): "Immediately the scales fell from my eyes, and low prices became my friend. Picking up that book was one of the luckiest moments in my life."
- Big Four as "partnership interests in wonderful businesses, not as marketable securities to be bought or sold based on their near-term prospects." Only dividends ($862m) show in reported earnings; Berkshire's share of earnings was $3.3b; the unreported $2.4b "creates at least that amount of value."
- Book value as proxy: "This yardstick is meaningless at most companies. At Berkshire, however, book value very roughly tracks business values" because the excess of intrinsic over book "does not swing wildly."
- Float in valuation: float is deducted in full as a liability in book value "just as if we had to pay it out tomorrow and were unable to replenish it. But that's an incorrect way to view float, which should instead be viewed as a revolving fund. If float is both costless and long-enduring, the true value of this liability is far lower than the accounting liability." Insurance goodwill ($15.5b) is "the price we paid for the float-generating capabilities"; its cost "has no bearing on its true value" — if underwriting losses are sustained, "any goodwill asset attributable to it should be deemed valueless."
- Float unlikely to grow much from $70b; any decline would be gradual.

- The five ways to build per-share intrinsic value: (1) improve subsidiaries' earning power; (2) bolt-on acquisitions; (3) growth of investees; (4) repurchase shares at a meaningful discount to intrinsic value; (5) occasional large acquisitions — "rarely, if ever, issuing Berkshire shares." The powerhouse five's $9.7b earnings gain came with only 6.1% dilution: "our goal of not simply growing, but rather increasing per-share results."
- Bolt-ons are loved because "usually they are low-risk, burden headquarters not at all, and expand the scope of our proven managers."
- Prefer "owning a non-controlling but substantial portion of a wonderful business to owning 100% of a so-so business"; capital-allocation flexibility is "a significant advantage over companies that limit themselves only to acquisitions they can operate."
- Unreported investee earnings ($2.8b of the Big Four's $3.9b) are "every bit as valuable to us as what we record"; retained earnings fund buybacks (raising Berkshire's stake) and advantageous opportunities.
- On "uncertainty": CEOs who shelve projects over short-term worries are making a mistake; Berkshire spent a record $9.8b; "Every Storm Runs Out of Rain." "American business will do fine over time. And stocks will do well just as certainly, since their fate is tied to business performance." "It's a terrible mistake to try to dance in and out of it based upon the turn of tarot cards, the predictions of 'experts,' or the ebb and flow of business activity. The risks of being out of the game are huge compared to the risks of being in it." His first purchase in spring 1942 amid Pacific war losses; GDP per capita quadrupled 1941–2012.
- Intrinsic value: can't be pinpointed "for Berkshire shares (or, for that matter, any other stock)"; two quantitative pillars updated (investments $113,786/share; non-insurance pre-tax earnings $8,085/share, both +15.7%); since 1970 these grew 19.4% and 20.8% and "it is no coincidence that the price of Berkshire stock over the 42-year period has increased at a rate very similar."
- Float as a "revolving fund" (repeated) — "That's like your taking out a loan and having the bank pay you interest." Industry premiums were inadequate in 37 of the 45 years ending 2011. New warning: insurers' "legacy" bond portfolios "are, in effect, wasting assets" — earnings will be hurt as higher-yielding bonds roll over.
- GEICO: a one-point sustained gain in persistency "increases intrinsic value by more than $1 billion"; a $410m accounting charge "changing neither cash, revenues, expenses nor taxes" only widened the gap between intrinsic and carrying value.
- Interest coverage defined as "pre-tax earnings/interest, not EBITDA/interest, a commonly-used measure we view as deeply flawed."
- Regulated businesses: society "will forever need massive investment in both transportation and energy"; "It is in the self-interest of governments to treat capital providers in a manner that will ensure the continued flow of funds."
- Amortization of intangibles: some intangibles "truly deplete over time while others never lose value" — software amortization is a real expense; "amortization of customer relationships" from purchase accounting is "clearly not real." Berkshire counts ~20% of its $600m amortization as real. Wells Fargo's ~$1.5b "amortization of core deposits" charge is not an expense in any sense "except GAAP accounting" even though core deposits grow; IBM's "adjusted operating earnings" exclude such items and "analysts focus on this number, as they should."
- Munger's wonderful-business-at-a-fair-price lesson: "More than 50 years ago, Charlie told me that it was far better to buy a wonderful business at a fair price than to buy a fair business at a wonderful price. Despite the compelling logic of his position, I have sometimes reverted to my old habit of bargain-hunting, with results ranging from tolerable to terrible."
- MSR group earned 16.3% after-tax on $22.6b net tangible assets — but "a business with terrific economics can be a bad investment if the price paid is excessive"; intrinsic value exceeds carrying value, though "the huge winners reside" in insurance and regulated segments.
- Marmon accounting: the 10% step-up required a $700m write-off; the purchase price implies $12.6b for 90% vs $8b carrying value — a $4.6b gap.
- Liquidity philosophy: "Charlie and I believe in operating with many redundant layers of liquidity, and we avoid any sort of obligation that could drain our cash in a material way. That reduces our returns in 99 years out of 100. But we will survive in the 100th while many others fail. And we will sleep well in all 100."
- Newspapers: "News ... is what people don't know that they want to know"; the old economics ("survival of the fattest"; a monopoly paper's profits were extraordinary "whether it was managed well or poorly"); primacy lost in most categories but not local news; giving papers away online while charging for print was "a faulty business strategy"; buy "at a very low multiple of current earnings"; cash earnings "will almost certainly trend downward"; closed one unprofitable paper under Principle 11.
- **Dividend policy essay** — the hierarchy of uses of earnings: (1) reinvest in the current business to widen the moat (managers "misfire" when they "start with the answer they want and then work backwards" — his own textile sin: "wishing makes dreams come true only in Disney movies; it's poison in business"); (2) acquisitions unrelated to current businesses — test: "Do Charlie and I think we can effect a transaction that is likely to leave our shareholders wealthier on a per-share basis than they were prior to the acquisition?" (BNSF is the proof — worth far more than carrying value; shareholders would be worse off had the funds gone to dividends/buybacks); (3) repurchases when shares sell "at a meaningful discount to conservatively calculated intrinsic value" — "the surest way to use funds intelligently: It's hard to go wrong when you're buying dollar bills for 80¢ or less"; limit raised from 110% to 120% of book; "price is all-important. Value is destroyed when purchases are made above intrinsic value"; (4) dividends.
- The dividend vs "sell-off" arithmetic: a $2m-net-worth business earning 12% on net worth, shares always purchasable at 125% of book. Paying out one-third ($80k) and retaining two-thirds grows value and dividends 8%/yr: after ten years net worth $4,317,850, dividend $86,357, each owner's shares worth $2,698,656. Alternative: retain everything and each owner sells 3.2% of shares annually (same initial $40k cash): net worth compounds at 12% to $6,211,696; each owns 36.12%, worth $2,804,425 — about 4% more value and 4% more annual cash. Assumptions (12% ROE, 125% of book) are below what the S&P delivers and "reasonable for Berkshire, though certainly not assured." Two further arguments: dividends force one cash-out ratio on all 600,000 shareholders, many of whom are net savers who "logically should prefer no payment at all" (reinvesting dividends means paying tax plus a 25% premium); and tax — dividends are fully taxed while the sell-off is taxed only on the gain portion. Buffett's own case: has given away ~4.25% of shares annually for seven years, yet the book value of his stake rose from $28.2b (2005) to $40.2b (2012).
- "Above all, dividend policy should always be clear, consistent and rational. A capricious policy will confuse owners and drive away would-be investors." Phil Fisher's hamburger/Chinese restaurant analogy (Chapter 7 of *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*, a book "that ranks behind only The Intelligent Investor and the 1940 edition of Security Analysis"). "We like increased dividends, and we love repurchases at appropriate prices." Berkshire will keep its no-dividend policy "as long as we believe our assumptions about the book-value buildup and the market-price premium seem reasonable."
- Information fairness: results released after Friday's close; no one-on-ones with institutions; wants a "credentialed bear" on the analyst panel.
- Recommends *The Outsiders* (Thorndike) on capital-allocating CEOs; Tom Murphy "overall the best business manager I've ever met"; Bogle's *The Clash of the Cultures*.

**Businesses/investments and why.**
- *Heinz*: partnership with Lemann, "an extraordinary manager"; preferred at 9% with redemption premium and warrants.
- *GEICO*: share 2.5% to 9.7%; premiums $2.8b to $16.7b; "When I count my blessings, I count GEICO twice."

## Part 2: Cross-cutting synthesis, 2007–2012

### 1. What investing IS

- The formal definition arrives in 2011: investing is "forgoing consumption now in order to have the ability to consume more at a later date" — measured in after-tax *purchasing power*, not nominal dollars (2011). The practical restatement: "what counts in investing is what you pay for a business – through the purchase of a small piece of it in the stock market – and what that business earns in the succeeding decade or two" (2009).
- A stock is a partnership interest in a business, not a trading instrument: the Big Four are "partnership interests in wonderful businesses, not ... marketable securities to be bought or sold based on their near-term prospects" (2011). Investee progress is judged by earnings growth and moat-widening, not price (2007). Undistributed investee earnings are as valuable as dividends received and historically show up in market value "albeit in a highly irregular manner" (2010, 2011, 2012).
- Time horizon: "At Berkshire, our time horizon is forever" (2010); "Time is the friend of the wonderful business" (2010); a Coke stake whose annual earnings share may exceed its original cost (2010); See's 40 years and $1.65b on $25m (2011).
- Risk is not volatility/beta but "the reasoned probability" of losing purchasing power over the holding period (2011); the ability to value a business, not an option, is "what investing is all about" (2010).
- Market timing is a "terrible mistake"; the game is "heavily stacked" in the investor's favour; "the risks of being out of the game are huge compared to the risks of being in it" (2012). He explicitly disclaims the ability to forecast markets even while forecasting the economy (2008, 2009).
- Passive investing wins as a class because of costs; "the 'know-nothings' – must win" (2007). The S&P is the right bogey because any shareholder can own it at no cost (2009, 2010, 2012).

### 2. What to look for in a business

- The four filters: understandable; favourable long-term economics; able, trustworthy management; sensible price (2007). Predictability of profits "for decades to come" is required; exciting growth industries (autos, aircraft, TV) are avoided because competitive dynamics, not demand, determine returns (2009).
- An enduring moat protecting high returns on invested capital is the defining trait; low-cost producer (GEICO, Costco) or worldwide brand (Coke, Gillette, Amex). Industries of rapid change are excluded ("A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all"), as are businesses dependent on a superstar manager (2007).
- Capital intensity is the sorting variable. *Great*: high and rising returns on little tangible capital with no need to reinvest (See's — $40m capital supporting $82m pre-tax; TV stations; Microsoft/Google; "Ask Microsoft or Google"). *Good*: attractive returns that require continuous reinvestment (FlightSafety, utilities, BNSF). *Gruesome*: growth that needs capital and earns little (airlines) (2007). Metrics he uses: pre-tax return on invested capital (See's 60% in 1972), earnings on unleveraged net tangible assets (MSR group: 25% to >100% for the best, 12–20% for the good, 16.3% after tax in aggregate in 2012) (2010, 2011, 2012).
- Growth is only valuable if it earns decent returns on incremental capital; investors should be "repelled," not attracted, by capital-hungry growth (2007). He now accepts capital-intensive regulated businesses because Berkshire's cash flow needs a home and they earn "decent returns on the incremental sums they invest," but says plainly these yield "above-average, though certainly not spectacular" results (2009).
- Inflation test (2011): the ideal business delivers output that keeps its purchasing-power value "while requiring a minimum of new capital investment" — Coke, IBM, See's pass; utilities fail.
- "Buy commodities, sell brands" (2011); "survival of the fattest" for newspapers; a monopoly local paper was great "whether it was managed well or poorly" — until the Internet removed primacy (2012).
- Franchise durability examples: Amex 1850, Wells 1852, P&G 1837, Coke 1886 — "Start-ups are not our game" (2007). Low-cost/efficiency moats: GEICO's policies per employee (2008); BNSF's three-times fuel efficiency vs trucks (2010, 2012).
- Essential-service regulated businesses operate under a "social compact": heavy investment ahead of demand in exchange for fair allowed returns (2009, 2010, 2011, 2012).

### 3. What to look for in management

- Owner-orientation and love of the business over money: managers who "have no financial need to work," "volunteers, not mercenaries," with a "here-today, here-forever mindset" (2007, 2010, 2012). "Brains, passion and integrity" over resumes (2007).
- "Hire well, manage little": trust in people rather than process; accept visible costs of a few bad decisions over invisible bureaucratic costs; HQ keeps capital allocation, risk control, choosing managers and setting compensation (2009, 2010).
- Candour and reputation: the managers' memo — lose money but never "a shred of reputation"; the unfriendly-reporter front-page test; "Everybody else is doing it" is never a justification; report bad news immediately (Salomon lesson); "Culture, more than rule books, determines how an organization behaves" (2010).
- Capital allocation skill is the key judgment about a CEO ("what-will-they-do-with-the-money"; Sears vs Sam Walton) (2010). IBM praised for debt used wisely, cash acquisitions and aggressive buybacks (2011); Jamie Dimon praised for price-conscious buybacks (2011); Tom Murphy "the best business manager I've ever met" (2012).
- Institutional imperative: insurers that "can't turn their back on business that their competitors are eagerly writing" (2010, 2011, 2012); bankers behaving "like teenage boys who had just discovered girls" (2009); CEOs who "start with the answer they want and then work backwards" (2012); CEOs opting for high pension-return assumptions and refusing to expense options (2007).
- Compensation: directors with token pay, no options, no D&O insurance (2010); investment managers paid on relative-to-S&P performance with deferrals and 80/20 own/partner splits to foster cooperation; hedge-fund GPs who walk away rich make clients "patsies, not partners" (2010, 2011).
- Accountability: the CEO must be the Chief Risk Officer; boards of financial institutions are "derelict" otherwise; failed CEOs and directors should pay a heavy, non-reimbursable price (2008, 2009).
- Managers of acquired family businesses are promised retention "through thick and thin"; Berkshire's "far from Darwinian" approach is a deliberate reputational asset with sellers (2008, 2011).

### 4. Valuation

- Intrinsic value cannot be calculated precisely ("Present that task to Charlie and me separately, and you will get two different answers") (2010, 2012). Book value serves as an understated proxy for Berkshire because the premium of intrinsic over book "seldom swings wildly" — but "this yardstick is meaningless at most companies" (2009, 2010, 2011).
- The three pillars of Berkshire's intrinsic value: per-share investments; per-share pre-tax earnings of non-insurance businesses; and the qualitative efficacy of future retained-earnings deployment (2010; updated 2011, 2012). Stock price has compounded at almost exactly the rate of the two quantitative measures over 40+ years (2010, 2012).
- Price vs value: "Price is what you pay; value is what you get" (2008); "what is smart at one price is dumb at another" (2011); "a business with terrific economics can be a bad investment if the price paid is excessive" (2012). Munger's wonderful-business-at-fair-price rule vs bargain-hunting (2012).
- Economic goodwill measured against the accounting figure: GEICO's $2.7b goodwill in 1996 = 97% of premium volume; now ~$14b of economic goodwill vs $1.4b carried (2010). Insurance goodwill = price paid for float-generating capability; worthless if underwriting losses are sustained, worth "far in excess" of cost at Berkshire (2011, 2012). Float should be valued as a revolving fund, not a liability due tomorrow (2011, 2012).
- Marmon's purchase-formula price implies $12.6b for 90% vs $8b carrying value (2012). BNSF "worth considerably more than our carrying value" (2012).
- Dividend/sell-off arithmetic uses explicit inputs — return on net worth (12%), payout ratio and the price/book multiple at which shares trade (125%) — to compare value creation (2012).
- Discount rates are not formally discussed; the closest is the 100-year put example in which an option premium is converted into an implied borrowing cost (0.7% or 6.2%) to show Black-Scholes is wrong for long-dated options (2008), and the 2011 inflation arithmetic (4.3% needed just to stay even since 1965).
- Issuing stock: value is destroyed when an undervalued stock is exchanged for a fully valued one; use the intrinsic value of what you give, not its market price (2009). Buybacks only below conservatively calculated intrinsic value (2011, 2012).
- Market inefficiency exists ("ridiculously inefficient" Amazon bonds; "mispriced at inception" derivatives) and should be exploited when "raining gold" (2007, 2008, 2009).

### 5. Temperament and psychology

- "Pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy"; enjoy price declines when you have cash (2008). "A climate of fear is their best friend" (2009). Low prices "became my friend" after Graham Chapter 8; net buyers should "wish for" languishing prices (2011).
- Approval is not the goal; "the great moves are usually greeted by yawns" (2008). Crowds at peaks of fear flee to cash or gold ("cash is king" / "cash is trash") and pay dearly for comfort (2011).
- Inversion as a thinking tool ("Invert, always invert"; know where you'll die and don't go there) and defining "What We Don't Do" (2009).
- Patience and inactivity: "we buy securities and bide our time" (2010); yearly results are not synchronized with the earth's orbit (2010); GEICO ad spend sacrifices short-term results (2010).
- Concentration: 75% of his $9,800 portfolio in GEICO in 1951 "(Even so, I felt over-diversified)" (2010); >98% of his net worth in Berkshire "defies conventional wisdom. But I'm fine with this arrangement" (2011).
- Circle of competence: avoid what can't be evaluated, however exciting (2009); "we knew we could not get our minds around" Gen Re's derivatives book, so exited (2008); IBM took 50 years of reading to "crystallize" (2011).
- Sleep-well-at-night over extra return: "I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits" (2008); "we will sleep well in all 100" (2012).
- Humility about forecasting: housing recovery call was "dead wrong" (2011); "I'll make more mistakes in the future – you can bet on that" (2007).

### 6. Accounting and financial-statement lessons

- *Net income is meaningless at Berkshire* because realized gains flow through earnings while unrealized don't; management could legally make it "almost any number"; follow operating earnings and book-value change (2010). Berkshire separates derivatives swings and realized gains in its reporting (2009).
- *Mark-to-market vs accrual*: identical economic risks (muni insurance as derivative vs in BHAC) produce a $631m loss vs a small profit in the same year; results converge eventually (2008). Equity-put liability rose by $700m in a quarter in which the actual settlement value fell (2010).
- *Black-Scholes*: "holy writ" that gives "absurd results" for long-dated options because historical volatility is irrelevant to century-long business values; still used for financial statements because any home-made valuation would be viewed with suspicion and auditors need consistency with counterparties (2008, 2010).
- *Goodwill*: written down when economic value falls, never written up (GEICO carried at $1.4b vs ~$14b economic value) (2010). Insurance goodwill vs float (2011, 2012). Equity-method accounting kicks in at 20% ownership (Moody's, BNSF) (2008). Marmon's step-ups required $614m and $700m write-offs "retroactive" — "Don't ask!" (2011, 2012).
- *Amortization of intangibles*: distinguish real (software) from "non-real" (customer relationships, Wells Fargo's $1.5b core-deposit amortization); Berkshire counts ~20% of its purchase-accounting amortization as a real expense; IBM's adjusted earnings are the right focus (2012).
- *EBITDA*: interest coverage is pre-tax earnings/interest, "not EBITDA/interest, a commonly-used measure we view as deeply flawed" (2012).
- *Stock options*: the 1994 Senate 88-9 vote; only 2 of 500 S&P companies expensed; backdating scandal (2007).
- *Pensions*: an 8% assumed return is implausible given 28% in bonds at ≤5% and a 5.3% historical equity appreciation plus 2% dividends minus fees; CEOs choose high assumptions to report higher earnings; public pension promises are a time bomb (2007, 2008).
- *Loss reserves* are an "honor system" guess that is "sure to be wrong"; the "rented suit" story (2007).
- *Equity accounting of investees*: reported earnings include only dividends; share of retained earnings ($2b+, $2.4b, $2.8b) is unreported but real (2010, 2011, 2012).
- *Back-tested models*: projecting uninsured-bond loss history onto insured bonds; mortgage models built on benign periods — "Beware of geeks bearing formulas" (2008).
- *Derivatives disclosure*: "Auditors can't audit these contracts, and regulators can't regulate them" (2008).
- *Segment presentation*: four sectors presented separately because "lumping them together impedes analysis" (every year).
- *Formulae given*: economic goodwill ≈ 97% of annual premium volume for GEICO-quality customers (2010); passive-beats-active-as-a-class cost logic (2007); Dow 5.3%/yr compounding → 2,000,000 by 2099 (2007); the 12%-ROE / 125%-of-book dividend-vs-sell-off model (2012); the A/B merger dilution example (2009); the IBM buyback share-count example (2011).

### 7. Capital allocation at the company level

- The 2012 hierarchy: reinvest to widen the moat → unrelated acquisitions that raise per-share wealth → buybacks below intrinsic value → dividends. Berkshire "will also try to maximize results for you by rarely, if ever, issuing Berkshire shares" (2012).
- Retained-earnings test, in substance: retained earnings are only worth retaining if deployed at adequate returns; "Some companies will turn these retained dollars into fifty-cent pieces, others into two-dollar bills" (2010). MidAmerican and BNSF retain everything and earn allowed returns on it (2008–2012).
- Berkshire's edge is having "no institutional restraints" and comparing businesses against each other and against securities (2010); taking cash from See's/Business Wire to buy BNSF (2010). Berkshire paid no dividends and did no buybacks for 40 years to 2010 (2010), then authorised buybacks at 110% (2011) and 120% (2012) of book value, with a $20b cash floor.
- Buybacks: two conditions — ample funds and a material discount to conservative intrinsic value; not to offset dilution or because of excess cash; "what is smart at one price is dumb at another"; no "supporting the stock" (2011). Repurchases below value are "the surest way to use funds intelligently" (2012). Long-term owners of repurchasing companies should hope for low prices (2011).
- Dividends: forced uniform cash-out and worse taxes vs a shareholder-chosen "sell-off"; policy must be "clear, consistent and rational" (2012).
- Acquisitions: don't issue undervalued stock; bankers never value what is given; hire a contrarian advisor paid only if the deal dies (2009). Bolt-ons are low-risk and expand proven managers' domains (2011, 2012). Be the "buyer of choice" by deserving it — no leveraging acquirees, autonomy, permanence (2008). Size "forges its own anchor" (2009, 2010).
- Leverage and liquidity: "Life and Debt" — avoid any zero; $10b minimum / $20b working cash in T-bills; no bank lines; no collateral-posting contracts; redundant liquidity costs "a minor amount" but enabled $15.6b of purchases in 25 days after Lehman (2010, 2011, 2012). Debt at utilities/railroad is non-recourse to Berkshire and amply covered (2010–2012). Private equity's debt piles vs Berkshire's model (2008).
- Capex through the cycle: record capital spending in 2010, 2011, 2012 regardless of "uncertainty" talk (2010–2012).

### 8. Insurance and float

- Float mechanics restated every year: collect-now-pay-later; float stable relative to premiums; underwriting profit means being paid to hold free money; the industry as a whole runs underwriting losses (State Farm lost money in 7 of 10 / 8 of 11 years; industry premiums inadequate in 37 of 45 years) so cost-free float is a Berkshire-specific outcome (2008–2012). Float grew $16m (1967) → $58.5b (2008) → $62b (2009) → $66b (2010) → $70.6b (2011) → $73b (2012); ten consecutive underwriting-profit years totalling $18.6b (2012).
- Float in valuation: a revolving fund, not a liability due tomorrow; its true cost is far lower than book shows (2011, 2012). Insurance goodwill = price of float-generating capacity (2011, 2012).
- The four disciplines of sound underwriting and the primacy of walking away (2010, 2011, 2012). Reserving is an honor-system estimate that will be wrong (2007). Catastrophe insurance: trade short-run earnings volatility for long-run net worth (2007); a $250b industry mega-cat would still leave Berkshire profitable (2011, 2012).
- Muni bond insurance (BHAC): historical defaults reflect uninsured bonds; insured universes behave differently and losses are correlated; second-to-pay at 3.3% beat first-to-pay at 1.5% (2008).
- Derivatives treated as insurance: premiums received up front create "derivatives float" ($8.1b, $6.3b, $4.2b); no counterparty risk; minimal collateral; credit contracts earned an underwriting profit through the crisis "because we charged the right premium" (2008–2012). New collateral rules end new positions (2011, 2012).
- Legacy bond portfolios are "wasting assets" for insurers as yields fall (2012). Float is unlikely to grow much further (2011), yet grew anyway (2012).
- GEICO's economics: low cost → low price → share growth (2.5% to 9.7%); one point of persistency = $1b+ of intrinsic value; economic goodwill ~$14b vs $1.4b carried (2008–2012). Ajit: billion-dollar limits retained, $35b float from nothing (2008–2012). General Re: restored after a culture collapse Buffett "totally failed to detect" (2007, 2008).

### 9. Mistakes and what changed in his thinking

- Dexter (1993) is named "the worst deal that I've made" — a misjudged moat compounded by paying in stock (2007). US Air preferred (1989) is "foolishness" (2007). Textiles: years of reinvestment plus buying a second textile company — "Aaaaaaargh!" (2010, 2012). Gen Re's culture and derivatives book "completely missed" (2007, 2008).
- Crisis-era errors: ConocoPhillips at the oil peak ("several billion dollars"), two Irish banks (-89%), thumb-sucking errors of omission (2008); not buying enough cheap bonds — "reach for a bucket, not a thimble" (2009); Energy Future Holdings bonds, a "big mistake" where he "totally miscalculated the gain/loss probabilities" (2011); the housing recovery call "dead wrong" (2011).
- Operational errors: the GEICO credit card ("I was just older") and letting NetJets' debt balloon to $1.9b (2009); NetJets' losses understated by subsidised credit (2010).
- Small acquisitions: repeated admission that several small purchases have poor returns from misjudged competitive strength or industry economics; Munger "voted no more than 'present'" (2010, 2011); "If something's not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well" (2011); reverting to "bargain-hunting" despite Munger's wonderful-business lesson "with results ranging from tolerable to terrible" (2012).
- The See's near-miss over $5m and the refused TV station show the cost of price-anchoring on a great business (2007), while the MidAmerican nickel shows the opposite — pay up a little for quality (2007).
- Evolution over these years: (a) explicit acceptance of capital-intensive regulated businesses (utilities, BNSF) as a necessary home for Berkshire's cash, while reaffirming that low-capital high-return businesses are "by far" the best (2009); (b) first-ever buyback authorisation and a fully articulated buyback/dividend doctrine (2011, 2012); (c) formal succession — Combs, Weschler, a designated CEO successor (2010–2012); (d) willingness to issue stock (BNSF) framed as a close, reluctant exception (2009); (e) a stated end to new derivatives positions once collateral rules changed (2011, 2012); (f) re-stated preference for large non-control stakes in wonderful businesses over 100% of so-so ones (2012).

### 10. Graham, Munger, Fisher, Keynes and others

- **Ben Graham**: Buffett's first employer (with Jerry Newman) who sent him to the Rockwood meeting in 1954 (2007); "Price is what you pay; value is what you get" (2008); his "hero" at Columbia; Graham's chairmanship of GEICO led to the Lorimer Davidson Saturday (2010); Chapter Eight of *The Intelligent Investor* on price fluctuations — "the scales fell from my eyes, and low prices became my friend. Picking up that book was one of the luckiest moments in my life" (2011); *The Intelligent Investor* and the 1940 *Security Analysis* top the all-time list for serious investors (2012).
- **Charlie Munger**: "If you've hit three balls out of bounds to the left, aim a little to the right on the next swing" (2007); "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there" and Jacobi's "Invert, always invert" (2009); "Are we supposed to applaud because the dog that fouls our lawn is a Chihuahua rather than a Saint Bernard?" (2009); "If something's not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well" (2011); "it was far better to buy a wonderful business at a fair price than to buy a fair business at a wonderful price" — told to Buffett "more than 50 years ago" (2012); voted only "present" on Buffett's errant purchases (2011); *Poor Charlie's Almanack* promoted every year.
- **Phil Fisher**: Chapter 7 of *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* — the hamburger vs Chinese restaurant analogy for consistent dividend policy; the book "ranks behind only The Intelligent Investor and the 1940 edition of Security Analysis" (2012).
- **Keynes**: not mentioned in these six letters.
- **Others**: Jay Pritzker as "business genius" of the Rockwood cocoa-bean arbitrage (2007); Tom Murphy, "overall the best business manager I've ever met" (2007, 2012); Lorimer "Davy" Davidson's four-hour GEICO education (2010); Lou Simpson "one of the investment greats" (2010); Jamie Dimon on price-conscious buybacks (2011); Lou Gerstner and Sam Palmisano's IBM financial management (2011); Jorge Paulo Lemann "an extraordinary manager" (2012); Walter Hussman's paywall example (2012); Ray DeVoe: "More money has been lost reaching for yield than at the point of a gun" (2010); Shelby Cullom Davis: "Bonds promoted as offering risk-free returns are now priced to deliver return-free risk" (2011); John Kenneth Galbraith on economists making graduate-school ideas last a lifetime (2010); Ben Franklin: "It's difficult for an empty sack to stand upright" (2008) and "Keep thy shop, and thy shop will keep thee" (2011); Thoreau: "It's not what you look at that matters, it's what you see" (2011); William Thorndike's *The Outsiders*, Jack Bogle's *The Clash of the Cultures* (2012); John Stumpf: "the industry has invented new ways to lose money when the old ways seemed to work just fine" (2007).

### 11. Macro and market views

- **Housing bubble and lending**: house-price-appreciation faith made income and equity "seem unimportant to lenders" (2007); the manufactured-housing fiasco of 1997–2000 was an ignored canary (2008); foreclosures come from unaffordable payments, not negative equity; prescription of 10% down and verified income (2008, 2010, 2012); supply must fall below household formation to cure the overhang (2009, 2011); cash-out refinancing fuelled a consumption binge (2011).
- **The crisis and policy response**: fear→contraction feedback loop; Treasury/Fed "all in," likely to bring "an onslaught of inflation" and dependence on "the public teat"; action was nonetheless "essential" (2008). Government guarantees made AAA Berkshire a worse credit than guaranteed cripples (2008). Bear Stearns rescue was right (2008). Bank shareholders lost $500b+ while CEOs and directors "largely gone unscathed" (2009).
- **Interest rates and bonds**: late-2008 Treasuries were a bubble; cash at near-zero yields "a terrible policy if continued for long" (2008); bonds "should come with a warning label"; the dollar lost 86% since 1965; the inflation "tax" exceeded the income tax threefold for a T-bill holder; buy bonds only when credits are mispriced or rates high enough to offer capital gains (2011). Low rates are "wasting assets" for insurers' legacy portfolios (2012). Money-market yields will eventually normalise and add $500m+ to income (2010).
- **Inflation**: certain over time; governments "gravitate to policies that produce inflation"; productive assets with low capital needs are the hedge; gold is sterile (2011); the dollar will be worth ~14¢ in a century at 2% inflation (2008).
- **Forecasting and timing**: he won't predict markets; economists/pundits perpetually wrong about "terrifying problems"; December 6 1941 / October 18 1987 / September 10 2001 show tomorrow is always uncertain (2010); "America's best days lie ahead" (2008, 2010, 2011); dancing in and out of the market is a terrible mistake (2012); the economy-in-shambles sentence misreported by media (2009).
- **Expected returns**: 5.3% Dow appreciation plus ~2% dividends is the historical base; double-digit equity expectations imply absurd Dow levels; fees eat returns (2007). Berkshire's own future advantage will be "a small fraction of our historical edge" (2009, 2010, 2012).
- **Derivatives and leverage**: derivatives raise systemic leverage, create unsettled multi-year counterparty webs, and make banks un-analyzable; transparency won't fix it (2008); collateral-posting is a lethal threat (2008, 2011, 2012); leverage produces zeroes; credit is oxygen (2010).
- **Dollar and trade**: $2b/day of IOUs; a falling dollar had not fixed deficits with Germany or Canada; sovereign wealth funds are "our doing"; don't single out countries; imbalances are unsustainable (2007); currency positions earned $2.3b over five years; "Currencies do matter" (2007).
- **Models**: beware history-based and volatility-based models; efficient-market theory treated refuting facts as "anomalies" (2008, 2010).
- **American investment opportunity**: 88–95% of record capex at home; "Money will always flow toward opportunity, and there is an abundance of that in America" (2010, 2011, 2012); railroads as the economy's "circulatory system" (2010–2012); renewables buildout under regulated returns (2008–2012).
