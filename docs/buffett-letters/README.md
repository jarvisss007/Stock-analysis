# What Makes the Difference

## Investing as Warren Buffett explains it across 48 years of Berkshire Hathaway letters (1977 to 2024), with the ideas he took from Graham, Fisher and Munger, and what Lynch and other great money managers add

---

### How this document was produced

Every Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter from 1977 through 2024 was read in full (about 3.3 million characters of text). The letters were read in eight batches of six years each, and for every letter the following were extracted: the context, the principles Buffett actually states, the businesses he discusses and why, the mistakes he admits, and verbatim quotations tagged with the year. Those year-by-year notes are in the `year-by-year/` folder next to this file and run to roughly 117,000 words. This document is the synthesis.

Three caveats. First, the 1965 to 1976 partnership and early Berkshire letters are not on Berkshire's website and were not available; what Buffett says about those years comes from his own retrospectives in later letters (especially 1989, 2014, 2020 and 2024). Second, the text of several letters came from mirrors with punctuation or inter-word spacing stripped by PDF extraction; quotes from those years have had spacing restored but no words changed. Third, Part VIII on Graham, Fisher, Munger, Lynch and the other managers draws on their own books, talks and letters rather than on the Berkshire letters, and is stated from knowledge of those sources rather than from a fresh reading of them.

Everything in Parts I through VII is sourced to a letter year in parentheses so you can go back to the original.

---

## Part I. What investing is

Buffett defines investing more carefully as the decades go by, but the definition never changes in substance. There are four layers to it.

**1. A stock is a piece of a business, nothing else.** This is the foundation and it comes directly from Graham. "We select our marketable equity securities in much the same way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety" (1977, restated almost word for word in 1978, 1987, 1992 and 2013). "Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers" (2021, 2022). Stocks are "interests in businesses, not ... ticker symbols to be bought or sold based on their 'chart' patterns, the 'target' prices of analysts or the opinions of media pundits" (2017). He says this thinking "has been the cornerstone of my investment behavior since I was 19" when he read The Intelligent Investor and "the scales fell from my eyes" (2004, 2011).

The consequence is that the investor's job is business analysis: "we view ourselves as business analysts, not as market analysts, not as macroeconomic analysts, and not even as security analysts" (1987).

**2. The return comes from what the business earns, not from what the next buyer pays.** "In the end, what counts in investing is what you pay for a business, through the purchase of a small piece of it in the stock market, and what that business earns in the succeeding decade or two" (2009). Over any long period "investor gains must equal business gains" (1985), and "stocks can't outperform businesses indefinitely" (1986). So the scoreboard is the earnings of the companies you own: "to put runs on the scoreboard one must watch the playing field, not the scoreboard" (1991). He advises every investor to compute the "look-through earnings" of their own portfolio (their share of each holding's total earnings, retained as well as paid out) and to try to maximize that number ten years out, because doing so forces you to think about business prospects rather than market prospects (1991).

The mechanism that makes equities compound is retained earnings reinvested inside the business. Buffett credits Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book and Keynes's review of it for the insight that "there is an element of compound interest operating in favour of a sound industrial investment" (2019). That is why, over a long horizon, a diversified group of productive businesses beats bonds, cash and gold.

**3. Investing is forgoing purchasing power now to get more purchasing power later, after tax.** "Investing is the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power, after taxes have been paid on nominal gains, in the future" (2011, repeated 2014, 2017). The word "purchasing power" matters. Measured that way, "safe" currency assets are the dangerous ones: the dollar lost 86 to 87 percent of its value between 1965 and 2014, so a T-bill holder paying income tax and the "inflation tax" earned nothing real (2011, 2014). Risk is therefore not volatility. "The riskiness of an investment is not measured by beta ... but rather by the probability, the reasoned probability, of that investment causing its owner a loss of purchasing-power over his contemplated holding period" (2011). "Risk is the possibility that this objective won't be attained" (2017).

**4. Investment versus speculation.** Following Graham's definition, Buffett calls any commitment where you cannot form a conviction about the asset's future output "speculative," however respectable it looks. Speculation focuses "not on what an asset will produce but rather on what the next fellow will pay" (2000). "If you instead focus on the prospective price change of a contemplated purchase, you are speculating. ... And the fact that a given asset has appreciated in the recent past is never a reason to buy it" (2013). "Value investing" is a redundancy: "What is 'investing' if it is not the act of seeking value at least sufficient to justify the amount paid?" (1992).

**The time horizon that follows.** If the return comes from business earnings, the holding period is as long as the business keeps earning well. "Our favorite holding period is forever" (1988). "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes" (1996). "At Berkshire, our time horizon is forever" (2010). "We would not be disturbed if markets closed for a year or two. We don't need a daily quote on our 100% position in See's or H. H. Brown to validate our well-being. Why, then, should we need a quote on our 7% interest in Coke?" (1993).

**The whole method in one sentence.** "Your goal as an investor should simply be to purchase, at a rational price, a part interest in an easily-understandable business whose earnings are virtually certain to be materially higher five, ten and twenty years from now" (1996). And the explicit decision rule: "We first have to decide whether we can sensibly estimate an earnings range for five years out, or more. If the answer is yes, we will buy the stock (or business) if it sells at a reasonable price in relation to the bottom boundary of our estimate. If, however, we lack the ability to estimate future earnings, which is usually the case, we simply move on to other prospects" (2013).

## Part II. What to look for in the business

This is the heart of the letters. Buffett's criteria appear as a short list in almost every year (1977, 1978, 1982, 1992, 2003, 2007, 2017, 2019). The 2017 version is the most complete: "durable competitive strengths; able and high-grade management; good returns on the net tangible assets required to operate the business; opportunities for internal growth at attractive returns; and, finally, a sensible purchase price." Behind that list is a body of economic reasoning developed over five decades. Here is each element with the logic.

### 1. High return on unleveraged net tangible capital

This is the master metric, from the first letter to the last. "The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share" (1979). "Economic gains must be evaluated by comparison with the capital that produces them" (1984). Earnings per share growth is a trap because retained earnings alone will grow it: "even a totally dormant savings account will produce steadily rising interest earnings each year because of compounding" (1977); "even a stopped clock can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low" (1979).

The benchmark numbers he uses: businesses earning 25 percent to well over 100 percent after tax on unleveraged net tangible assets have "terrific economics"; 12 to 20 percent is "good" (2013). Berkshire's marketable portfolio earns "about 20% on the net tangible equity capital required to run their businesses" without excessive debt (2018, 2019). The "Sainted Seven" operating companies earned 57 percent and then 67 percent after tax on historical equity with no leverage (1987, 1988). He notes that only 25 of Fortune's 1,000 largest companies averaged 20 percent or more on equity over a decade with no year under 15 percent, that 24 of those 25 beat the S&P, that most used little leverage and that most sold mundane products (1987).

Why return on capital rather than margin, growth or earnings? Because it measures what the business does with the money that owners leave in it, and that is where compounding happens. The counter-example is the average American business, which needs "about $5 of additional capital to generate an additional $1 of annual pre-tax earnings" (1985), so growth consumes most of what it earns.

### 2. Low capital intensity: growth that does not need capital

The second question is how much capital the business needs to grow. The ideal business grows earnings "with little additional capital investment" (See's, 1977). The letters return to See's again and again as the archetype: bought in 1972 for $25 million when it had $8 million of net tangible assets and earned under $5 million pre-tax (60 percent pre-tax on invested capital). By 2007 it was earning $82 million pre-tax on $40 million of capital. Only $32 million of earnings had been reinvested in 35 years; $1.35 billion had been sent to Berkshire to buy other businesses (2007; updated to $1.9 billion earned on $40 million added in 2014). The reasons See's needed so little capital: it sold for cash (no receivables) and had a short production and distribution cycle (low inventories) (2007).

The 2007 letter gives the taxonomy. Think of three kinds of savings account: "The great one pays an extraordinarily high interest rate that will rise as the years pass. The good one pays an attractive rate of interest that will be earned also on deposits that are added. Finally, the gruesome account both pays an inadequate interest rate and requires you to keep adding money at those disappointing returns." See's is great. FlightSafety (which spent $1.635 billion on simulators against $923 million of depreciation to add $159 million of earnings) and the regulated utilities are good: "put-up-more-to-earn-more." Airlines are gruesome: "The worst sort of business is one that grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money. Think airlines." And then the line that summarises the whole idea: "It's far better to have an ever-increasing stream of earnings with virtually no major capital requirements. Ask Microsoft or Google" (2007).

Two refinements. First, the very best business of all is one that "can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return" (1992, repeated 2023: "we particularly favor the rare enterprise that can deploy additional capital at high returns in the future. Owning only one of these companies, and simply sitting tight, can deliver wealth almost beyond measure"). Such businesses are rare; "most high-return businesses need relatively little capital," so their owners should get the cash out via dividends or buybacks (1992). Second, Berkshire later accepted capital-heavy regulated businesses (BNSF, the utilities) because its own cash flow needed a home and they earn "decent returns on the incremental sums they invest," but he is explicit that this yields "above-average, though certainly not spectacular, returns" and that "the best businesses by far for owners continue to be those that have high returns on capital and that require little incremental investment to grow" (2009). By 2023 he says plainly "railroads eat capital" and that BNSF earns "a pittance on the replacement value" of its assets.

The 1983 appendix on goodwill makes the inflation case with numbers. Compare See's, earning $2 million on $8 million of net tangible assets (25 percent), with a mundane business earning $2 million on $18 million (11 percent). If the price level doubles, both must roughly double their nominal earnings to stand still, and both must roughly double their tangible capital to do it: See's needs $8 million more, the mundane business $18 million more. See's value doubles from $25 million to $50 million on $8 million of new capital; the mundane business goes from $18 million to $36 million on $18 million of new capital, which is no gain at all. "Any unleveraged business that requires some net tangible assets to operate (and almost all do) is hurt by inflation. Businesses needing little in the way of tangible assets simply are hurt the least." That is why "what a business can be expected to earn on unleveraged net tangible assets, excluding any charges against earnings for amortization of Goodwill, is the best guide to the economic attractiveness of the operation" (1983 appendix).

The inflation test is the same idea from another angle. A business is truly protected against inflation only if "business earnings consistently must increase in proportion to the increase in the price level without any need for the business to add to capital, including working capital, employed. (Increased earnings produced by increased investment don't count.)" (1980). Inflation "acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm" that eats the receivables, inventory and fixed-asset dollars of capital-heavy businesses just to keep unit volume flat (1981). Hence the 1981 definition of the favoured business: one with "(1) an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume, and (2) an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases in business ... with only minor additional investment of capital."

### 3. Pricing power: franchise versus business

Pricing power is the observable sign of a durable competitive advantage, and the 1991 letter gives the sharpest definition in the whole collection. An economic franchise arises from a product or service that "(1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation." All three together let a company "regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital." Franchises "can tolerate mis-management. Inept managers may diminish a franchise's profitability, but they cannot inflict mortal damage." A mere "business," by contrast, "earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight," and "a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management" (1991).

The See's insight was exactly this: "In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: We saw that the business had untapped pricing power" (1991). The opposite case, commodity businesses, gets the harshest treatment: "producers of relatively undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses must earn inadequate returns except under conditions of tight supply or real shortage. As long as excess productive capacity exists, prices tend to reflect direct operating costs rather than capital employed" (1978, textiles). "Persistent over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability" (1982). "In a business selling a commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor" (1990). The test for whether you have a brand or a commodity: "Customers by the millions say 'I need some Gillette blades' or 'I'll have a Coke' but we wait in vain for 'I'd like a National Indemnity policy, please'" (2004). "Buy commodities, sell brands" (2011).

The franchise can erode, which is why he keeps checking. Newspapers and TV went from franchise toward business as the supply of channels grew against fixed demand (1990, 1991, 2006, 2012). He revalued his media holdings downward in his own head while keeping them.

### 4. Durability of the moat; stable industries; no superstar dependence

"A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital. The dynamics of capitalism guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning high returns" (2007). The word "moat" first appears in 1986 for GEICO's cost advantage, "a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much sought after business castle," and in 1993 for Coke and Gillette: brand, product and distribution "setting up a protective moat around their economic castles." Two sources of durable moats are named: "being the low-cost producer (GEICO, Costco) or possessing a powerful world-wide brand (Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express)" (2007).

Durability requires a stable industry. "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix" (1987). The best returns come from companies "doing something quite similar today to what they were doing five or ten years ago" because "economic terrain that is forever shifting violently is ground on which it is difficult to build a fortress-like business franchise" (1987). "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all" (2007). This is why he stayed out of technology during the bubble: not because he doubted its importance, but because "we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage" and "this can't be solved by studying up" (1999). He makes the same point about any exciting growth industry: autos in 1910, aircraft in 1930 and TV in 1950 all had obvious growth and "competitive dynamics that would decimate almost all of the companies entering those industries." "Just because Charlie and I can clearly see dramatic growth ahead for an industry does not mean we can judge what its profit margins and returns on capital will be as a host of competitors battle for supremacy" (2009).

A great business also cannot depend on one person. "If a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot be deemed great" (2007). The ideal is a business so good that "a shiftless and backward nephew" could run it, a "have-to-be-smart-once" business like a network TV station, as opposed to "have-to-be-smart-every-day" businesses like retailing, where "to coast is to fail" (1995). Related rule: "Good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags" (1989). "When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact" (1980, 1985, 1989). "If you want to get a reputation as a good businessman, be sure to get into a good business" (2006, 2019).

The test he applies: "One question I always ask myself in appraising a business is how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it" (1983). And he checks every year whether the moats of his investees have widened: "The second test, more subjective, is whether their 'moats' ... have widened during the year" (2007). "Every day, in countless ways, the competitive position of each of our businesses grows either weaker or stronger ... when short-term and long-term conflict, widening the moat must take precedence" (2005).

### 5. Predictability and the circle of competence

He will only buy what he can forecast. "Charlie and I avoid businesses whose futures we can't evaluate, no matter how exciting their products may be" (2009). "If a business is complex or subject to constant change, we're not smart enough to predict future cash flows" (1992). The published acquisition criteria have said since 1982: "demonstrated consistent earning power (future projections are of little interest to us, nor are turn-around situations)" and "simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)."

The circle of competence doctrine: "What an investor needs is the ability to correctly evaluate selected businesses. ... You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital" (1996). "What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know" (1992). "If you are Bobby Fischer, you must play only chess for money" (2019). He lists his own blind spots openly: investment banking, airlines, paper, patents, manufacturing processes, geology, technology (1989, 1995, 1999, 2017).

He also prefers stability to upside: "I would rather be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one" (1996). "A fast-changing industry environment may offer the chance for huge wins, but it precludes the certainty we seek" (1996). "Why search for a needle buried in a haystack when one is sitting in plain sight?" (1993). "Investors should remember that their scorecard is not computed using Olympic-diving methods: Degree-of-difficulty doesn't count" (1994, 2020).

### 6. The low-cost producer (the only safe way to own a commodity business)

Where a product is a commodity, the only durable advantage is cost. "When a company is selling a product with commodity like economic characteristics being the low cost producer is all important" (2000). GEICO is the case: a direct-writing auto insurer with an expense ratio of 14.7 percent of premiums against about 25 percent at agency-based competitors (2015), running a virtuous circle in which "low costs permit low prices, and low prices attract and retain good policyholders," who refer their friends, which lowers acquisition cost further (1996). Nebraska Furniture Mart and Borsheim's are the retail versions: operating expenses of 15 to 18 percent of sales against 40 percent at competitors, low prices drive volume, volume drives selection, "and so the circle goes 'round and 'round" (1984, 1989, 1990). Nick Sleep later named this pattern "scale economies shared."

The other way to survive in a commodity business is a management that refuses to compete on price and lets volume shrink: National Indemnity cut volume for 14 straight years rather than underprice ("We never left customers, but they left us," 2004).

### 7. Cash generation and "owner earnings"

He wants earnings that turn into cash. "Our acquisition preferences run toward businesses that generate cash, not those that consume it. ... However attractive the earnings numbers, we remain leery of businesses that never seem able to convert such pretty numbers into no-strings-attached cash" (1980). Associated Retail's earnings were praised because they were "realized in cash and not in increased receivables and inventories" (1979). Scott Fetzer was the model: it paid Berkshire $1.03 billion of dividends on a $230 million net purchase price with no leverage (2000).

The formula for the cash an owner can actually take out is "owner earnings," defined in the 1986 appendix as (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization and other non-cash charges, less (c) the average annual capital expenditures for plant and equipment that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and unit volume (plus any required increase in working capital). The crucial point is (c): most businesses must spend more than their depreciation just to hold their ground, so Wall Street's "cash flow" (which adds back depreciation but subtracts nothing for capex) overstates what owners get. See Part IV for the accounting consequences.

### 8. Little or no debt

"Really good businesses usually don't need to borrow" (1987). The acquisition criteria require "good returns on equity while employing little or no debt" (1982 onward). High leverage converts a survivable mistake into a fatal one: at 20 to 1, "a small mistake can destroy equity" (1990, on banks). Buffett treats the absence of debt as evidence about the business's quality, not just its safety: a business that earns high returns on capital does not need to borrow to grow.

### 9. Growth: when it creates value and when it destroys it

Growth is not a virtue in itself. "Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive" (1992). "Growth benefits investors only when the business in point can invest at incremental returns that are enticing, in other words, only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value. In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental funds, growth hurts the investor" (1992). "Growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs in the early years of a project or enterprise that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later years" (2000). The airline industry is the standing example: "Investors have poured money into a bottomless pit, attracted by growth when they should have been repelled by it" (2007).

The corollary for the company itself is the retained-earnings test: "for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners" (1984), tested on a rolling five-year basis (1983). Munger's version of the Berkshire rule: no dividends "so long as more than one dollar of market value for shareholders was being created by each dollar of retained earnings" (2014).

### 10. Red flags that disqualify a business

Across the letters, these are the recurring reasons he passes:

- Turnarounds: "turnarounds seldom turn" (1979); he excludes them from acquisition criteria from 1982 on. The exception is "a wonderful business with a one-time, huge but solvable problem" (American Express after the salad-oil scandal, GEICO in 1976), what he calls "extraordinary business franchises with a localized excisable cancer" (1980, 1989).
- Businesses with projections instead of records (every acquisition ad; "Only in the sales presentations of investment banks do earnings move forever upward," 2000).
- Auctions and unfriendly deals (1983 onward; "we go only where we are welcome," 2015).
- Commodity products in over-supplied industries without a cost advantage (1978, 1982, 1985, 1990, 1994).
- High-cost producers in deregulated industries (USAir: "In an unregulated commodity business, a company must lower its costs to competitive levels or face extinction," 1994).
- Industries of rapid change or exciting new technologies (1987, 1993, 1999, 2007, 2009).
- Start-ups: "Start-ups are not our game" (2007). His long-held companies were founded in 1837, 1850, 1852 and 1886.
- Businesses that lose money over their lifetime whatever their interim valuation (2000, on bubble companies).
- Management that is "hell-bent on issuing shares," which signals "a promotion-minded management, weak accounting, a stock that is overpriced and, all too often, outright dishonesty" (2014).
- A company that has paid no income tax for a decade, "a blinking yellow light" (2024).
- Any industry he cannot forecast ten to twenty years out.

## Part III. What to look for in management

Buffett's management criteria are short but he applies them harshly, and he says that misjudging managers has cost him as much as misjudging businesses (2015, 2016, 2024: mistakes come from misjudging "the future economics of a business" or "the abilities or fidelity of the managers").

**Honesty first, and verified by behaviour, not words.** "Go into business only with people you like, trust, and admire. ... We've never succeeded in making a good deal with a bad person" (1989). "No matter what the rate, trying to write good contracts with bad people doesn't work" (2001). "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you. ... Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked" (2023). The evidence he trusts is conduct: Gene Abegg laying every negative "face up on the table" and later volunteering items of value he had not mentioned (1980); Mrs. B, Jack Ringwalt, Ike Friedman and Pete Liegl selling on a handshake with no audit (1983, 1988, 2006, 2024). Candour in reporting is a test of character: "the CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private" (1983); "if you start fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney" (2024). The "except for" manager who always explains away results "may be missing the only important lesson, namely, that the real mistake is not the act, but the actor" (1985).

**Owner-orientation.** The highest compliment: managers who "unfailingly think like owners" (1986). The instruction to every Berkshire manager: "Just run your business as if (1) you own 100% of it, (2) it is the only asset in the world that you and your family have or will ever have, and (3) you can't sell or merge it for at least a century" (1998, 2004). Compensation should match: pay tied to results the manager controls, "merit badges, not lottery tickets" (1997), with a charge for capital employed and a credit for capital released so "alignment means being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside" (1994). Fixed-price stock options fail every test because they give managers "capital that is free" and reward mere retention of earnings (1985, 1994, 2005). "We do not use options or other one-sided forms of compensation; if you lose money, so do we" (2024).

**Capital allocation skill, the rarest skill.** Most CEOs rise through marketing, production, engineering or law and then "must make capital allocation decisions, a critical job that they may have never tackled"; after ten years of retaining 10 percent of earnings a CEO has deployed more than 60 percent of the capital in the business (1987). The best capital allocators he names repeatedly are Tom Murphy and Dan Burke of Capital Cities, Henry Singleton of Teledyne, Ralph Schey of Scott Fetzer, Kay Graham, Roberto Goizueta, Jack Byrne and Lou Simpson at GEICO, Carl Reichardt at Wells Fargo, Jamie Dimon on buybacks, and the Japanese trading house managers (1981, 1984, 1985, 1990, 1997, 2011, 2012, 2023). Their common traits: they buy back stock when it is cheap and not when it is dear, they do not issue stock, they do few acquisitions and only at the right price, they keep headcount and overhead low in good times as well as bad, and they stay within what they understand.

**Resistance to the institutional imperative.** This is "my most surprising discovery" (1989): "(1) As if governed by Newton's First Law of Motion, an institution will resist any change in its current direction; (2) Just as work expands to fill available time, corporate projects or acquisitions will materialize to soak up available funds; (3) Any business craving of the leader, however foolish, will be quickly supported by detailed rate-of-return and strategic studies prepared by his troops; and (4) The behavior of peer companies, whether they are expanding, acquiring, setting executive compensation or whatever, will be mindlessly imitated." The letters are full of its symptoms: insurers who cannot let volume fall (1977, 1979, 2004), bankers who "played follow-the-leader with lemming-like zeal" (1990), CEOs who "start with the answer they want and then work backwards" (2012), deal-hungry CEOs for whom "Spreadsheets never disappoint" (2017), the "Aw, fellas, all the other kids have one" excuse (1994, 2006). He looks for managers "alert to the problem" (1989).

**Focus.** "What most worries Charlie and me" about great businesses is management losing focus: Coke once grew shrimp, Gillette explored for oil (1996). Goizueta's "obsession" was "focus" (1997). Tony Nicely "never engages in wishful thinking or otherwise distorts reality as so many managers do when the unexpected happens" (2000). Thomas Watson Sr.: "I'm smart in spots, but I stay around those spots" (1990).

**Cost consciousness as a habit.** "The manager of an already high-cost operation frequently is uncommonly resourceful in finding new ways to add to overhead, while the manager of a tightly-run operation usually continues to find additional methods to curtail costs" (1978). Berkshire's own headquarters: 5 people in 1982, 25 for 300,000 employees in 2016, overhead under two basis points of net worth (1996), no restructuring charges ever (2015).

**Candour with owners, and the right scoreboard.** Managers should "supply you with the financial information that we would wish you to give us if our positions were reversed" (1993, 2024). "Yardsticks seldom are discarded while yielding favorable readings. But when results deteriorate, most managers favor disposition of the yardstick rather than disposition of the manager" (1982). "At too many companies, the boss shoots the arrow of managerial performance and then hastily paints the bullseye around the spot where it lands" (1988). Be suspicious of managers who promise and always hit numbers: "Managers that always promise to make the numbers will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers" (2002, 2016).

**Delegation to proven people.** "Our trust is in people rather than process. A 'hire well, manage little' code suits both them and me" (2010). "At Berkshire, we do not tell .400 hitters how to swing" (1993). But the bet on people cannot rescue bad economics: that is Part II, criterion 4.

**Talent is innate; pedigree is irrelevant.** "Extraordinary business ability is largely innate" (2004); "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!" (2024). Ben Rosner never went past sixth grade; only one of National Indemnity's four CEOs graduated from college.

## Part IV. Valuation: intrinsic value, price and margin of safety

**The definition.** "Intrinsic value can be defined simply: It is the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life" (1994; also 1983, 1989, 1992, 1993). He attributes the formula to John Burr Williams' The Theory of Investment Value: "The value of any stock, bond or business today is determined by the cash inflows and outflows, discounted at an appropriate interest rate, that can be expected to occur during the remaining life of the asset" (1992). The same formula applies to stocks and bonds; the difference is that a bond's coupons are printed on it while for a stock "the investment analyst must himself estimate the future 'coupons'" (1992).

**Aesop's version.** In 2000 he restates it as "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush," with three questions: "How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)? If you can answer these three questions, you will know the maximum value of the bush." The formula "applies to outlays for farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants" and was not changed by the steam engine, electricity, the automobile or the Internet.

**What multiples mean.** Price-to-earnings, price-to-book, dividend yield and growth rates "have nothing to do with valuation except to the extent they provide clues to the amount and timing of cash flows into and from the business" (2000). "Low P/B, low P/E and high yield are far from determinative" of value and their opposites are "in no way inconsistent with a 'value' purchase" (1992). The letters do contain one worked example that shows how multiples follow from economics (1991): if a business earns $1 million after tax, can grow 6 percent forever with no incremental capital, and you discount at 10 percent, it is worth $25 million (25 times after-tax earnings, about 16 times pre-tax). If the same $1 million is "normal earning power" that merely bobs around with the cycle and grows only when owners put in more capital, the same discount rate gives $10 million (10 times after-tax, about 6.5 times pre-tax). "Dollars are dollars whether they are derived from the operation of media properties or of steel mills." What justified the premium was growth without incremental capital. That is the reason a high return on capital with low capital needs (Part II, criteria 1 and 2) deserves a high multiple and a capital-hungry business does not.

**Interest rates are the gravity on all values.** The 1981 letter is the earliest statement: when long tax-exempts yielded 14 percent, "a typical American business is no longer worth one hundred cents on the dollar to owners who are individuals" because the "crossbar of passive return" had been raised above what businesses earn. In 1997 the market was "not overvalued" only if rates stayed low and returns on equity stayed exceptionally high. In 2021: "Long-term interest rates that are low push the prices of all productive investments upward, whether these are stocks, apartments, farms, oil wells, whatever. Other factors influence valuations as well, but interest rates will always be important." Stocks are safer than bonds over long horizons "assuming that the stocks are purchased at a sensible multiple of earnings relative to then-prevailing interest rates" (2017). Munger's discount rate is opportunity cost; Buffett's is the long Treasury yield, with the margin of safety applied to the estimate rather than to the rate.

**Precision is impossible, so use ranges and a wide margin.** "Calculations of intrinsic value, though all-important, are necessarily imprecise and often seriously wrong. The more uncertain the future of a business, the more possibility there is that the calculation will be wildly off-base" (2005). "Using precise numbers is in fact foolish; working with a range of possibilities is the better approach. Usually the range must be so wide that no useful conclusion can be reached" (2000). He and Munger "might differ by 10% in our appraisals" (1990, 2010). "It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong" (1993, 2010). Hence the margin of safety: "we insist on a margin of safety in our purchase price. If we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not interested in buying. We believe this margin-of-safety principle, so strongly emphasized by Ben Graham, to be the cornerstone of investment success" (1992). "Confronted with a challenge to distill the secret of sound investment into three words, we venture the motto, Margin of Safety. Forty-two years after reading that, I still think those are the right three words" (1990). Buy at "a reasonable price in relation to the bottom boundary of our estimate" (2013). For Berkshire's own buybacks: "neither of us feels any urgency to buy an estimated $1 of value for a very real 95 cents" (2019).

**Price can ruin a great business.** This warning recurs in almost every decade. "For the investor, a too-high purchase price for the stock of an excellent company can undo the effects of a subsequent decade of favorable business developments" (1982). "You can, of course, pay too much for even the best of businesses" (1996). "A business with terrific economics can be a bad investment if the purchase price is excessive" (2012, 2013, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2018). "A sound investment can morph into a rash speculation if it is bought at an elevated price. Berkshire is not exempt from this truth" (2014). Coca-Cola fell 50 percent from its 1920 IPO price and investors in 1972's "Nifty Fifty" waited many years to break even (1982, 1993). His Precision Castparts write-down of $11 billion was "almost entirely the quantification of a mistake I made in 2016": he was right about the business quality and "wrong in judging the average amount of future earnings and, consequently, wrong in my calculation of the proper price to pay" (2020).

**But a cheap price cannot rescue a bad business, and quality is worth paying for.** This is the lesson Munger taught him and it is the single most important shift in the letters. "Despite a fancy price tag, the easy business may be the better route to go" (1979). "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner" (1989). "Our goal is to find an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price" (1987). "If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price or a comfortable business at a questionable price, we much prefer the latter. What really gets our attention, however, is a comfortable business at a comfortable price" (1999). "Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre" (1989). He nearly lost See's over $5 million of price haggling: "Otherwise I would have balked, and that $1.35 billion would have gone to somebody else" (2007). Fisher's advice not to quibble over eighths applies when the business is truly great.

**Value versus growth is a false distinction.** "The two approaches are joined at the hip" (1992). Growth-versus-value labels reflect "ignorance, not sophistication" (2000).

**Book value is not value.** "Book value tells you what has been put in; intrinsic business value estimates what can be taken out" (1983). "At most companies, the two values are unrelated" (1993). Berkshire's textile looms bought for $5,000 sold for $26 as scrap; "the economic goodwill attributable to two paper routes in Buffalo, or a single See's candy store, considerably exceeds the proceeds we received from this massive collection of tangible assets" (1985). Economic goodwill "is much like land": its value is not ordained to fall, and See's goodwill grew for 78 years (1999). Graham's net-net method depended on book value; Buffett's does not.

**Where bargains come from.** The secondary market, "periodically ruled by mass folly," sets prices that can be half of value; new-issue markets never give bargains because sellers choose the timing (1992). Fractional interests in wonderful businesses "sometimes sell in the securities markets at very large discounts from the prices they would command in negotiated transactions involving entire companies" (1977). "Stocks often trade at truly foolish prices, both high and low. 'Efficient' markets exist only in textbooks" (2022). Pessimism, whether pervasive or company-specific, is "the most common cause of low prices," so "It's optimism that is the enemy of the rational buyer" (1990). "We have usually made our best purchases when apprehensions about some macro event were at a peak. Fear is the foe of the faddist, but the friend of the fundamentalist" (1994).

**The yardstick for Berkshire itself** is given for anyone who wants to replicate it: per-share investments plus per-share pre-tax operating earnings of the businesses, with a judgment about how well retained earnings will be deployed (1995 to 2015; the "five groves" in 2018). The stock has compounded at almost exactly the rate of those two numbers over 40 years (2010, 2012). "Market price and intrinsic value often follow very different paths, sometimes for extended periods, but eventually they meet" (2010).

## Part V. Reading the accounts: the traps Buffett warns about

Buffett treats accounting as "the language of business" but insists that "accounting is but an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it" (1986) and "accounting numbers are the beginning, not the end, of business valuation" (1982). Financial reports should let a literate reader answer three questions: "(1) Approximately how much is this company worth? (2) What is the likelihood that it can meet its future obligations? and (3) How good a job are its managers doing, given the hand they have been dealt?" (1988). The specific lessons, in rough order of how often they recur:

1. **EBITDA is not earnings.** Depreciation is a real cost, paid in advance. "Whenever an investment banker starts talking about EBDIT ... zip up your wallet" (1989). "References to EBITDA make us shudder; does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" (2000). "Those who believe that EBITDA is in any way equivalent to true earnings are welcome to pick up the tab" (2001). "When Wall Streeters tout EBITDA as a valuation guide, button your wallet" (2013); "wire them up for a polygraph test" (2014); "watch their noses lengthen while they speak" (2015). "'EBITDA' is a banned measurement at Berkshire" (2023). Interest coverage should be pre-tax earnings over interest, "not EBITDA/interest, a commonly-used measure we view as deeply flawed" (2012). The FlightSafety and railroad examples show that capital-intensive businesses must spend an amount equal to depreciation "simply to stay in the same economic place" and often more (2000, 2015, 2023).

2. **Owner earnings versus "cash flow."** Defined above. Wall Street cash flow numbers "are frequently used by marketers of businesses and securities in attempts to justify the unjustifiable" and are "meaningless in such businesses as manufacturing, retailing, extractive companies, and utilities" (1986).

3. **Depreciation can also understate the true cost.** At BNSF, capex above depreciation ran $22 billion over 14 years, so "BNSF must annually spend more than its depreciation charge to simply maintain its present level of business" and reported earnings overstate distributable cash (2015, 2016, 2023).

4. **Purchase-accounting amortization is mostly not real.** Amortization of goodwill and "customer relationships" created by an acquisition is "clearly not real," while amortization of software is real; about 20 percent of Berkshire's amortization charge is a true cost (2012 to 2018). Economic goodwill usually grows while accounting goodwill is amortized or written down and never written up: GEICO's goodwill was carried at $1.4 billion when its economic goodwill was about $14 billion (2010). The 1983 appendix on goodwill explains the whole topic.

5. **Stock options are compensation, compensation is an expense, and expenses belong in earnings.** "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?" (1992, 1998). Berkshire adjusts reported earnings of companies it analyses by the value at which equivalent options could be sold, which "frequently cut the reported per share figures by 5%, with 10% not all that uncommon" (1998). "What else could it be, a gift from shareholders?" (2018).

6. **Restructuring charges and "adjusted" earnings.** The golf-scorecard analogy: a player who turns in one 140 and then counts only his good holes (1998). Restructuring charges are the "tuition" shareholders pay for CEOs' bad acquisitions (1992). "Bull markets breed bloviated bull...." (2021). "Bold imaginative accounting ... has become one of the shames of capitalism" (2022).

7. **Look-through earnings.** Reported earnings from a minority stake include only dividends. The investee's retained earnings are just as real ("If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don't record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree," 1980). Their value depends on how they are used, not on how they are accounted for. Historically Berkshire's investees' retained earnings have produced "more than one dollar" of capital gains per dollar retained, "albeit in a highly irregular manner" (2010, 2018, 2019).

8. **Net income can be whatever management wants.** At Berkshire, realized gains flow through earnings while unrealized gains do not, so "Charlie and I could, quite legally, cause net income in any given period to be almost any number we would like" (2010). After the 2018 rule change putting unrealized gains into net income, GAAP earnings became "worse-than-useless" and "100% misleading when viewed quarterly or even annually" (2019, 2022, 2023). Focus on operating earnings, defined after "interest, depreciation, taxes, non-cash compensation, restructuring charges, all of those pesky, but very real, costs" (2019).

9. **Insurance reserves and any long-tail liability are a self-graded exam.** Loss reserves are "at best, only a first rough draft of earnings" (1984); "Where 'earnings' can be created by the stroke of a pen, the dishonest will gather" (1987); "The losses didn't develop; they were there all along" (2001). The same applies to pension assumptions (an 8 percent assumed return with 28 percent in bonds is impossible, 2007), post-retirement health promises (1992) and "mark to model" derivatives that become "mark to myth" (2002).

10. **Book value, realized gains and yearly snapshots.** Book value measures input, not output (1983, 1993). Annual realized gains "are meaningless for analytical purposes" (1983, 1985, 2003). One year's results prove nothing: use at least five years (1983), and remember that "the pace of the earth's movement around the sun is not synchronized with the time required for either investment ideas or operating decisions to bear fruit" (2010).

11. **Growth rates depend on the chosen endpoints** and CEOs who predict growth rates are "both deceptive and dangerous," because fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies of 2000 would grow earnings per share 15 percent a year for 20 years (2000, 2003, 2005).

12. **Securitization and sale-leasebacks front-load income.** Holding receivables on the balance sheet shows more debt and lower reported earnings but makes more money: "we prefer better profits to better cosmetics" (2003, 2005).

13. **Watch how accounting choices reveal management.** "The reaction of weak managements to weak operations is often weak accounting" (1982). "Unintelligible footnotes usually indicate untrustworthy management" (2002). "There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen" (2002, 2014). "Bad accounting drives out good" (1998).

## Part VI. Capital allocation inside the company

How a company uses the cash it generates is, in Buffett's view, the decision that most determines what a share is worth, and it is the thing to examine about management. The hierarchy he lays out in 2012 is:

1. **Reinvest in the existing business to widen the moat,** but only where the return on the incremental dollar is attractive. Managers fail here when "they start with the answer they want and then work backwards to find a supporting rationale," his own sin with textiles: "wishing makes dreams come true only in Disney movies; it's poison in business" (2012). Capital projects in commodity industries that look sensible individually can be collectively irrational, like everyone in a parade standing on tiptoe (1985).

2. **Acquire other businesses,** if and only if the deal "is likely to leave our shareholders wealthier on a per-share basis than they were prior to the acquisition" (2012). The record of corporate acquisitions is bad: "most deals do damage to the shareholders of the acquiring company" (1995); "the acquirer typically gives up more intrinsic value than it receives ... you are running a chain letter in reverse" (1994). The test is intrinsic value given versus intrinsic value received, "this most fundamental of yardsticks is almost always ignored by both managements and their investment bankers" (2005). Never evaluate a deal by earnings-per-share accretion (1982, 1994, 2014) and never count synergies ("At Berkshire, we never count on synergies when we acquire companies," 2016). Evaluate on an all-equity basis so cheap debt does not flatter the arithmetic (2000, 2017). Post-mortems should be standard practice (2000, 2014). "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut" (1982, 2009, 2014, 2019).

3. **Repurchase shares, but only below intrinsic value.** "When companies with outstanding businesses and comfortable financial positions find their shares selling far below intrinsic value in the marketplace, no alternative action can benefit shareholders as surely as repurchases" (1984); such companies "often find it easy to get $2 of present value for $1" (1984). The two conditions: ample funds beyond business needs, and a price "below its intrinsic value conservatively calculated" (1999, 2011). "The first law of capital allocation, whether the money is slated for acquisitions or share repurchases, is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another" (2011). "Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around" (1999); "It's hard to go wrong when you're buying dollar bills for 80 cents or less" (2012). Buybacks to offset option dilution or to "support the stock" are value-destroying (1999, 2011). The investor's corollary: if you own a company that is buying back stock, you should hope its price stays low (the IBM example, 2011; "The math of repurchases grinds away slowly, but can be powerful over time," 2020).

4. **Pay dividends** when the company cannot reinvest at adequate returns. "A company with historic and prospective high returns on equity should retain much or all of its earnings ... Conversely, low returns on corporate equity would suggest a very high dividend payout" (1981). "If earnings have been unwisely retained, it is likely that managers, too, have been unwisely retained" (1984). Policy must be "clear, consistent and rational" (2012, citing Fisher's chapter 7).

**Never issue stock cheaply.** "We will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic business value as we give" (1982, repeated every decade). Issuing stock is "a partial sale" of the whole company; "In a trade, what you are giving is just as important as what you are getting" (1982). "You simply can't exchange an undervalued stock for a fully-valued one without hurting your shareholders" (2009). His own stock-financed deals have "on balance ... cost you money" (1997, 2005); Dexter paid in shares cost not $433 million but what those shares became, over $6 billion (2007, 2014, 2016); Gen Re paid in stock was "a terrible mistake" (2016). "Today, I would rather prep for a colonoscopy than issue Berkshire shares" (2016).

**Keep debt low and liquidity high.** "We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet" (1983). Obligations must be "certain," not "likely," and policies are set for "extraordinarily adverse conditions" (1987). "A small chance of distress or disgrace cannot, in our view, be offset by a large chance of extra returns" (1989). "Any series of positive numbers, however impressive the numbers may be, evaporates when multiplied by a single zero" (2010, 2022). "Credit is like oxygen" (2010). Berkshire keeps a floor of $20 to 30 billion in Treasury bills, no bank lines, no collateral-call exposure, so that it could invest $15.6 billion "in 25 days of panic following the Lehman bankruptcy" (2010). "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations. When forced to choose, I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits" (2008). "Rational people don't risk what they have and need for what they don't have and don't need" (2018). Borrow before you need it: "if you want to shoot rare, fast-moving elephants, you should always carry a loaded gun" (1987).

**Prefer a piece of a great business to all of a mediocre one.** "We would rather buy 10% of Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X per share. Most corporate managers prefer just the reverse" (1981). "It's far better to own a significant portion of the Hope diamond than 100% of a rhinestone" (1994, 2007, 2013). Owning a non-controlling piece of a wonderful business "is more profitable, more enjoyable and far less work than struggling with 100% of a marginal enterprise" (2020).

## Part VII. Temperament: the part that most investors get wrong

Buffett repeats, in different words, that intelligence is not the bottleneck. "Investing is not a game where the guy with the 160 IQ beats the guy with the 130 IQ." What his successor needs is "Independent thinking, emotional stability, and a keen understanding of both human and institutional behavior ... I've seen a lot of very smart people who have lacked these virtues" (2006). The elements:

**Mr. Market.** Graham's parable, told in full in 1987: a manic-depressive partner who names a price every day for your share of the business. "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful." "If you aren't certain that you understand and can value your business far better than Mr. Market, you don't belong in the game." Therefore "the true investor welcomes volatility" (1993), because a manic-depressive market "periodically attaches irrationally low prices to solid businesses" and cannot hurt "an investor who is totally free to either ignore the market or exploit its folly." The investor is hurt by volatility only if "forced, by either financial or psychological pressures, to sell at untoward times" (1987). The 2013 version: a moody neighbour yelling a price for your farm every day can only benefit you.

**Fear and greed.** "We simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful" (1986, repeated 2004, 2006). "When investing, pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy" (2008). "A climate of fear is your friend when investing; a euphoric world is your enemy" (2013). "Widespread fear is your friend as an investor, because it serves up bargain purchases. Second, personal fear is your enemy" (2016). Net savers should want prices to fall: the hamburger test, "Only those who will be sellers of equities in the near future should be happy at seeing stocks rise. Prospective purchasers should much prefer sinking prices" (1997, 2011). "Approval is not the goal of investing ... Beware the investment activity that produces applause; the great moves are usually greeted by yawns" (2008). "Nothing sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money" (2000). But contrarianism for its own sake is as foolish as crowd-following: "What's required is thinking rather than polling" (1990).

**Inactivity and patience.** "Lethargy bordering on sloth remains the cornerstone of our investment style" (1990). "We continue to make more money when snoring than when active. Inactivity strikes us as intelligent behavior" (1996). "In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement" (1998). "It pays to be active, interested and open-minded, but it does not pay to be in a hurry" (1992). Ted Williams waited for pitches in his happy zone; "waiting for the fat pitch would mean a trip to the Hall of Fame; swinging indiscriminately would mean a ticket to the minors" (1997). "Stick with big, 'easy' decisions and eschew activity" (2017). The whole record came from "about a dozen truly good decisions, that would be about one every five years" (2022); "Owning only one of these companies, and simply sitting tight, can deliver wealth almost beyond measure" (2023). "The stock market serves as a relocation center at which money is moved from the active to the patient" (1991). "For investors as a whole, returns decrease as motion increases" (2005). Munger: "The big money is not in the buying or the selling, but in the waiting."

**Concentration, for those who know something.** "Our policy is to concentrate holdings. We try to avoid buying a little of this or that when we are only lukewarm about the business or its price" (1978). "If you are a know-something investor, able to understand business economics and to find five to ten sensibly-priced companies that possess important long-term competitive advantages, conventional diversification makes no sense for you. It is apt simply to hurt your results and increase your risk" (1993). "We believe that a policy of portfolio concentration may well decrease risk if it raises, as it should, both the intensity with which an investor thinks about a business and the comfort-level he must feel with its economic characteristics before buying into it" (1993). Keynes's 1934 letter is quoted in support (1991). The flip side, for everyone else: "Paradoxically, when 'dumb' money acknowledges its limitations, it ceases to be dumb" (1993). The know-nothing investor should own a low-cost index fund, accumulate steadily and never sell on bad news (1993, 2003, 2013, 2014, 2016, 2017); his own will puts 90 percent in a Vanguard S&P 500 fund (2013).

**Risk is permanent loss, not fluctuation.** "Volatility is far from synonymous with risk. Popular formulas that equate the two terms lead students, investors and CEOs astray" (2014). Beta tells you that the Washington Post became "riskier" after it fell 50 percent in 1973 (1993). The five things that determine real risk: how certain the long-term economics are, how certain management's ability and honesty are, the purchase price, and taxes and inflation (1993). "One investment rule at Berkshire has not and will not change: Never risk permanent loss of capital" (2023).

**No leverage.** "Borrowed money has no place in the investor's tool kit: Anything can happen anytime in markets" (2014). Berkshire's stock fell 59, 37, 49 and 51 percent on four occasions; "There is simply no telling how far stocks can fall in a short period ... And an unsettled mind will not make good decisions" (2017). "It is insane to risk what you have and need in order to obtain what you don't need" (2017). "A string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal zero. Don't count on getting rich twice" (Munger, 2022).

**Ignore forecasts.** "We do not have, never have had, and never will have an opinion about where the stock market, interest rates, or business activity will be a year from now" (1988). Short-term market forecasts "are poison and should be kept locked up in a safe place, away from children and also from grown-ups who behave in the market like children" (1992). "The cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters" (2003). "Forming macro opinions or listening to the macro or market predictions of others is a waste of time. Indeed, it is dangerous because it may blur your vision of the facts that are truly important" (2013). "In the 54 years we have worked together, we have never foregone an attractive purchase because of the macro or political environment, or the views of other people" (2013). "We try to price, rather than time, purchases. ... Why scrap an informed decision because of an uninformed guess?" (1994). Being out of the market is the real risk: "The risks of being out of the game are huge compared to the risks of being in it" (2012).

**Avoid difficult problems; invert.** "Charlie and I have not learned how to solve difficult business problems. What we have learned is to avoid them. ... we've done better by avoiding dragons than by slaying them" (1989). Munger's "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there" (1985, 1996, 2009, 2014). "If we can't tolerate a possible consequence, remote though it may be, we steer clear of planting its seeds" (1996). Prefer "mono-linked chains": ten independent 90 percent variables give a 35 percent chance of success (2004).

**Distrust precision, models and helpers.** "Beware of geeks bearing formulas" (2008). Models "lull decision-makers into a false sense of security" (1996). "If merely looking up past financial data would tell you what the future holds, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians" (1990, 1996, 2008). Costs matter: the Gotrocks parable shows "Helpers" taking perhaps 20 percent of all corporate earnings (2005); a 1 percent annual fee would have halved a 77-year result (2018); "Performance comes, performance goes. Fees never falter" (2017). "Their expenses are Wall Street's income" (2020).

**Own your mistakes and fix them fast.** "Agonizing over errors is a mistake. But acknowledging and analyzing them can be useful, though that practice is rare in corporate boardrooms" (2000). "The cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes or what Charlie Munger called 'thumb-sucking.' Problems, he would tell me, cannot be wished away" (2024). Errors of omission matter as much as commission: Fannie Mae ($1.4 billion foregone, 1991), Walmart ("at least $50 billion," Munger 2014), Coca-Cola noticed in 1936 and bought in 1988 ("Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes," 1989). "You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it" (1994). "Mistakes fade away; winners can forever blossom" (2024).

## Part VIII. How Buffett's thinking evolved, and the mistakes that taught him

The letters are unusual in recording a mind changing. The arc, in his own words:

- **1950s: pure Graham.** Cheap statistics, net-nets, cigar butts. "Picking up a discarded cigar butt that had one puff remaining in it. Though the stub might be ugly and soggy, the puff would be free" (2014). The 1950s were his best decade because the method worked at small scale. He bought Berkshire Hathaway itself this way in 1965, a textile mill at $7.50 against $10.25 of working capital, "a monumentally stupid decision" made out of pique (2014), "a mistake, my mistake, and one that plagued us for two decades" (2024).
- **1965: Munger's instruction.** "Warren, forget about ever buying another company like Berkshire. But now that you control Berkshire, add to it wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices and give up buying fair businesses at wonderful prices. In other words, abandon everything you learned from your hero, Ben Graham. It works but only when practiced at small scale" (2023). Buffett followed it "with much back-sliding."
- **1972: See's.** Paid three times tangible book value, which Graham would never have done, and learned "the value of powerful brands" and of pricing power (1991, 2014). "We've made significant money in certain common stocks because of the lessons we learned at See's" (1991).
- **1977 to 1985: the textile lessons.** Waumbec Mills, a sub-working-capital bargain, "was a mistake"; "turnarounds seldom turn" (1979). The textile business is closed in 1985: "a good managerial record ... is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row" (1985).
- **1983 to 1989: the explicit conversion.** "My own thinking has changed drastically from 35 years ago when I was taught to favor tangible assets and to shun businesses whose value depended largely upon economic Goodwill" (1983). "Your Chairman, always a quick study, required only 20 years to recognize how important it was to buy good businesses" (1987). "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years" (1989) sets out the whole creed: wonderful business at a fair price; good jockeys need good horses; avoid difficult problems; the institutional imperative; only deal with people you like, trust and admire; errors of omission; and conservative leverage was not a mistake.
- **1990s: the great franchises.** Coca-Cola, Gillette, GEICO (bought outright), American Express, Wells Fargo. The formalisation of "Inevitables" (1996), of risk versus beta (1993), of the DCF definition (1992), of the circle of competence (1996), and of float as the engine (1990 to 1999). The stock-financed Dexter deal (1993) becomes "the worst deal that I've made" (2007).
- **2000s: scale and whole businesses.** Size forces a shift from stocks (114 percent of net worth in the 1980s) toward whole businesses, cash and later capital-heavy regulated assets. "A fat wallet, however, is the enemy of superior investment results" (1994). Gen Re's derivatives book and under-reserving teach "the time to act is now" (2005). The 2002 derivatives warning and the 2008 crisis confirm the no-leverage, no-kindness-of-strangers doctrine.
- **2010s: the accounting and fee crusades, and the Bet.** Buybacks adopted at Berkshire (2011). Munger's 50th-anniversary essay lays out "the Berkshire system" (2014). The index fund bet is won (2017). Apple is bought (2016), a rare technology holding justified on consumer-franchise grounds.
- **2020s: candour and succession.** Precision Castparts written down for overpaying (2020). Japan bought because the financial records showed "the low prices of their stocks" (2024), funded in yen because currencies cannot be forecast. The 2022 "Secret Sauce" and 2023 Munger tribute distil everything: a dozen good decisions in 58 years, held for decades, overwhelm a lifetime of mediocre ones.

**The catalogue of admitted mistakes** (useful because each one maps to a rule): buying Berkshire (cheap bad business); Waumbec and Hochschild Kohn (cigar butts); the 1970s reinsurance underwriting and three years of under-reserving (not knowing your costs); 15-year bonds not sold when views changed (inaction); selling GEICO in 1952, Disney in 1967, Cap Cities twice and McDonald's in 1998 (selling wonderful businesses); USAir (high-cost producer in a deregulated commodity industry, "sloppy analysis or hubris"); Salomon and the preferreds (outside the circle); Fannie Mae and Walmart (omission); Dexter and Gen Re (paying in stock; misjudging moat durability and culture); Gen Re Securities (thumb-sucking); not selling in the 1999 bubble ("I talked when I should have walked"); ConocoPhillips at the oil peak and the Irish banks (2008); Energy Future Holdings bonds ("without consulting with Charlie"); the GEICO credit card; NetJets' debt; Tesco (slow to sell); Precision Castparts (overpaying); the utilities' regulatory risk (2023). He is explicit that "most of my capital-allocation decisions have been no better than so-so" (2022) and that the record came from a few big winners held a very long time.

## Part IX. The lineage: Graham, Fisher, Munger, Lynch and the others

Buffett has said he is "85% Graham and 15% Fisher," and that Munger moved him from fair businesses at wonderful prices to wonderful businesses at fair prices. This part sets out what each contributed and where they differ. Unlike Parts I to VIII it is drawn from these investors' own books, talks and letters rather than from the Berkshire letters.

### Benjamin Graham (Security Analysis, 1934; The Intelligent Investor, 1949)

Graham taught Buffett at Columbia and employed him at Graham-Newman. Buffett calls The Intelligent Investor "by far the best book on investing ever written" (1984) and says that Chapters 8 and 20 "guide my investing decisions today" (2013). Three ideas are the foundation everything else rests on:

1. **A stock is a piece of a business.** "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike." Your result is determined by the enterprise, not the quote. Every Buffett sentence about "business-pickers" is Graham.
2. **Mr. Market (Chapter 8).** Your partner in a private business shows up daily offering to buy or sell at a price set by his mood. He serves you; he does not guide you. Buffett: "Picking up that book was one of the luckiest moments in my life" (2011).
3. **Margin of safety (Chapter 20).** Buy far enough below your estimate of value to survive your own errors and bad luck. "The function of the margin of safety is, in essence, that of rendering unnecessary an accurate estimate of the future."

Graham's own application was quantitative and defensive, because he was nearly wiped out in 1929 to 1932: net-nets (buy below two-thirds of net current asset value, diversify widely because any one may be a trap); the defensive investor's rules (strong balance sheet, ten years of earnings stability, twenty years of dividends, price under 15 times three-year average earnings and under 1.5 times book, or the product under 22.5); average earnings across a full cycle, never a single year; the sharp line between investment ("upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return") and speculation; and temperament over IQ ("The investor's chief problem, and even his worst enemy, is likely to be himself"). Walter Schloss ran this pure method for 47 years at about 16 percent a year with a thousand mostly lacklustre stocks, which Buffett cites as proof that the Graham approach works for anyone with the discipline (2006).

Where Buffett left Graham: Graham cared little for business quality or management and wanted assets at a discount and statistical diversification. Buffett learned from Dempster, Berkshire textiles and Hochschild Kohn that a cheap bad business stays bad and that the method does not scale. But the three foundations never changed, and he still says Graham's principles survived every shock from Vietnam to 17 percent T-bills without "the slightest dent" (1994).

### Philip Fisher (Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits, 1958)

Fisher is the 15 percent. He supplied the idea that business quality, growth runway and management quality are worth paying for, and that you learn them by "scuttlebutt" (talking to customers, suppliers, competitors and former employees). His 15 points cover sales growth runway, product development, R&D effectiveness, sales organisation, margins and the effort to maintain them, labour and executive relations, management depth, cost accounting, industry-specific advantages, long-range profit outlook, the need for future equity financing, management candour in bad times, and integrity. Three Fisher rules Buffett absorbed: hold for the very long run ("If the job has been correctly done when a common stock is purchased, the time to sell it is almost never"); know a few companies deeply rather than many shallowly; and do not quibble over small price differences for a truly great business. Buffett cites Fisher's restaurant analogy for having a consistent shareholder policy (1979, 2020) and ranks his book "behind only The Intelligent Investor and the 1940 edition of Security Analysis" (2012).

### Charlie Munger (Poor Charlie's Almanack; the 1994 "Elementary Worldly Wisdom" and 1995 "Psychology of Human Misjudgment" talks; his 2014 Berkshire essay)

Munger's contribution is less a method than a way of thinking, and Buffett calls him "the architect" of Berkshire with himself as "general contractor" (2023).

- **The great business at a fair price.** Over a long holding period a stock's return converges on the return on capital of the business. If it earns 6 percent for 40 years you will earn about 6 percent even if you bought it cheaply; if it earns 18 percent you will do about 18 percent even if you paid a seemingly full price. So the question is not "is it cheap" but "will it compound."
- **The latticework of mental models.** Businesses cannot be understood with finance alone. You need the big ideas from psychology (incentives, social proof, commitment, availability), mathematics (compound interest, probability, decision trees), engineering (breakpoints, redundancy, margin of safety as an engineering idea), biology (competition, evolution), microeconomics (scale, network effects) and accounting with its limits. "To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail."
- **Invert, always invert.** Ask what would guarantee failure and avoid it: leverage, envy, overpaying, not understanding the business, trusting the wrong people, trading too much. "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."
- **The psychology of human misjudgment.** Twenty-five tendencies, chiefly incentive-caused bias ("show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome"), social proof, loss aversion, consistency and commitment, contrast, authority, and the "lollapalooza" effect when several combine. Assume you are subject to all of them.
- **Sit on your ass investing.** Buy a few great businesses when they are available and do almost nothing. "The big money is not in the buying or the selling, but in the waiting." "Patience can be learned. Having a long attention span and the ability to concentrate on one thing for a long time is a huge advantage" (2022).
- **Three baskets: yes, no, too hard.** Most things go in "too hard." There is no penalty for passing.
- **Opportunity cost as the discount rate.** Every idea is measured against the best alternative you already have.
- **The Berkshire system** (his 2014 essay): extreme autonomy for subsidiary CEOs, almost nothing at headquarters, cash not stock for acquisitions, no dividends while each retained dollar creates more than a dollar of value, almost never sell a subsidiary, little debt, trust rather than rules, and "almost inhuman patience."

### Peter Lynch (One Up on Wall Street, 1989; Beating the Street, 1993)

Lynch ran Fidelity Magellan from 1977 to 1990 at about 29 percent a year. He agrees with Buffett that you are buying businesses and that macro forecasting is useless, but his style was the opposite: hundreds of positions, high turnover, cyclicals and turnarounds included. What he adds:

- **Know what you own and be able to explain it in two minutes:** the story, what has to happen for it to work, the pitfalls.
- **Classify before you value.** Slow growers (own for yield), stalwarts (buy at a discount, sell at a 30 to 50 percent gain), fast growers (20 to 25 percent growers, the ten-baggers), cyclicals (buy at the trough; P/E is misleading, low multiples at peak earnings are a trap), turnarounds and asset plays. Each has its own rules and applying stalwart logic to a cyclical gets you hurt.
- **The PEG ratio.** A fast grower is fairly priced when P/E roughly equals the growth rate; add the dividend yield to the growth rate before dividing. Under 1 is poor, over 2 is attractive.
- **The perfect stock sounds dull,** does something dull or disagreeable, is a spin-off, is ignored by institutions, is in a no-growth industry, has a niche, has repeat purchases, uses technology rather than makes it, and insiders are buying and the company is buying back shares.
- **Avoid** the hottest stock in the hottest industry, the "next" anything, "diworseification," the whisper stock and the single-customer company.
- **Invest in what you know, then do the work.** Daily life gives you an early signal; the balance sheet, cash, debt (bank debt that can be called is the dangerous kind), inventories and pension still have to be checked. Rising inventories at a manufacturer are a warning. Draw the earnings line on the price chart.
- **Stay invested.** "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections, or trying to anticipate corrections, than has been lost in corrections themselves."

Buffett quotes Lynch twice with approval: selling winners and holding losers is "cutting the flowers and watering the weeds" (1988), and commodity-product stocks should carry the warning "Competition may prove hazardous to human wealth" (1993).

### Other managers whose ideas are worth taking

- **John Maynard Keynes.** Moved from macro speculation, which nearly ruined him twice, to a concentrated portfolio of a few businesses he understood. Buffett quotes his 1934 letter: "put fairly large sums into enterprises which one thinks one knows something about and in the management of which one thoroughly believes" (1991), and his line that "it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally" (2004).
- **Seth Klarman (Margin of Safety, 1991).** Absolute returns, cash when nothing is cheap, risk defined as permanent loss, and a focus on where forced sellers create bargains (spin-offs, index deletions, distress). The modern Graham.
- **Howard Marks (Oaktree memos; The Most Important Thing).** Second-level thinking (you must be right and the consensus wrong); risk is highest when it is least perceived; you can know where you are in a cycle without predicting its turn; being too early is indistinguishable from being wrong.
- **Joel Greenblatt (The Little Book That Beats the Market).** The "magic formula" is Buffett in two numbers: rank on return on capital (EBIT over net working capital plus net fixed assets) to find the good business, and on earnings yield (EBIT over enterprise value) to find the fair price. His earlier book on special situations shows where structural mispricing lives.
- **John Templeton.** Buy at "the point of maximum pessimism"; look worldwide; "the four most dangerous words in investing are 'this time it's different.'"
- **Walter Schloss.** Graham's method, undiluted, for 47 years (Buffett, 2006).
- **Lou Simpson (GEICO).** Think independently; invest in high-return businesses run for shareholders; pay only a reasonable price even for an excellent business; invest for the long term; do not diversify excessively. Buffett reported his 20.3 percent a year against 13.5 percent for the S&P from 1980 to 2004.
- **Tom Murphy (Capital Cities) and Henry Singleton (Teledyne).** Buffett's models of the capital-allocating CEO: decentralise, keep costs down, buy back stock when it is cheap, buy businesses only at the right price, never dilute. "To achieve a reputation as a good manager, just be sure you buy good businesses" (Murphy, 2019). "Start with Tom Murphy, and you'll never need a second exemplar" (2015). William Thorndike's The Outsiders, which Buffett recommends (2012), profiles eight such CEOs.
- **Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria (Nomad letters).** "Scale economies shared": the business that passes cost savings to customers (Costco, Amazon, GEICO) deepens its moat every year. Most portfolio value comes from a very few holdings held a very long time, so the key skill is not selling them.
- **Terry Smith (Fundsmith).** "Buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing." Good means high return on capital employed, high gross margin, low capital intensity, high cash conversion, and growth not driven by leverage or acquisitions.
- **Chuck Akre.** The three-legged stool: an extraordinary business, talented honest management, and the opportunity to reinvest at high rates. The third leg separates a compounder from a dividend stock.
- **Li Lu and Mohnish Pabrai.** Buffett's principles applied abroad; "heads I win, tails I don't lose much"; few bets, big bets, infrequent bets; clone the best investors' public filings without shame.
- **Jack Bogle.** For everyone who is not a know-something investor, the low-cost index fund. Buffett says Bogle has done more for American investors than anyone and deserves a statue (2016).
- **George Soros and Stanley Druckenmiller, for contrast.** Macro traders whose edge is reflexivity and position sizing. Worth knowing precisely to see what Buffett is not doing: no leverage, no shorting, no currency or rate bets, no attempt to be right about the next 12 months.

## Part X. What is actually making the difference

Pulling it all together, here is the answer to the question. The difference between Buffett's results and most investors' results is not a secret formula, a superior model or access to information. It is a small number of ideas applied without exception for sixty years, each of which most participants know and almost none of them practise. In order of importance:

**1. He buys businesses, so he only has to be right about the business.** Everyone else is trying to be right about the business and the market's opinion of it next quarter. By refusing to care about the second, he eliminates most of the ways to be wrong, and he gets paid by the market's errors instead of being victimised by them. "In the end, what counts in investing is what you pay for a business and what that business earns in the succeeding decade or two" (2009).

**2. He restricts himself to businesses whose earnings he can estimate a decade out,** which in practice means businesses with durable pricing power or a durable cost advantage, in industries that change slowly, that earn high returns on little tangible capital, with little debt, run by honest people. That filter eliminates most of the market, most of the excitement, and most of the mistakes. The See's economics (high returns on tiny capital, growth that costs nothing, pricing power) are the template, and the three savings accounts (2007) are the test to apply.

**3. He understands that return on incremental capital, not growth, is what compounds.** This is the single idea that separates the letters from ordinary "growth" or "value" thinking. A business that needs $5 of capital per $1 of new earnings cannot make its owners rich however fast it grows; a business that needs nothing can. It is also why he judges management by what they do with the cash, and why he cares so much about buybacks, dividends and stock issuance.

**4. He pays attention to price, but as a margin of safety against his own errors, not as the source of return.** The return comes from the business; the discount to value protects him from being wrong about the business. That is the synthesis of Graham (margin of safety) and Munger (quality). Pay up for the truly great, never pay too much for anything, and never let a cheap price seduce you into a bad business.

**5. He does almost nothing, for decades.** A dozen big decisions in 58 years. No market timing, no macro, no rebalancing out of winners, no selling of a wonderful business because it has gone up. Buffett's record is the arithmetic of a few compounders held for 30 years (Coca-Cola, American Express, GEICO, See's, Apple) plus the discipline not to dilute them with activity. "The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom" (2022).

**6. He makes survival the first rule.** No leverage, enormous liquidity, no dependence on anyone's goodwill, no position that can force a sale at a bad time. This costs a little return in 99 years out of 100 and is why Berkshire is the buyer when "it's raining gold" in the hundredth. Every blow-up in the letters, from the 1980s LBOs to Long-Term Capital to 2008, is someone who had a long string of good numbers and then multiplied by zero.

**7. He has a structural edge he built on purpose: permanent capital and float.** Berkshire's shareholders do not redeem, its managers do not report to Wall Street, and its insurance float gives it tens of billions of dollars of costless, long-duration funding. That is what let him hold through 50 percent drawdowns and buy in panics. The individual investor's equivalent is simpler: no debt, no need to sell, a long horizon, and nobody looking over your shoulder. "If you can enjoy Saturdays and Sundays without looking at stock prices, give it a try on weekdays" (2013).

**8. He is honest with himself.** The letters contain the word "mistake" more than any other CEO's communications, and he says that is not modesty but method: candour prevents self-deception, and self-deception is where capital goes to die. He changed his mind in public (Graham to Munger; against airlines then owning them; against railroads then buying one; against technology then buying Apple) when the facts changed, and he acted on it rather than "thumb-sucking."

**9. He kept costs and friction near zero.** No fees, no turnover, no taxes triggered unnecessarily. The arithmetic of the Gotrocks family (2005) and the hedge-fund bet (2017) shows that most of the investing industry's underperformance is simply the cost of the industry.

**10. Temperament over intellect.** Everything above is simple to understand and hard to do, because it requires standing still while others run, buying when others are frightened, and ignoring the daily invitation to act. Buffett's own words for what the job requires: "a few simple fundamentals," "an ability to disregard mob fears or enthusiasms," and "a willingness to look unimaginative for a sustained period, or even to look foolish" (2017).

What Graham gave him was the first and fourth of these. What Fisher and Munger gave him was the second and third. What Lynch adds for an individual investor is a practical taxonomy for the businesses that do not fit the Buffett mould, and a reminder that your own daily experience is a legitimate research source. What the rest of the lineage confirms, from Keynes to Sleep to Smith, is that everyone who has compounded capital for decades arrived at the same handful of rules, usually after being burned by their opposites.

## Part XI. A practical checklist drawn from the letters

For use when looking at any company. Each item cites where the standard comes from.

**The business**

1. Can I explain how it makes money in a paragraph, and can I estimate a range for its earnings five to ten years out? If not, stop here (1992, 1996, 2013).
2. Return on unleveraged net tangible capital: over 20 percent is excellent, 12 to 20 percent good, under that it is not a compounder (1979, 2013, 2018).
3. How much new capital does each dollar of new earnings require? Compare capex to depreciation over a decade, and track net tangible assets against earnings. A business that funds growth from its own cash and still distributes most of its earnings is the ideal (1985, 1991, 2007).
4. Does it have pricing power? Has it raised prices ahead of inflation without losing volume? Is the product one customers ask for by name? (1981, 1991, 2004).
5. What is the moat, specifically: lowest cost, or a brand or franchise with no close substitute? Is it widening or narrowing each year? Would I like to compete against it with unlimited capital? (1983, 1986, 2005, 2007).
6. Is the industry stable? Will the product be sold in essentially the same form in 20 years? Does success depend on a technology shift or on one person? (1987, 1996, 2007).
7. Is it a commodity producer? If so, is it demonstrably the low-cost producer with a cost gap that will persist? (1978, 1982, 2000).
8. Do reported earnings turn into cash? Compute owner earnings: net income plus depreciation and amortization minus maintenance capex minus working capital needs (1980, 1986).
9. Debt: can it meet its obligations with certainty under extraordinarily adverse conditions? Is interest covered by pre-tax earnings, not EBITDA, several times over? (1987, 1989, 2012).
10. Red flags: turnaround, start-up, projections rather than record, serial acquirer, serial share issuer, heavy use of adjusted earnings, EBITDA in the headline, restructuring charges every year, fancy pension assumptions, a decade without paying income tax, incomprehensible footnotes.

**The management**

11. Candour: do they report bad news in their own words, and have they ever admitted a mistake? (1979, 1983, 2024).
12. Ownership: do they own meaningful stock bought with their own money, and is their pay tied to things they control with a charge for capital? Are options the main pay? (1985, 1994, 1996, 2024).
13. Capital allocation record: have they repurchased shares only when cheap, issued shares only when dear or never, acquired only at sensible prices, and paid out what they could not reinvest well? Have retained earnings produced at least a dollar of market value per dollar retained over five years? (1984, 2011, 2012).
14. Signs of the institutional imperative: expansion for its own size, deals justified by consultants and bankers, imitation of peers, refusal to shrink (1989, 2004, 2017).
15. Focus and cost consciousness: do they stay in the business they understand, and is overhead lean in good times? (1978, 1996, 2015).

**The price**

16. Estimate intrinsic value as the present value of the cash that can be taken out over the business's life, discounted at the long government bond rate, as a range not a number (1992, 1994, 2000).
17. Is there a wide margin between that range's low end and the price? If the value is only slightly above price, pass (1992, 2013, 2019).
18. Compare the earnings yield on normalized owner earnings with the long bond and with your best existing holding (1981, 2017; Munger's opportunity cost).
19. Would I be comfortable owning this if the market closed for five years? If the price fell 50 percent, would I buy more? (1987, 1993, 2017).
20. Am I being driven by the business or by the price action, the story, the crowd or a forecast? (1990, 2000, 2013).

**Yourself**

21. Is this inside my circle of competence, and am I honest about where its edge is? (1996, 1999, 2019).
22. Am I using borrowed money or any arrangement that could force me to sell? (2010, 2014, 2017).
23. How many positions am I running, and do I know each as well as I would know a private business I had bought outright? (1978, 1991, 1993).
24. What is my all-in cost of fees, turnover and taxes per year? (1983, 2005, 2016, 2018).
25. If I cannot do items 1 to 24 for most of the companies I look at, the right answer is a low-cost index fund, steady accumulation, and never selling on bad news (1993, 2013, 2014).
